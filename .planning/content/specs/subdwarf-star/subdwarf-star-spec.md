## Definition

A subdwarf is a star whose luminosity is 1.5 to 2 magnitudes below that of main-sequence stars of the same spectral type, placing it below the main sequence on the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram. The term encompasses two physically unrelated families: cool subdwarfs (types sdG through sdM), which are old, metal-poor stars whose low metallicity makes them intrinsically smaller and fainter, and hot subdwarfs (types sdB and sdO), which are the stripped helium-burning cores of former red giants.

## Full Content

### What is a subdwarf star?
- Subdwarfs occupy luminosity class VI in the Yerkes (MKK) spectral classification, sitting below the main sequence on the HR diagram [Wikipedia/Subdwarf]
- They are 1.5–2 magnitudes fainter than main-sequence stars of the same spectral type, yet brighter than white dwarfs of comparable temperature [astro4edu.org/glossary/480]
- The "sd" prefix in spectral notation identifies them: e.g. sdM1 for a cool metal-poor red star, sdB for a hot helium-burning remnant [Wikipedia/Subdwarf]
- Despite sharing a name and HR diagram position, cool and hot subdwarfs are physically unrelated; they were grouped together because both sit below the main sequence [Wikipedia/Subdwarf]

### Cool subdwarfs (sdG, sdK, sdM)
- Generate energy by hydrogen fusion, like ordinary main-sequence stars, but low metallicity reduces opacity and radiation pressure, producing a smaller and hotter star for a given mass [Wikipedia/Subdwarf]
- The reduced size makes them intrinsically less luminous — same spectral type but 1.5–2 mag fainter — placing them below the main sequence [Wikipedia/Subdwarf]
- They display an ultraviolet excess relative to solar-metallicity stars of the same colour [Wikipedia/Subdwarf]
- Typically move at high space velocities relative to the Sun, indicating membership in the galactic halo or thick disk — ancient stellar populations [Wikipedia/Subdwarf]
- Low metallicity is a direct record of age: these stars formed when the universe was young and the interstellar medium was still metal-poor [astro4edu.org/glossary/480]
- Notable examples: Kapteyn's Star (sdM1), one of the nearest known subdwarfs; Groombridge 1830 [Wikipedia/Subdwarf]
- The metallicity spread is large: three subclasses are defined by [Fe/H], ranging from mildly metal-poor (sd) to extremely metal-poor (usd) [Wikipedia/Subdwarf]
- Late-type subdwarfs extend to brown dwarf territory: sdL (first found 2003), sdT, and sdY classes are old metal-poor substellar objects with suppressed near-infrared spectra due to collision-induced hydrogen absorption [Wikipedia/Subdwarf]

### Hot subdwarfs (sdB and sdO)
- Entirely different evolutionary stage from cool subdwarfs: these are the exposed helium-burning cores of former red giants that lost their hydrogen envelopes prematurely [arxiv:2410.11663]
- sdB stars sit at the extreme horizontal branch of the HR diagram, fusing helium in their cores at temperatures of 20,000–40,000 K; sdO stars are more evolved, burning helium in a shell at 40,000–100,000 K [Heber 2009; Wikipedia/sdB; Wikipedia/sdO]
- Both types have masses of ~0.5 M☉ and radii of only 0.15–0.25 R☉ — far smaller than the red giants from which they descended [Wikipedia/sdB]
- Their thin hydrogen envelopes (~1% by mass) are too thin to sustain hydrogen shell burning, preventing them from ascending the asymptotic giant branch and sending them directly toward the white dwarf cooling track [Wikipedia/sdB]
- About 50% of sdB stars reside in close binary systems; stripping of the hydrogen envelope during a common-envelope phase or stable Roche-lobe overflow by a binary companion is the leading formation channel [Heber 2009; arxiv:2410.11663]
- Single hot subdwarfs may form through the merger of two helium white dwarfs, or through interaction with a substellar companion [Wikipedia/sdB; Wikipedia/sdO]
- Hot subdwarfs are prominent in the ultraviolet; their large numbers in old stellar systems explain the "UV upturn" — the excess ultraviolet emission seen in elliptical galaxies and globular clusters [Heber 2009]
- Tightest sdB binary systems (orbital periods as short as 20 minutes) are strong gravitational wave sources detectable by the future LISA mission; some are potential Type Ia supernova progenitors [arxiv:2410.11663]
- Pulsating sdB stars (sdBVr and sdBVs classes) have been studied with Kepler and TESS, enabling asteroseismic measurements of their interiors, rotation rates, and synchronisation [arxiv:2410.11663]
- US 708, an sdO star, is a hypervelocity star travelling faster than the Milky Way's escape velocity — likely the surviving donor star from a Type Ia supernova explosion [Wikipedia/sdO]

### Significance
- Cool subdwarfs are fossil tracers of the early Milky Way; their kinematics and metallicities preserve records of ancient galactic structure [astro4edu.org; sentinelmission.org]
- Hot subdwarfs are key testbeds for common-envelope evolution and mass-transfer physics — processes central to the formation of many compact binaries, gravitational wave sources, and Type Ia supernovae [arxiv:2410.11663]
- Understanding hot subdwarf formation constrains the overall white dwarf production rate and binary stellar evolution more broadly [Heber 2009]

## Historical Context

- Gerard Kuiper coined the term "subdwarf" in 1939 to describe stars with anomalous spectra previously labelled "intermediate white dwarfs" [Wikipedia/Subdwarf]
- Hot subdwarfs (sdB class) were first systematically identified by Fritz Zwicky and Humason around 1947 during a survey near the galactic pole [Wikipedia/Subdwarf B star]
- The formal Yerkes luminosity class VI designation placed subdwarfs in a recognised spectral classification tier distinct from main-sequence (class V) stars
- The physically distinct nature of cool vs. hot subdwarfs became clear as evolutionary models matured in the latter half of the 20th century; both groups had been loosely called "subdwarfs" simply because they fell below the main sequence [Wikipedia/Subdwarf]
- Kapteyn's Star, an sdM1 subdwarf discovered in 1898 as a high-proper-motion object, remains among the nearest known cool subdwarfs [Wikipedia/Subdwarf]
- The UV upturn in elliptical galaxies — a puzzle since the 1970s — was eventually explained by populations of hot subdwarfs contributing copious ultraviolet flux [Heber 2009]
- The Annual Review paper by Ulrich Heber (2009) established the modern framework for hot subdwarf classification and formation, synthesising decades of spectroscopic survey work [Heber 2009, ARA&A 47:211]

## Sub-types / Classifications

### Cool subdwarfs (Population II main-sequence analogues)
- **sdG, sdK, sdM**: Metal-poor hydrogen-fusing stars; classified by spectral type and metallicity; sit on a metal-poor main sequence shifted below the standard sequence
- **Metallicity subclasses** (Gizis 1997 system):
  - **sd** (subdwarf): −1.0 < [Fe/H] ≤ −0.3
  - **esd** (extreme subdwarf): −1.7 < [Fe/H] ≤ −1.0
  - **usd** (ultrasubdwarf): [Fe/H] ≤ −1.7
- **sdL, sdT, sdY**: Very low-mass and substellar metal-poor objects; old brown dwarfs with suppressed near-infrared flux due to collision-induced hydrogen absorption; sdL first confirmed 2003

### Hot subdwarfs (evolved, helium-burning cores)
- **sdB**: Extreme horizontal-branch stars; core helium burning; T = 20,000–40,000 K; ~0.5 M☉; most common hot subdwarf type
  - **sdBVr**: Rapid pulsators (periods 90–600 s; pressure-mode oscillations)
  - **sdBVs**: Slow pulsators (periods 45–180 min; gravity-mode oscillations)
  - **sdBVrs**: Hybrid pulsators showing both modes
- **sdO**: Post-extreme horizontal branch; shell helium burning; T = 40,000–100,000 K; ~1/3 as numerous as sdB; carbon–oxygen core surrounded by helium shell
  - **He-sdO**: Helium-dominated spectra; ionised helium prominent; fairly rare
  - **H-sdO**: Hydrogen-dominated spectra
- **sdOB**: Intermediate helium-rich type between sdB and sdO
- **Heavy-metal subdwarfs**: Subclass with extreme surface enrichment of Ge, Sr, Y, Zr, and Pb produced by radiative levitation in stable, slowly rotating atmospheres (examples: HE 2359-2844, LS IV-14 116)
