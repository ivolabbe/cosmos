## Definition

A nova is a transient astronomical event in which a white dwarf in a close binary star system undergoes a thermonuclear runaway on its surface, causing a sudden brightening of thousands to 100,000 times its normal luminosity before fading over weeks to months. Unlike a supernova, the white dwarf is not destroyed and the event may repeat.

## Full Content

### Physical Mechanism

- A nova occurs in a **cataclysmic variable (CV)** binary system: a white dwarf (WD) and a donor companion star (typically a red dwarf or subgiant; a red giant in symbiotic recurrent novae) in a close orbit [Wikipedia: Nova]
- The companion overfills its **Roche lobe**, transferring hydrogen-rich material through the inner Lagrangian point; this forms an **accretion disk** around the WD [Wikipedia: Nova; WebSearch thermonuclear runaway]
- Accreted hydrogen accumulates in a **degenerate layer** on the WD surface — degenerate matter cannot expand to regulate temperature, so the layer heats without pressure relief [Wikipedia: Nova]
- When the base of the accumulated layer reaches ~20 million K, hydrogen fusion ignites explosively: a **thermonuclear runaway (TNR)** [Wikipedia: Nova]
- Radiation pressure exceeds the Eddington limit; the WD is **briefly super-Eddington** by up to 10× [A&A 2019, aa34238-18]
- The outer envelope is **ejected at 500–5,000 km s^{−1}**, visible as an expanding shell or nebula; the WD itself survives intact [Wikipedia: Nova; WebSearch]
- Mass ejected per outburst: ~10^{−7}–10^{−3} M☉, depending on WD mass — higher-mass WDs need less accreted mass to ignite, yielding smaller ejecta [WebSearch]
- After ejection, the WD resumes accretion from its companion; the cycle can repeat on timescales of 10^3–10^5 years for classical novae, or decades for recurrent novae [Wikipedia: Recurrent nova]

### Observational Appearance

- Nova appears as a sudden bright "new star" — often invisible before outburst — reaching naked-eye visibility in hours to days [Britannica]
- Peak absolute magnitude typically −6 to −10; fast novae tend to be more luminous (M_V ~ −8 to −11) than slow ones (M_V ~ −6 to −7) [OSTI 5423768]
- Light curve: rapid rise (hours), brief peak, then power-law decline over weeks–months; some show secondary oscillations, oscillations, or plateaux [Wikipedia: Nova]
- Spectra at peak show broad emission lines with P Cygni profiles indicating fast-moving ejecta [Wikipedia: Nova]
- After decline: an expanding nova remnant detectable for centuries in optical, radio, and X-ray [WebSearch]

### Chemical Enrichment

- Ejecta are enriched in **helium, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, neon, and magnesium** — material dredged from the underlying WD core mixes into the ejected shell [WebSearch: galactic nova rate]
- Novae involving **oxygen-neon (ONe) white dwarfs** produce neon-rich ejecta, distinct from carbon-oxygen (CO) WD novae [WebSearch; arXiv 2401.02307]
- Classical novae are now considered a **major — possibly dominant — source of galactic lithium-7 (^7Li)** enrichment in the Galactic disc [arXiv: 2401.02307]
- Novae contribute He, C, N, and isotopes to the **interstellar medium** on galactic timescales [WebSearch]

### Gamma-ray Emission (recent development)

- Since the launch of the *Fermi* Gamma-ray Space Telescope (2008), several novae have been detected as **gamma-ray sources** — unexpected and not predicted by classical nova models [arXiv: 1903.08134]
- Leading explanation: **internal shocks** within the ejecta accelerate particles to relativistic energies, producing gamma rays via pion decay [arXiv: 1709.00763]
- This reveals novae as **particle accelerators** — relevant to understanding cosmic-ray origins [arXiv: 1903.08134]
- First gamma-ray nova: V407 Cygni (2010), detected by *Fermi* LAT [arXiv: 1903.08134]

### Distinction from Supernovae

- A nova involves only the **surface** of the white dwarf; the star survives [Wikipedia: Nova]
- A supernova (Type Ia) involves the **total thermonuclear destruction** of the WD or core collapse of a massive star — roughly 10^{10}× more energetic than a nova [Wikipedia: Supernova]
- Recurrent novae on massive WDs near the **Chandrasekhar limit** (~1.4 M☉) are studied as potential **Type Ia supernova progenitors** if mass accretes net per cycle [Wikipedia: Recurrent nova]

## Historical Context

- The term **nova** (Latin: "new") was coined by **Tycho Brahe** when he described the bright "new star" of 1572 in his work *De nova stella* (1573) — though that event (SN 1572) was actually a supernova [Wikipedia: Nova; History of supernova observation]
- For centuries, novae and supernovae were not distinguished; both were called "novae stellae" (new stars)
- In **1934**, **Walter Baade** and **Fritz Zwicky** formally separated the two classes, coining the term "super-novae" for far more luminous events and establishing the physical distinction [Wikipedia: History of supernova observation; Baade & Zwicky 1934]
- Before the binary mechanism was understood, novae were thought to be single-star phenomena
- The **thermonuclear runaway model** was developed in the 1950s–1970s, with foundational theoretical work by Schatzman (1951), Gurevitch & Lebedinsky (1957), and Starrfield et al. (1970s) [Wikipedia: Nova]
- The **binary accretion model** — now the accepted mechanism — was confirmed observationally through radial velocity studies of nova remnants revealing the companion star
- *Fermi* LAT gamma-ray detection of novae began in **2010** (V407 Cygni), reshaping understanding of nova shocks and particle acceleration [arXiv: 1903.08134]
- **T Coronae Borealis** ("Blaze Star") was widely anticipated to outburst around 2024–2025, expected to reach ~magnitude 2, based on a pre-eruption dimming pattern identical to that seen before its 1946 outburst [NASA Marshall; Wikipedia: T CrB]

### Recurrent Novae (sub-class NR)

- Multiple observed outbursts separated by years to decades [Wikipedia: Recurrent nova]
- Generally involve **more massive WDs** (near Chandrasekhar limit) and **higher accretion rates** → shorter intervals [Wikipedia: Recurrent nova]
- ~10 confirmed recurrent novae in the Milky Way; the true recurrent fraction of all novae may be far higher [Wikipedia: Recurrent nova]
- Typically brighten ~9 mag (vs >12 mag for classical novae) [Wikipedia: Recurrent nova]

Notable recurrent novae:

| Nova | Known eruptions | ~Interval | Notes |
|------|----------------|-----------|-------|
| RS Ophiuchi | 1898, 1933, 1958, 1967, 1985, 2006, 2021 | ~15–20 yr | Symbiotic; red giant companion |
| T Coronae Borealis | 1866, 1946; next ~2024–2025? | ~80 yr | "Blaze Star"; visible to naked eye |
| T Pyxidis | 1890, 1902, 1920, 1944, 1966, 2011 | ~12–50 yr | 6 eruptions; ejecta blobs in Hubble images |
| U Scorpii | Multiple (1863–2010) | 8–43 yr | Fastest-fading known recurrent nova |
