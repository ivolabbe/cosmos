# Isobars — Article Spec

## Category
Physics (Nuclear Physics, Atomic Physics)

## Definition (1–2 sentences)
Isobars are nuclides (atomic nuclei) of different chemical elements that share the same mass number A — the total count of protons and neutrons — but differ in atomic number Z (the proton count). Because beta decay conserves A while changing Z by one unit, the parent and daughter nucleus in any beta decay are always an isobaric pair.

## Full Content (ALL facts needed for 200–400 words)

### Core Definition
The mass number A is defined as A = Z + N, where Z is the number of protons and N the number of neutrons. Isobars have identical A but different (Z, N) combinations. The term was coined by British chemist Alfred Walter Stewart in 1918, derived from the Greek isos ("equal") and baros ("weight"), reflecting that isobars have nearly — but not exactly — equal atomic masses. [Source: Wikipedia — Isobar (nuclide)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobar_(nuclide))

### Distinction from Isotopes, Isotones, and Isomers
- **Isotopes**: same Z, different N (and A). E.g., carbon-12 and carbon-14 are isotopes of carbon.
- **Isotones**: same N, different Z (and A). E.g., nitrogen-15 (Z=7, N=8) and oxygen-16 (Z=8, N=8).
- **Isobars**: same A, different Z and N. E.g., ⁴⁰Ar (Z=18, N=22) and ⁴⁰Ca (Z=20, N=20).
- **Nuclear isomers**: identical Z and N, different energy state (same nuclide in an excited metastable state).
[Source: Wikipedia — Isobar (nuclide)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobar_(nuclide)); [Britannica — Isobar](https://www.britannica.com/science/isobar-nuclear-physics)]

### A Classic Example: The A = 40 Isobar Chain
The A = 40 chain is one of the most instructive examples: ⁴⁰S → ⁴⁰Cl → ⁴⁰Ar (stable) and ⁴⁰K → ⁴⁰Ar or ⁴⁰Ca (stable). Each arrow is a beta decay (or electron capture), changing Z by ±1 while leaving A unchanged. ⁴⁰K (potassium-40, the naturally occurring radioisotope used in potassium-argon dating) undergoes branched beta decay: ~89% β⁻ to ⁴⁰Ca and ~11% electron capture to ⁴⁰Ar. Both ⁴⁰Ar and ⁴⁰Ca are stable beta-decay endpoints — a situation characteristic of even-A chains. [Source: Wikipedia — Isobar (nuclide)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobar_(nuclide)); [Wikipedia — Beta-decay stable isobars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta-decay_stable_isobars)]

### Beta Decay and Isobaric Chains
In beta-minus decay, a neutron converts to a proton (Z increases by 1); in beta-plus decay or electron capture, a proton converts to a neutron (Z decreases by 1). In both cases A is unchanged, so parent and daughter are isobars. This means any radioactive isobar decays along the isobar chain, stepping through successive elements, until it reaches the energetically most stable isobar (the one with the greatest binding energy for that A). [Source: Britannica — Isobar](https://www.britannica.com/science/isobar-nuclear-physics)]

### The Mass Parabola
For a fixed mass number A, the semi-empirical mass formula of Bethe and Weizsäcker (1935–1936) predicts that nuclear mass — equivalently, binding energy — varies parabolically with proton number Z. The two dominant terms responsible for this curvature are:
1. **Coulomb (repulsion) term**: energy proportional to Z(Z-1), growing with Z.
2. **Asymmetry term**: energy proportional to (N−Z)² = (A−2Z)², penalising large neutron-proton imbalance.

Together these create a parabola in mass vs Z at fixed A, with the minimum (most bound, most stable) isobar at an optimal Z_opt ≈ A/(1.98 + 0.015A^(2/3)). [Source: Wikipedia — Semi-empirical mass formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-empirical_mass_formula)]

For **odd-A** isobars there is one parabola; only one beta-stable isobar typically exists. For **even-A** isobars the pairing term (δ) splits the parabola into two offset curves — one for even-even (Z even, N even) and one for odd-odd nuclei. Even-even nuclei lie on the lower (more stable) parabola, which means two or more even-even isobars can sit close to the minimum and both be stable against beta decay. This is why 79 even-A values have two stable isobars (e.g., ⁸⁶Kr and ⁸⁶Sr; ⁹²Mo and ⁹²Zr), while odd-odd nuclei are almost always unstable. [Source: Wikipedia — Beta-decay stable isobars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta-decay_stable_isobars); [Wikipedia — Semi-empirical mass formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-empirical_mass_formula)]

### Mattauch's Isobar Rule
Josef Mattauch (1934) formulated an empirical rule: if two adjacent elements (differing in Z by 1) have isotopes of the same mass number A, at least one of those isobars must be radioactive. This follows directly from the mass-parabola: two adjacent Z values cannot both sit at the minimum of the parabola — one will be higher in energy and thus unstable to beta decay. Only two "adjacent-pair" stable isobars are known (¹¹³In/¹¹³Cd being possible exceptions with extremely long half-lives), consistent with the rule. [Source: Wikipedia — Isobar (nuclide)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobar_(nuclide))]

### Isobaric Analog States
Isobaric analog states (IAS) arise from isospin symmetry: because the strong nuclear force treats protons and neutrons nearly identically, a nucleus (Z, N) and the isobar (Z+1, N−1) obtained by converting one neutron to a proton should have nearly identical nuclear energy levels (quantum states), differing mainly by Coulomb energy. This "converted" state in the daughter nucleus is the IAS of the ground state (or excited state) of the parent. The energy difference between a state and its IAS — the Coulomb displacement energy — is approximately 1.44·Z/r₀A^(1/3) MeV, where r₀ ≈ 1.2 fm. IAS were first experimentally confirmed in the 1960s and are now used to:
- Test isospin conservation in nuclei.
- Extract nuclear symmetry energy coefficients relevant to neutron star structure.
- Identify excited states in neighboring isobars and constrain nuclear reaction rates.
[Source: Wikipedia — Isospin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isospin); [ScienceDirect — Symmetry energy II: Isobaric analog states](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0375947413007872); [MDPI — Excited States in Isobaric Multiplets](https://www.mdpi.com/2624-8174/4/3/66)]

### Role in Stellar Nucleosynthesis
Isobars are central to understanding how nuclei are forged and destroyed in stars:

**r-process (rapid neutron capture):** The r-process builds nuclei far from the valley of beta stability by successive rapid neutron captures, followed by beta decays. The cascade of beta decays from neutron-rich, unstable progenitors along each isobar chain terminates at the most neutron-rich stable isobar. Abundance peaks in solar-system material at A ≈ 80, 130, and 195 correspond to waiting-point nuclei near closed neutron shells (N = 50, 82, 126) where neutron capture stalls and beta decays along the isobar chain accumulate abundance. [Source: Cowan et al. (1991) / arXiv r-process review](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/0705.4512)]

**s-process (slow neutron capture):** The s-process proceeds along the valley of stability, stepping through isotopes one neutron at a time. When a stable isobar exists on the neutron-rich side of the valley, it "shields" the neutron-deficient isobar at the same A from receiving any s-process flux — the s-process flow cannot jump the stable nucleus. Nuclides that are shielded from the s-process and also cannot be made by the r-process are called p-only (or p-nuclides) and require a separate process (the p-process / γ-process) for their production. [Source: arXiv r-process/s-process review (0705.4512)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/0705.4512)]

**rp-process (rapid proton capture):** In X-ray bursts on accreting neutron stars, the rp-process builds proton-rich nuclei by rapid proton captures and positron emissions. The process stalls at "waiting-point" nuclei where the proton-capture Q-value becomes unfavourable and the beta-plus half-life is long. These waiting points are isobars on the proton-rich side of stability; their masses and half-lives determine burst energetics and final isotopic abundances. [Source: Nature Physics — Mass measurements at waiting-point nucleus ⁶⁴Ge](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41567-023-02034-2)]

### Stability Summary
- 346 nuclides are confirmed beta-decay stable (as of current nuclear databases). [Source: Wikipedia — Beta-decay stable isobars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta-decay_stable_isobars)]
- Odd-A isobars: one stable isobar per mass number (the single parabola minimum).
- Even-A isobars: one or two stable isobars; some have three (e.g., A = 124, 130, 136, 150, 154).
- No stable isobars exist for A = 5, 8, 147, 151, or A ≥ 209 (alpha decay dominates for heavy nuclei). [Source: Wikipedia — Isobar (nuclide)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobar_(nuclide))]

## Historical Context

- **1918**: Alfred Walter Stewart (chemist, UK) introduces the term "isobars" (originally "isobares") for nuclides of equal mass number. [Wikipedia — Isobar (nuclide)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobar_(nuclide))]
- **1934**: Josef Mattauch formulates the isobar rule — adjacent-element isobars cannot both be stable. [Wikipedia — Isobar (nuclide)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobar_(nuclide))]
- **1935–1936**: Carl Friedrich von Weizsäcker (and Hans Bethe) develops the semi-empirical mass formula (SEMF), explaining the mass parabola for isobar chains. [Wikipedia — Semi-empirical mass formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-empirical_mass_formula)]
- **Early 1960s**: First experimental confirmation of isobaric analog states; isospin shown to be an approximate symmetry of nuclear structure. [OSTI — Analogue States in Isobaric Nuclei (1965)](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/4727415)]
- **1957**: Burbidge, Burbidge, Fowler, and Hoyle (B²FH) paper establishes the r- and s-process frameworks for nucleosynthesis, placing isobar chains at the centre of heavy-element production. [NASA NTRS — r-, s-, p-processes in nucleosynthesis](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19980007188/downloads/19980007188.pdf)]

## Sub-types / Classifications

1. **Stable isobars**: Nuclides with the same A that are both beta-decay stable (e.g., ⁸⁶Kr and ⁸⁶Sr). Only exist for even A due to pairing energy.
2. **Radioactive isobars**: Unstable nuclides in an isobar chain that beta-decay toward the stable minimum (e.g., ⁴⁰K decaying to ⁴⁰Ar and ⁴⁰Ca).
3. **Isobaric analog states (IAS)**: Excited quantum states in one nucleus that mirror the ground (or excited) states of its isobar neighbour, connected by isospin symmetry.
4. **r-process waiting-point isobars**: Neutron-rich isobars where nuclear flow stalls during rapid neutron capture in explosive environments.
5. **rp-process waiting-point isobars**: Proton-rich isobars where nuclear flow stalls during rapid proton capture in X-ray bursts.
6. **p-nuclides / s-shielded isobars**: Proton-rich stable isobars that cannot be produced by the r- or s-process because a neutron-rich stable isobar shields them from both processes.
