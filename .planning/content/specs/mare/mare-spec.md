## Definition
A mare (plural: maria) is a large, dark, flat plain on the Moon composed of ancient basaltic lava that flooded impact basins billions of years ago; the name is Latin for "sea," applied by 17th-century astronomers who mistakenly believed these dark regions were bodies of water. Maria are visually distinct from the brighter, heavily cratered lunar highlands and cover approximately 16% of the total lunar surface, overwhelmingly concentrated on the near side facing Earth.

## Full Content

- Maria are large basaltic plains that fill ancient multi-ring impact basins; the basins were excavated by giant asteroid impacts ~3.9 billion years ago during a period called the Late Heavy Bombardment, and lava later welled up from the lunar mantle to flood them [Wikipedia: Lunar mare; Britannica]
- Magma originated hundreds of kilometres within the Moon's interior, erupting through fissures and flooding the basins over hundreds of millions of years, with most activity between 3.0–3.5 Ga [Britannica; Wikipedia: Lunar mare]
- The 2020 Chang'e-5 sample-return mission retrieved basalt from Mons Rümker in Oceanus Procellarum dated to 2.03 Ga — extending the known volcanic history ~1 billion years beyond previous estimates from Apollo samples [Wikipedia: Lunar mare]
- Crater-counting studies suggest the youngest flows anywhere on the lunar surface may be as recent as ~1.2 Ga, though these have not been confirmed by sample return [Wikipedia: Lunar mare]
- Maria cover ~16% of the total lunar surface, predominantly on the near side (~26% coverage) vs. the far side (~2%); the asymmetry is thought to reflect a thinner crust on the near side that allowed easier volcanic intrusion [Wikipedia: List of maria; Lunar mare]
- The dark colour of the maria relative to the highlands results from their iron- and titanium-rich basaltic composition, which absorbs more visible light; albedo of maria is significantly lower than lunar highlands [Wikipedia: Lunar mare]
- Mare basalts are tholeiitic and classified into three groups: high-titanium (TiO₂ up to 15 wt.%), low-titanium, and very-low-titanium; by comparison, terrestrial basalts rarely exceed 4 wt.% TiO₂ [Wikipedia: Lunar mare]
- The dominant minerals in mare basalts are pyroxene (51–69 vol.%), plagioclase (17–35 vol.%), and oxide minerals including ilmenite, armalcolite, and chromite-ulvöspinel; olivine is also present [LPI Lunar Sourcebook Ch. 5; NASA JSC Curator]
- Pyroxenes in lunar mare basalts are more Fe-rich than their terrestrial counterparts; feldspars are generally more Ca-rich; mare basalts completely lack water-bearing minerals [LPI Lunar Sourcebook; Wikipedia: Lunar mare]
- The uppermost ~5 m of the maria is regolith — a layer of fragmented rock and fine dust (called "fines") produced by billions of years of micrometeorite impacts [Britannica]
- Maria are associated with mascons (mass concentrations): dense cores of solidified basalt that create positive gravitational anomalies detectable by orbiting spacecraft; mascons can destabilise spacecraft orbits [Wikipedia: Mascon; NASA GRAIL mission]
- Mare Tranquillitatis is unusual among maria in lacking a central mascon; it also displays a slight bluish tint relative to the rest of the Moon, attributed to higher metal content in its basalts [Wikipedia: Mare Tranquillitatis]
- Maria contain sinuous rilles (channels carved by flowing lava) and wrinkle ridges (compressional features formed as lava flows cooled and contracted) [Britannica; Wikipedia: Lunar mare]
- Oceanus Procellarum ("Ocean of Storms") is the largest mare at ~4,000,000 km² and is the only one called an "oceanus"; unlike other maria it does not occupy a single circular impact basin — NASA GRAIL gravity data revealed ancient rectangular rift valley structures beneath it, suggesting tectonic/volcanic rather than pure impact-basin origins [Wikipedia: Oceanus Procellarum]
- All six Apollo crewed landings and several uncrewed Soviet Luna and American Surveyor/Ranger missions targeted maria, which offered flat, accessible terrain; Apollo 11 (1969) landed in Mare Tranquillitatis, Apollo 12 (1969) in Oceanus Procellarum [Wikipedia: Mare Tranquillitatis; Oceanus Procellarum]
- Only a handful of named maria exist on the lunar far side; Mare Ingenii and Mare Moscoviense are two of the most notable; Mare Moscoviense was discovered by the Soviet Luna 3 probe (1959) [Wikipedia: List of maria]

## Historical Context

- Ancient Greek philosophers (Thales, Democritus) regarded the Moon as rocky, with no true seas; Plutarch (c. 100 CE) compared Mare Crisium to Earth's Caspian Sea but attributed the dark patches to terrain rather than water [Wikipedia: Lunar mare]
- Pliny the Elder used the term "mascula" (spottedness) for the dark lunar patches — not the sea analogy [Wikipedia: Lunar mare]
- **Thomas Harriot** (England) was the first person to draw the Moon through a telescope, in 1609; his detailed map (1611) was not published and remained unknown until 1965 [Wikipedia: Lunar mare]
- **Galileo Galilei** published telescopic lunar observations in *Sidereus Nuncius* (1610), describing a rocky surface with craters; notably he made no reference to water or "mare" [Wikipedia: Lunar mare]
- **Johannes Kepler** appears to have introduced the term "maria" and "terrae" for the dark and bright lunar regions respectively, using it in *Dioptrice* (1611); the watery interpretation persisted despite Galileo's contrary evidence [Wikipedia: Lunar mare]
- **Michael van Langren** (Netherlands) produced the first published lunar map with named features in 1645; **Johannes Hevelius** (Poland) published a competing lunar atlas in 1647 (*Selenographia*), using place names from Earth for the dark plains [Wikipedia: Lunar mare; Riccioli]
- **Giovanni Battista Riccioli** (Italy), working with Francesco Maria Grimaldi, published the *Almagestum Novum* in 1651 containing a lunar map that used sea-related Latin names for the dark plains (e.g., *Mare Tranquillitatis*, *Mare Imbrium*) and named craters for astronomers; his nomenclature system was adopted internationally and remains the basis for IAU-approved lunar names today [Wikipedia: Riccioli; Lunar mare]
- The IAU (International Astronomical Union) formally codified lunar nomenclature in the 20th century; new maria discovered by Soviet probes (e.g., Mare Moscoviense, identified by Luna 3 in 1959) were proposed by the Soviet Union and approved by the IAU [Wikipedia: Lunar mare]
- **Robert Hooke** (*Micrographia*, 1665) first explicitly connected lunar maria and craters to volcanism [Wikipedia: Lunar mare]
- The true volcanic nature of maria was confirmed definitively by Apollo sample analysis (1969 onward): returned basalt samples were radiometrically dated to 3.1–3.8 Ga, and their mineralogy matched terrestrial flood basalts [Wikipedia: Lunar mare; NASA]
- **Etymology**: "mare" (Latin: sea), plural "maria"; pronounced MAH-ray / MAH-ree-uh in astronomical usage; the singular is occasionally written "mare" and mistakenly pronounced like the English word for a female horse [Britannica]

## Sub-types / Classifications

**By titanium content (geochemical):**
- **High-titanium basalts** — TiO₂ > ~6 wt.%; common in Mare Tranquillitatis and parts of Oceanus Procellarum; notably darker
- **Low-titanium basalts** — TiO₂ 1–6 wt.%; dominant type in most Apollo landing sites (e.g., Apollo 12, 15)
- **Very-low-titanium (VLT) basalts** — TiO₂ < 1 wt.%; discovered at Apollo 17 station; also found in lunar meteorites
[Wikipedia: Lunar mare; LPI Lunar Sourcebook]

**By size/designation (IAU nomenclature):**
- **Mare** — a standard-sized dark plain on the Moon (e.g., Mare Imbrium, Mare Crisium)
- **Oceanus** — reserved for the single largest dark plain: Oceanus Procellarum; no other feature on the Moon carries this designation
- **Lacus** — "lake"; a smaller dark region (e.g., Lacus Somniorum)
- **Sinus** — "bay"; a mare extension or inlet (e.g., Sinus Iridum)
- **Palus** — "swamp/marsh"; a small, irregular dark region (e.g., Palus Putredinis)
[IAU Gazetteer of Planetary Nomenclature; Wikipedia: Lunar mare]
