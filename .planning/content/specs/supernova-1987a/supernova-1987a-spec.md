## Definition
Supernova 1987A (SN 1987A) is a Type II supernova that occurred in the Large Magellanic Cloud on 23 February 1987, approximately 168,000 light-years from Earth — the closest supernova observed since Kepler's Supernova of 1604, and the first whose neutrino emissions were directly detected, marking the birth of neutrino astronomy.

## Full Content

- SN 1987A was a core-collapse supernova in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a dwarf satellite galaxy of the Milky Way, located approximately 168,000 light-years from Earth [Wikipedia, Britannica]
- It was the first supernova visible to the naked eye since Kepler's Supernova of 1604 and the closest observed in almost four centuries [Britannica]
- The progenitor star was Sanduleak −69 202 (Sk −69 202), a B3 blue supergiant — a surprise, since theoretical models at the time predicted only red supergiants as Type II progenitors [Wikipedia]
- The identification was confirmed when the star disappeared after the supernova faded [Wikipedia]
- Light from the explosion reached Earth on 23 February 1987; the supernova was designated "1987A" as the first supernova discovered that year [Wikipedia]

### Neutrino detection
- A burst of 25 antineutrinos was detected 2–3 hours before the arrival of visible light, at 07:35 UT on 23 February 1987, by three independent detectors: Kamiokande II (Japan, 12 events), IMB (USA, 8 events), and Baksan (USSR, 5 events) [Wikipedia]
- The burst lasted fewer than 13 seconds and carried a total energy of approximately 3 × 10⁴⁶ J, consistent with theoretical predictions for core collapse [Wikipedia]
- This was the first direct detection of neutrinos from a supernova and is widely considered the birth of neutrino astronomy [Wikipedia, CERN Courier]
- Masatoshi Koshiba shared the 2002 Nobel Prize in Physics for "pioneering contributions to astrophysics, in particular for the detection of cosmic neutrinos," work that included the Kamiokande II detection of SN 1987A neutrinos [NobelPrize.org]
- The detection placed an upper limit on the electron neutrino rest mass of < 16 eV/c² at 95% confidence [Wikipedia]

### Light curve and radioactive decay
- The light curve was powered initially by the decay of ⁵⁶Ni (half-life 6 days) to ⁵⁶Co, confirming theoretical predictions about radioactive energy sources in supernovae [Wikipedia]
- After the first few weeks, ⁵⁶Co decay (half-life 77.3 days) to ⁵⁶Fe dominated the luminosity [Wikipedia]
- Gamma-ray line emission from ⁵⁶Co and ⁵⁷Co was directly detected, providing the first observational confirmation that radioactive decay powers supernova light curves [Wikipedia]
- The present-day luminosity is powered by ⁴⁴Ti decay (half-life ~60 years), with a measured ⁴⁴Ti mass of (3.1 ± 0.8) × 10⁻⁴ M☉ [Wikipedia]

### Remnant structure
- Hubble Space Telescope images reveal a distinctive triple-ring structure: a bright inner equatorial ring and two fainter outer rings, composed of material ejected by the progenitor's stellar wind before the explosion [Wikipedia, NASA/ESA]
- The inner ring has a radius of 0.66 light-years (angular size 0.808 arcseconds) [Wikipedia]
- Around 2001, the supernova blast wave began colliding with the inner ring, producing bright hotspots visible in Hubble images [Wikipedia]
- The shock wave initially traveled at > 7,000 km/s, slowed to ~2,300 km/s during ring interaction, and re-accelerated to ~3,600 km/s by 2018 [Wikipedia]

### Dust formation
- Significant quantities of cold dust (~0.25 M☉ at ~26 K) were discovered in the ejecta by the Herschel Space Observatory in 2011 and confirmed by ALMA in 2014 [Wikipedia]
- Molecules including carbon monoxide (CO) and silicon monoxide (SiO) were detected in the ejecta [Wikipedia]
- These observations support the hypothesis that supernovae are major dust factories in the early universe [Wikipedia]

### Compact remnant — the neutron star
- The nature of the compact remnant was debated for decades: neutron star or black hole? [Wikipedia]
- In 2019, ALMA observations provided the first indirect evidence for a neutron star, hidden within the brightest dust clump at the centre of the remnant [Wikipedia]
- In 2021, hard X-ray emissions consistent with a pulsar wind nebula were detected by NuSTAR and Chandra [Wikipedia]
- In 2024, JWST observations (taken July 2022) identified ionised argon (Ar⁺ and Ar⁵⁺) and sulphur emission lines from the centre of the ejecta, requiring a source of high-energy ionising radiation — providing the strongest evidence yet for a young neutron star [NASA/JWST, Fransson et al. 2024, Science]

## Historical Context

- **23 February 1987**: Neutrino burst detected at 07:35 UT by Kamiokande II, IMB, and Baksan [Wikipedia]
- **24 February 1987**: Ian Shelton (University of Toronto) and Oscar Duhalde (Las Campanas Observatory, Chile) independently discovered the supernova visually; Albert Jones (New Zealand) confirmed it within hours [Wikipedia]
- **28 February 1987**: Progenitor identified as Sanduleak −69 202, a blue supergiant — overturning the assumption that only red supergiants produce Type II supernovae [Wikipedia]
- **May 1987**: Brightness peaked at apparent magnitude +2.9, making it visible to the naked eye in the Southern Hemisphere [Wikipedia]
- **1987–1990s**: Gamma-ray lines from ⁵⁶Co and ⁵⁷Co detected, confirming radioactive decay model [Wikipedia]
- **1990**: Hubble Space Telescope began imaging the triple-ring structure [NASA/ESA]
- **~2001**: Blast wave reached the inner ring, producing hotspots [Wikipedia]
- **2002**: Masatoshi Koshiba awarded Nobel Prize in Physics, partly for Kamiokande II neutrino detection from SN 1987A [NobelPrize.org]
- **2011–2014**: Herschel and ALMA detected massive cold dust reservoir in the ejecta [Wikipedia]
- **2019**: ALMA found indirect evidence for the neutron star [Wikipedia]
- **2024**: JWST detected ionised argon emission, providing the strongest evidence for the neutron star (Fransson et al., published in Science) [NASA/JWST]
- Naming: "SN 1987A" follows the standard IAU supernova naming convention — the year of discovery followed by a letter indicating the order of discovery within that year [Wikipedia]

## Sub-types / Classifications

SN 1987A is classified as a Type II supernova (core-collapse), but is considered "peculiar" within that class for several reasons:
- **Blue supergiant progenitor**: Most Type II supernovae arise from red supergiants; SN 1987A's B3 blue supergiant progenitor was unexpected [Wikipedia]
- **Unusual light curve**: The light curve shape differs from the standard Type II-P (plateau) and Type II-L (linear) subtypes, partly because the compact blue supergiant produced a faster-rising, lower-peak light curve than a red supergiant would have [Wikipedia]
- **Triple-ring nebula**: The distinctive ring system around the remnant is unlike typical supernova remnants and is thought to result from the progenitor's complex mass-loss history, possibly involving a binary merger ~20,000 years before the explosion [Wikipedia]
