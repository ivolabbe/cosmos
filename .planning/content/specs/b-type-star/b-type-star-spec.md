# B-type Star — Article Spec

## Category
stars

## Definition
A B-type star is a hot, luminous star with a surface temperature between roughly 10,000 and 33,000 K and spectral features dominated by neutral helium absorption lines and moderately strong hydrogen (Balmer) lines. B-type stars are classified as the second-hottest broad spectral class in the Morgan–Keenan (MK) system, sitting between the hotter O-type stars and the cooler A-type stars.

## Full Content

### Overview
- Classified under the Morgan–Keenan (MK) system, denoted by the letter B; subdivided B0 (hottest) through B9 (coolest) [Wikipedia: Stellar classification]
- Appear blue-white to the unaided eye; intrinsically among the most luminous stars in the Galaxy [Britannica: stellar-classification]
- Only ~0.125% of main-sequence stars in the solar neighbourhood are B-type; the nearest is Regulus, at ~80 light-years [Wikipedia: B-type main-sequence star]
- Classified as extreme Population I objects, concentrated in the spiral arms of the Milky Way [Britannica: B-type star]

### Physical Properties
- Surface temperature: ~10,700–31,400 K (main sequence; up to ~33,000 K in some references) [Wikipedia: B-type main-sequence star; Wikipedia: Stellar classification]
- Mass: ~2.75–18 solar masses [Wikipedia: B-type main-sequence star]
- Radius: ~2.49–7.16 solar radii (B9V to B0V) [Wikipedia: B-type main-sequence star]
- Luminosity: ~73–44,875 solar luminosities (B9V to B0V) [Wikipedia: B-type main-sequence star]
- Rapid rotation common: equatorial velocities ~200 km/s [Wikipedia: B-type main-sequence star]
- Strong stellar winds: ~3,000 km/s wind velocities; mass-loss rates exceed those of smaller stars [Wikipedia: B-type main-sequence star]
- No outer convection zone and no corona; energy generation via the CNO cycle creates a convective core [Wikipedia: B-type main-sequence star]

### Spectral Features
- Defining signature: neutral helium (He I) absorption lines, strongest at B2; weaker in early (B0) and late (B9) subtypes [Wikipedia: Stellar classification]
- Hydrogen Balmer lines present and grow stronger across the B sequence, peaking at A2 [Wikipedia: B-type main-sequence star]
- Early-B supergiants show Si IV and Si III lines; late-B stars show Mg II relative to He I [Wikipedia: Stellar classification]
- Lines of singly ionised oxygen, nitrogen, and neon present in hotter B-type stars such as Epsilon Orionis [Britannica: B-type star]

### Key Examples
- **Rigel** (β Orionis, B8 Ia): blue supergiant, ~850 ly distant, T ≈ 12,100 K, ~120,000 L☉, radius ~74 R☉, mass ~21 M☉ [Wikipedia: Rigel]
- **Spica** (α Virginis, B1 III-IV + B2V binary): ~250 ly distant, T ≈ 25,300 K (primary), 20,500 L☉, 11.43 M☉; spectroscopic binary with 4-day orbit [Wikipedia: Spica]
- **Regulus** (α Leonis, B8 IV-V): nearest prominent B-type star, ~80 ly, rapid rotator [Wikipedia: Stellar classification]
- **Achernar** (α Eridani, B6 Ve): brightest Be star; extreme oblate shape — equatorial radius 50% larger than polar radius, confirmed by VLTI interferometry in 2003 [ESO press release eso0316; Wikipedia: Be star]
- **Alkaid** (η Ursae Majoris, B3V): classic B main-sequence standard [Wikipedia: Stellar classification]
- **Alnilam** (ε Orionis, B0 Ia): blue supergiant in Orion's Belt; spectral standard [Wikipedia: Stellar classification]
- **Gamma Cassiopeiae**: first star identified with Balmer emission lines (1866), prototype Be star [Wikipedia: Be star]

### Subtypes and Variants

#### Be Stars
- "Classical Be stars" are rapidly rotating non-supergiant B stars that show (or have shown) one or more Balmer emission lines [Wikipedia: Be star]
- Emission arises from a circumstellar gaseous decretion disk, ejected by rapid rotation and possibly non-radial pulsations [Wikipedia: Be star]
- Disk causes infrared excess and polarisation; when viewed edge-on, narrow "shell" absorption lines appear [Wikipedia: Be star]
- Rotation rates among the fastest of any stellar class; often near critical rotation velocity [Wikipedia: Be star]
- Achernar (1976), Gamma Cassiopeiae (1866) are the two best-known historical examples [Wikipedia: Be star]

#### Chemically Peculiar B Stars (Bp)
- **Helium-strong stars** (B0–B3): unusually strong He I lines; strong surface magnetic fields [Wikipedia: B-type main-sequence star]
- **Helium-weak stars**: understrength helium lines; strong hydrogen spectra [Wikipedia: B-type main-sequence star]
- **Silicon/Bp stars** (Ap/Bp): overabundances of Si, Sr, Cr, Eu; slow rotation; detectable magnetic fields 100 G – 30 kG [Wikipedia: Chemically peculiar star]
- **Mercury-manganese (HgMn) stars** (B7–B9): distinctive Hg II line at 398.4 nm; no detected magnetic fields; very slow rotation [Wikipedia: B-type main-sequence star]

### Stellar Evolution
- Main-sequence lifetime: 10–100 million years (decreasing with mass) [Wikipedia: B-type main-sequence star; astrobackyard.com]
- Burn hydrogen via the CNO cycle in a convective core [Wikipedia: B-type main-sequence star]
- Post-main-sequence: evolve into blue or red supergiants, then explode as core-collapse supernovae [Wikipedia: B-type main-sequence star]
- Remnants: neutron stars (most commonly) or stellar black holes depending on mass [Wikipedia: B-type main-sequence star]
- High-mass B stars (B0–B2) can evolve into Wolf-Rayet stars before supernova [general astrophysics reference]
- Runaway B stars: ejected from OB associations via binary supernova or dynamical encounters; some found at high Galactic latitudes [arXiv: 2511.20566; NASA: Runaway Star]

### OB Associations
- B-type stars commonly form alongside O-type stars in OB associations — loose groups of young massive stars in spiral arms [arXiv: astro-ph/0010057]
- OB associations expand outward over time; most B-type stars found within or near their birth clusters during the first few tens of millions of years [arXiv: 2511.20566]

### Historical Context
- Angelo Secchi (1860s) first recognised spectral types; B-type stars fell in his helium-rich "Orion subtype" of Class I [Wikipedia: Stellar classification]
- Williamina Fleming (1890 Henry Draper Catalogue): classified B as stars with slightly weaker hydrogen lines than A [Wikipedia: Stellar classification]
- Antonia Maury (1897): placed Orion-type (modern B) before modern A, correctly ordering spectral sequence by temperature [Wikipedia: Stellar classification]
- Annie Jump Cannon (1901): consolidated O, B, A, F, G, K, M sequence; adopted by astronomical community c. 1920 [Wikipedia: Stellar classification]

## Historical Context

B-type stars occupy a central place in the history of stellar spectroscopy. The class was established through the Harvard spectral survey of the late 19th century. Williamina Fleming first catalogued B stars as having slightly weaker hydrogen absorption than A stars in the 1890 Henry Draper Catalogue. Antonia Maury correctly identified that these hot helium-rich "Orion stars" were hotter than A-type stars, reversing their earlier alphabetical order. Annie Jump Cannon's 1901 reorganisation — placing O before B before A — produced the OBAFGKM sequence still in use today. The discovery of Be stars dates to 1866, when Angelo Secchi noted emission lines in the spectrum of Gamma Cassiopeiae, but the physical explanation (circumstellar disk + rapid rotation) took another century to establish. ESO's 2003 VLTI interferometric measurement of Achernar, revealing an oblateness of 56%, was the most dramatic observational confirmation of rapid rotation effects in a Be star to date.
