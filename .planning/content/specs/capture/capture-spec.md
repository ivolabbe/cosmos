## Summary

"Capture" in astronomy refers to the process by which one body permanently enters a bound orbit around another under gravity — converting what was a hyperbolic or unbound trajectory into an elliptical orbit. The term also applies to two distinct nuclear processes: neutron capture and electron capture (K-capture). An encyclopedia article under this slug should cover all three meanings clearly.

---

## 1. Gravitational Capture (Orbital Mechanics)

### Definition
Gravitational capture occurs when a body on a hyperbolic (unbound) orbit loses sufficient energy to fall below escape velocity and enter a stable, closed orbit around a larger body. [Wikipedia: Gravitational capture] Because energy must be removed from the system, a purely two-body encounter cannot produce permanent capture — a third body or dissipative mechanism is always required. [Britannica: capture, celestial mechanics]

### The Hill Sphere Constraint
A planet dominates the orbits of bodies within its **Hill sphere** (also called the Roche sphere), the region where the planet's gravity exceeds the tidal perturbation from the Sun. For Jupiter the Hill sphere radius is ~0.35 AU; for Neptune ~0.77 AU. A passing body that enters the Hill sphere and loses enough energy will be permanently captured. [Wikipedia: Hill sphere]

### Three-Body Capture
The most general capture mechanism involves three gravitating bodies. In the restricted three-body problem a small body (e.g., an asteroid) moves in the combined gravity of two larger masses (e.g., Sun + planet). Within the planet's Hill sphere, particle trajectories can enter a region of **chaotic** phase space in which the body is neither bound nor clearly ejected — it may linger for many orbital periods. Any dissipative process (gas drag, binary splitting, collision) occurring during this temporary capture window can make the orbit permanent. This is called **chaos-assisted capture (CAC)**. [Astakhov et al. 2004, MNRAS 354, 971] [Phil, Hamilton & Agnor 2010, UMD preprint]

### Energy-Loss Mechanisms

| Mechanism | Context | Key examples |
|-----------|---------|--------------|
| **Gas drag** | Early solar system; planet embedded in gas disk | Proposed for some irregular moons; gas-drag capture of Triton (McKinnon & Leith 1995) |
| **Binary dissociation** | Two co-orbiting bodies approach a planet; tidal force separates them; one escapes, carrying away energy; other is captured | Triton's likely capture (Agnor & Hamilton 2006, *Nature* 441, 192) |
| **Tidal circularization** | Post-capture; eccentric orbit circularizes via tidal dissipation | Triton's orbit became nearly circular over ~Gyr [Wikipedia: Capture of Triton] |
| **Collisions** | Impact within Hill sphere removes kinetic energy | Proposed for some small irregular moons |

### Conditions for Permanent Capture
- Body must be moving more slowly than escape velocity after energy loss. [Wikipedia: Gravitational capture]
- Binary dissociation is most effective when (i) the binary's mutual orbital speed is high, and/or (ii) the encounter speed with the planet is low. [Agnor & Hamilton 2006]

## 2. Captured Objects in the Solar System

### Irregular Moons of the Giant Planets
Every giant planet hosts a population of **irregular moons**: small satellites on distant, inclined, often retrograde orbits far outside the planet's regular (prograde, near-equatorial) satellite system. [Britannica: capture, celestial mechanics] These orbits are signatures of capture: a body forming in situ with the planet would inherit the planet's prograde rotation sense and lie near the equatorial plane.

- **Jupiter** has ~95 known moons; the irregular moons include the **Himalia group** (prograde, ~C-type asteroid colours), thought to be fragments of a single captured C- or D-type asteroid. [Wikipedia: Himalia group] [NASA Science: Himalia]
- **Saturn** has over 140 known moons; irregulars are grouped into the prograde **Inuit group** (~38 moons) and the retrograde **Norse group** (over 200 moons). [Wikipedia: Moons of Saturn]
- **Phoebe** (Saturn's largest irregular, diameter ~213 km) has a retrograde orbit, a surface resembling C-type asteroids and Chiron-class outer Solar System bodies, and is thought to be a captured Kuiper Belt object. Cassini confirmed water ice on its surface. [Johnson et al. 2005, *Science* 307 — Saturn's moon Phoebe as a captured body from the outer Solar System; PubMed 15875015]
- **Uranus and Neptune** also host irregular moon families whose orbital clustering implies capture of disrupted parent bodies. [Wikimedia: Neptune irregular moon orbits, Jan 2021]

### Triton: The Clearest Capture Case
**Triton** is Neptune's largest moon (diameter 2707 km) and the only large moon in the Solar System on a **retrograde** orbit — revolving opposite to Neptune's spin. [Wikipedia: Triton (moon)] This alone rules out in-situ formation.

Evidence for Kuiper Belt origin:
- Triton's density (~2.06 g cm⁻³), surface composition (N₂, CO, CO₂, CH₄ ices), and size are consistent with large KBOs such as Pluto and Eris. [NASA Science: Triton]
- McKinnon (1984) first proposed Triton and Pluto are kindred outer Solar System icy planetesimals. [Wikipedia: Capture of Triton]

Capture mechanism — **binary dissociation** (leading hypothesis): Before capture, Triton was half of a binary KBO analogous to the Pluto–Charon system. When the binary encountered Neptune, tidal forces unbound the pair: Triton's companion was ejected (carrying away the excess energy) and Triton entered a bound orbit. [Agnor & Hamilton 2006, *Nature* 441, 192; Space.com 2006]

Post-capture evolution: Triton's initial orbit was highly eccentric. Tidal dissipation inside Triton (tides raised by Neptune) and inside Neptune circularized the orbit over ~1–2 Gyr. During this process Triton may have melted entirely and possibly sustains a subsurface liquid-water ocean today (Gaeman et al. 2012). [Wikipedia: Capture of Triton] Triton's arrival also disrupted any pre-existing regular moons of Neptune; debris re-accreted into today's small inner moons. Triton will pass within Neptune's Roche limit in ~3.6 Gyr (Chyba et al.). [Wikipedia: Capture of Triton]

### Captured Asteroids and Co-Orbitals
Small bodies can be temporarily or semi-permanently captured as **quasi-satellites** or into **horseshoe orbits** (1:1 resonance with a planet). [Wikipedia: 3753 Cruithne]
- **3753 Cruithne** is a Q-type, Aten-class asteroid in 1:1 resonance with Earth; relative to Earth it traces a kidney-bean path over ~770-year cycles. It is NOT in Earth's Hill sphere and thus is not a true moon. [Wikipedia: 3753 Cruithne]
- **469219 Kamo'oalewa** is a true Earth quasi-satellite — spending significant time within Earth's Hill sphere in a stable co-orbital state.
- **2006 RH120** (~5 m) was temporarily captured into Earth orbit 2006–2007, completing several orbits before escaping — a brief true capture. [EarthSky]

Captured asteroids with permanent status are rare; most "captures" are temporary. The giant planets, with their large Hill spheres and (during the early solar system) dense gas envelopes, were far more efficient at permanent capture than the terrestrial planets.

### Binary Star Capture
In dense stellar environments (globular cluster cores, galactic nuclei) two unbound stars can form a **tidal capture binary** during a close hyperbolic encounter. At closest approach, dynamical tides excite oscillation modes in each star, extracting kinetic energy from the orbit and converting it to internal energy. If sufficient energy is removed, the stars become bound. [Mardling 1995, ApJ; Kroupa 2001, MNRAS 321, 398; Kiseleva et al. 1992, ApJ 385, 604]

Tidal capture binaries are relevant to:
- Formation of **blue straggler** stars in clusters (merger or mass-transfer aftermath)
- Progenitors of **gravitational-wave sources** (compact binary mergers)
- Formation of **millisecond pulsars** (neutron star + companion)
[MNRAS 457, 1339 (2016); A&A 2025 cluster study]

## 3. Neutron Capture (Nuclear Process)

### Definition
Neutron capture is a nuclear reaction in which an atomic nucleus absorbs one or more free neutrons, forming a heavier (and usually excited) nucleus. [Wikipedia: Neutron capture] Because neutrons carry no electric charge they experience no Coulomb barrier and can penetrate nuclei at thermal energies.

### Astrophysical Importance
Nuclei with mass number A > 56 cannot be built by exothermic fusion (the binding energy per nucleon peaks at iron-56). Virtually all elements heavier than iron are synthesised by neutron capture. [Wikipedia: Neutron capture]

### The s-process (slow neutron capture)
- Operates in asymptotic giant branch (AGB) stars and massive stars.
- Neutron flux is low enough that each capture is followed by beta decay before the next capture.
- Builds elements along the valley of stability up to bismuth (Bi, Z=83).
- Typical neutron densities: ~10⁷–10¹¹ cm⁻³. [Wikipedia: Neutron capture]

### The r-process (rapid neutron capture)
- Requires extremely high neutron fluxes (~10²⁰–10²⁴ cm⁻³).
- Nuclei capture many neutrons faster than they beta-decay, producing very neutron-rich isotopes far from the stability valley, which then decay to stable heavy elements including gold, platinum, uranium, and thorium.
- Confirmed astrophysical sites: neutron star mergers (kilonova; GW170817 detected in gravitational waves + electromagnetic follow-up 2017). Possible additional site: core-collapse supernovae. [Wikipedia: r-process; Cowan et al. 2021, Rev. Mod. Phys. 93, 015002]
- Specific line: neutron capture on protons yields a 2.223 MeV gamma-ray line observable in solar flares. [Wikipedia: Neutron capture]

### Example Reaction
¹⁹⁷Au + n → ¹⁹⁸Au* → ¹⁹⁸Au + γ  (natural gold-197 captures a neutron, emitting a gamma ray) [Wikipedia: Neutron capture]

## 4. Electron Capture / K-Capture (Nuclear Process)

### Definition
Electron capture (also called **K-capture** or **K-electron capture**) is a weak-interaction decay mode in which a proton-rich nucleus absorbs an inner-shell atomic electron — usually from the K shell (closest to nucleus), sometimes from the L shell — converting a proton into a neutron and emitting an electron neutrino:
```
p + e⁻ → n + νₑ
```
The atomic number decreases by 1; the mass number is unchanged. [Wikipedia: Electron capture]

### Stellar Physics Context
- In **white dwarf interiors** and **collapsing stellar cores** (where electron density is extreme), electron capture converts free protons (or protons in nuclei) to neutrons. This is a key step in core-collapse supernovae: as the core contracts to nuclear density, electron capture accelerates neutronization, reducing the electron degeneracy pressure that was holding the core up and triggering collapse. [Wikipedia: Electron capture]
- In the outer envelope of a supernova, fully ionized atoms may avoid beta decay because there are no free electrons to capture — modifying nucleosynthesis pathways (r-process survival). [Wikipedia: Electron capture]
- **K-capture** is an alternative name for the same process, historically used because the innermost electron shell is called the K shell (Bohr's labelling). The COSMOS slug `k-capture` is a related but separate article. [pipeline-status.md]

### Examples of Electron Capture Nuclides
| Isotope | Half-life | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| ⁷Be | 53.28 days | Produced in solar fusion; EC to ⁷Li |
| ⁵⁷Co | 271.8 days | Used in nuclear medicine; EC to ⁵⁷Fe |
| ⁴⁰K | 1.25 × 10⁹ yr | Geologically important; partial EC to ⁴⁰Ar |

[Wikipedia: Electron capture]

Threshold: electron capture occurs preferentially when the decay energy (Q-value) is less than 1.022 MeV — below the threshold for positron emission (which requires 2mₑc² of energy). [Wikipedia: Electron capture]

## 5. Connecting the Three Senses

The COSMOS article should make clear at the outset that "capture" carries three distinct meanings:
1. **Gravitational/orbital capture** — a body permanently enters orbit under gravity (main focus)
2. **Neutron capture** — a nucleus absorbs a neutron (s-process, r-process; heavy element synthesis)
3. **Electron capture / K-capture** — a nucleus absorbs an orbital electron (weak-force decay; important in stellar collapse and solar fusion)

Note: the slug `k-capture` exists separately in the pipeline. The `capture` article should link to it rather than duplicate material.
