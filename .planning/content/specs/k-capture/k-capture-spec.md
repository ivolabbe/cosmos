# K Capture — Article Spec

## Category
Physics (Nuclear Physics, Radioactive Decay, Weak Interaction)

## Slug
`k-capture`

## Definition (1–2 sentences)
K capture is a form of radioactive decay in which the nucleus of a proton-rich atom absorbs one of its own electrons from the innermost (K) electron shell, converting a proton into a neutron and emitting an electron neutrino. The process is mediated by the weak nuclear force and leaves a vacancy in the K shell that is subsequently filled by outer-shell electrons, producing characteristic X-rays or Auger electrons.

## Full Content (ALL facts needed for 200–400 words)

### The Process and Weak Interaction
K capture—formally called K-electron capture or K-shell electron capture—belongs to the family of beta decay processes, all mediated by the weak nuclear force [Wikipedia: Electron capture]. The fundamental reaction is:

> p + e⁻ → n + νₑ

A proton in the nucleus captures an inner-shell electron and converts to a neutron, while an electron neutrino (νₑ) is emitted carrying the full decay energy. Unlike beta-minus decay (which emits an electron) or positron emission (which emits a positron), K capture produces no charged particle in the primary reaction—only the neutrino escapes the nucleus [Wikipedia: Electron capture].

The "K" in K capture refers to the principal quantum number n = 1 shell (K shell), the innermost electron shell, which has the highest electron density at the nucleus. Electrons from the L shell (n = 2) and higher shells may also be captured in analogous L-capture and M-capture processes, but these are far less probable because the wavefunction overlap with the nucleus is smaller [Wikipedia: Electron capture].

### Energy Conditions: When K Capture Occurs
K capture occurs when the mass difference between parent and daughter atom is positive but less than 1.022 MeV (twice the electron rest-mass energy). If the mass difference exceeds 1.022 MeV, positron emission becomes energetically possible and typically competes with K capture. If the mass difference is less than 1.022 MeV, positron emission is forbidden and K capture is the only available decay pathway [Wikipedia: Electron capture]. The Q-value for K capture equals:

> Q = (M_parent − M_daughter) × c²

where masses are atomic (not nuclear) masses.

### Characteristic X-Rays and Auger Electrons
When the K-shell vacancy is created, an electron from an outer shell (typically L or M shell) falls in to fill the gap. The energy released—equal to the difference in binding energies between the two shells—is emitted as a characteristic X-ray photon specific to the daughter element [Wikipedia: Electron capture; Wikipedia: Characteristic X-ray]. For example, K-shell X-rays of lithium-7 (from ⁷Be decay) lie in the soft X-ray range.

Alternatively, this energy can be transferred internally to eject another outer electron—the Auger effect—producing an Auger electron and leaving the daughter atom doubly ionized [Wikipedia: Electron capture]. Whether X-ray emission or the Auger effect dominates depends on the element's atomic number: lighter elements favour the Auger effect, heavier elements favour X-ray emission (fluorescence yield increases with Z).

### History
Gian-Carlo Wick first proposed the theoretical possibility of electron capture in 1934. Luis Alvarez made the first experimental observation of K-electron capture in vanadium-48 in 1937 using a Geiger counter to detect the characteristic X-rays [Wikipedia: Electron capture].

### Key Examples

**Beryllium-7 (⁷Be)**
Half-life: 53.28 days [Wikipedia: Electron capture]. Decays exclusively by K capture (no positron emission possible, Q ≈ 0.862 MeV < 1.022 MeV):
> ⁷₄Be + e⁻ → ⁷₃Li + νₑ

The emitted neutrinos are monoenergetic: 90% carry 0.861 MeV (to ⁷Li ground state) and 10% carry 0.383 MeV (to ⁷Li excited state) [Wikipedia: Proton-proton chain reaction]. This is the only isotope where the decay rate has been observed to differ measurably (by ~0.9%) between metallic and insulating chemical environments—because the valence electrons of beryllium sit unusually close to the nucleus [Wikipedia: Electron capture].

**Potassium-40 (⁴⁰K)**
Half-life: 1.248 × 10⁹ years [Wikipedia: Potassium-40]. ⁴⁰K has two decay modes:
- Beta-minus decay to ⁴⁰Ca: ~89.3% of decays
- Electron capture to ⁴⁰Ar: ~10.7% of decays [Wikipedia: Potassium-40]

About 99% of K-capture decays produce an excited state of ⁴⁰Ar, which de-excites by emitting a 1.46 MeV gamma ray. The remaining ~0.1% (ground-state K capture) has a branching ratio of approximately 0.098% of total ⁴⁰K decays—roughly half the value previously assumed, per a recent precision measurement [USGS: First observation of ground-state electron-capture of ⁴⁰K, 2023]. The accumulation of ⁴⁰Ar from K capture is the basis of potassium-argon (K-Ar) and argon-argon (Ar-Ar) geochronology, used to date rocks from millions to billions of years old [Wikipedia: K-Ar dating].

**Gallium-67 (⁶⁷Ga)**
Half-life: 3.260 days. Used in nuclear medicine for tumour and infection imaging; decays by K capture to ⁶⁷Zn [Wikipedia: Electron capture].

### Astrophysical Significance

**The pp-chain in stellar interiors**
In the interior of main-sequence stars such as the Sun, ⁷Be produced by ³He + ⁴He fusion captures a free electron from the surrounding plasma, yielding ⁷Li + νₑ (pp-II branch). This branch accounts for approximately 16.4% of the total solar luminosity [Wikipedia: Proton-proton chain reaction]. The Be-7 neutrinos are among the most studied solar neutrino species and were observed by the Homestake Experiment and subsequently by Borexino [Nature 2018: Comprehensive measurement of pp-chain solar neutrinos]. The electron capture rate in a stellar plasma differs from the laboratory rate because it depends on temperature, density, and the degree of ionisation—at solar interior conditions (~15 MK, density ~150 g cm⁻³), Be-7 is fully ionised and captures free electrons from the plasma continuum rather than from a bound K shell [A&A 2019: Effects of a revised ⁷Be e⁻-capture rate on solar neutrino fluxes].

**Nucleosynthesis**
Electron capture by proton-rich nuclei in stellar interiors and explosive environments shifts nuclear abundances toward neutron-rich species. During silicon burning and explosive nucleosynthesis in massive stars, a cascade of electron captures on iron-group nuclei reduces the electron fraction Yₑ and shifts the composition toward neutron-rich isotopes [Wikipedia: Supernova].

**Electron-capture supernovae**
Stars with initial masses of approximately 8–10 M☉ develop degenerate oxygen-neon-magnesium cores. When the core reaches a critical density (~4 × 10⁹ g cm⁻³), electrons are captured by ²⁴Mg and ²⁰Ne nuclei in rapid succession, reducing electron pressure (which supports the degenerate core against gravity) and triggering gravitational collapse—an electron-capture supernova [Kavli IPMU 2020; Janka et al. A&A 2016]. These events produce neutron stars with relatively low kick velocities and may explain some millisecond pulsars in binary systems [arXiv: 2509.25915].

### Neutrino Emission and Detection
Because K capture emits only a neutrino and no charged particle, the decay is inherently difficult to detect directly. The recoiling daughter nucleus carries a single characteristic momentum equal and opposite to the neutrino, providing indirect evidence of the decay. The neutrino energy is monoenergetic (for a given transition between definite nuclear states), which makes K-capture sources—especially ⁷Be—valuable calibration sources for neutrino detectors [Physics Forums]. The Borexino experiment at Gran Sasso measured the ⁷Be solar neutrino flux to better than 3% precision [Nature 2018].

### Comparison with Related Processes
| Process | Particle emitted | Condition |
|---------|-----------------|-----------|
| Beta-minus decay | e⁻, ν̄ₑ | Neutron-rich nucleus |
| Positron emission | e⁺, νₑ | Q > 1.022 MeV, proton-rich |
| K capture | νₑ only | Q > 0, proton-rich; sole mode if Q < 1.022 MeV |
| Inverse beta decay | e⁺ from p + ν̄ₑ | Anti-neutrino interaction (distinct process) |

Note: "inverse beta decay" is sometimes loosely applied to K capture, but more precisely refers to p + ν̄ₑ → n + e⁺, a distinct reaction [Wikipedia: Electron capture].

### Detection Methods
1. **Characteristic X-ray detection**: Counting the K-shell X-rays of the daughter element identifies the decay and measures its rate. This was the method used by Alvarez (1937).
2. **Neutrino detection**: Neutrino telescopes and underground detectors (e.g., Borexino, Super-Kamiokande) detect the monoenergetic neutrinos from ⁷Be K capture in the Sun.
3. **Coincidence counting**: Correlating X-ray/Auger signals with daughter nucleus recoil provides high-confidence identification.
4. **Gamma-ray spectroscopy**: For ⁴⁰K, the 1.46 MeV gamma ray from the excited ⁴⁰Ar daughter is detected in environmental radioactivity surveys and geological dating.

## Historical Timeline

- **1934**: Gian-Carlo Wick proposes the theoretical possibility of electron capture from atomic shells.
- **1937**: Luis Alvarez observes K-electron capture in ⁴⁸V via characteristic X-ray detection.
- **1938**: Capturing electrons from the L shell (L capture) also recognised.
- **1960s**: K-Ar geochronology matures, exploiting ⁴⁰K → ⁴⁰Ar for rock dating.
- **1987–present**: Solar neutrino experiments (Homestake, Kamiokande, Borexino) detect and precisely measure ⁷Be solar neutrinos arising from K capture in the Sun.
- **2004**: Confirmed observation of an electron-capture supernova progenitor class.
- **2023**: First precise measurement of the ⁴⁰K ground-state electron-capture branching ratio (~0.098%) with implications for geochronology [USGS].

## Sub-types / Variants

1. **K capture (K-electron capture)**: Capture from the n = 1 (K) shell — most probable because electron density at nucleus is highest.
2. **L capture**: Capture from the n = 2 (L) shell — occurs when K capture is energetically forbidden or when K shell is empty.
3. **Bound-state beta decay**: The inverse: a neutron in a fully ionised atom decays and the emitted electron is captured into a bound atomic orbit rather than emitted freely (distinct but related).
4. **Electron-capture supernovae**: A whole class of stellar explosions driven by rapid electron capture on neon and magnesium nuclei in degenerate stellar cores.
