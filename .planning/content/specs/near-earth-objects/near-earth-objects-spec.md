## Definition
A Near-Earth Object (NEO) is any small Solar System body — asteroid or comet — whose orbit around the Sun brings it within 1.3 astronomical units (AU) of the Sun (i.e., within 0.3 AU of Earth's orbit). They are the most accessible small bodies in the Solar System and include a subset of objects large enough and close enough to Earth's orbit to pose a potential impact hazard.

## Full Content

### Physical Nature and Definition
- NEOs are small Solar System bodies — asteroids and comets — with perihelia < 1.3 AU, bringing them into Earth's neighbourhood [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
- The 1.3 AU threshold is set by the IAU; it encompasses all objects that could plausibly approach or cross Earth's orbit [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
- Approximately 99.67% of NEOs are asteroids (NEAs); only 0.33% are comets (NECs) [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
- Most NEAs are rocky, metallic, or carbonaceous remnants from the early Solar System, compositionally similar to main-belt asteroids [NASA Science]
- NEAs span a wide size range: from sub-metre fragments to 1036 Ganymed (~38 km equivalent diameter) [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]

### Origins and Orbital Evolution
- The majority of NEAs originate in the main asteroid belt and are nudged into near-Earth orbits by gravitational resonances with Jupiter, particularly through Kirkwood gap resonances [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object — Origins]
- The Yarkovsky effect — a subtle radiation-pressure force from uneven thermal emission — slowly migrates asteroids into these resonance zones, providing a steady replenishment of the NEO population over billions of years [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object — Origins]
- A small fraction (~few percent) of NEAs are thought to be extinct cometary nuclei that have lost their surface volatiles and no longer display cometary activity [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object — Origins]
- Near-Earth comets (NECs) include short-period comets (period < 200 years), Halley-type comets, and some long-period comets that pass within 1.3 AU [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
- The NEO population is continuously replenished; the main belt has lost less than 6% of its mass to this migration over 3.5 billion years [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object — Origins]

### Sub-types by Orbit
- NEAs are divided into four dynamical groups based on semi-major axis and perihelion/aphelion distances:
  - **Apollos**: a > 1 AU, perihelion < 1.017 AU; cross Earth's orbit; most numerous group (56.5%) [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
  - **Amors**: 1.017 AU < perihelion < 1.3 AU; orbit just outside Earth's orbit and do not currently cross it; 35.1% of NEAs [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
  - **Atens**: a < 1 AU, aphelion > 0.983 AU; mostly inside Earth's orbit but cross it [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
  - **Atiras** (also called Inner Earth Objects): orbits entirely within Earth's orbit (aphelion < 0.983 AU); rarest group with only 34 known [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
- Near-Earth comets include Jupiter-family comets and Halley-type comets whose orbits carry them within 1.3 AU [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]

### Potentially Hazardous Objects (PHOs/PHAs)
- A subset of NEOs is classified as Potentially Hazardous Objects (PHOs) — those with a Minimum Orbit Intersection Distance (MOID) < 0.05 AU (~7.5 million km) and absolute magnitude H ≤ 22 (diameter ≥ ~140 m) [Wikipedia: Potentially Hazardous Object]
- As of early 2025, 2,473 PHAs are known; more than 99% pose no impact threat within the next 100 years [Wikipedia: Potentially Hazardous Object]
- The largest known PHA is (53319) 1999 JM8, with a mean diameter of approximately 7 km [Wikipedia: Potentially Hazardous Object]
- PHO impacts are estimated to occur on average roughly once per 10,000 years [Wikipedia: Potentially Hazardous Object]

### Impact Hazard Assessment
- Two standardised scales communicate impact risk to the public:
  - **Torino Scale (0–10)**: combines impact probability and kinetic energy over the next 100 years; 0 = negligible, 10 = certain global catastrophe; no object has ever exceeded Level 4 [Wikipedia: Torino scale]
  - **Palermo Scale**: logarithmic comparison of impact probability versus background rate; objects below −2 typically require no special concern [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
- Estimated impact frequencies scale steeply with size: ~4 m objects hit annually, ~60 m objects every 1,300 years, ~1 km objects every 440,000 years [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
- The Chelyabinsk event (Feb 15, 2013) demonstrated the hazard of smaller, undetected NEOs: an ~18 m asteroid entered undetected over Russia, releasing 400–500 kt TNT equivalent energy, injuring ~1,491 people and damaging 7,200+ buildings — yet produced no deaths [Wikipedia: Chelyabinsk meteor]

### Discovery and Surveys
- The first known near-Earth asteroid, 433 Eros, was discovered in 1898 by Carl Gustav Witt and Auguste Charlois [Wikipedia: 433 Eros]
- Systematic surveys began in the 1990s: LINEAR, Spacewatch, NEAT, Catalina Sky Survey, and NEOWISE are the primary detection programmes [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
- A 1998 US Congressional mandate required NASA to catalogue 90% of NEOs ≥ 1 km by 2008; this goal was largely achieved by 2011 [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
- As of December 2024, 37,378 NEOs are known; discovery rates continue to accelerate [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
- The Vera C. Rubin Observatory (first light ~2025) and NEO Surveyor satellite (~2027) are expected to dramatically increase completeness for objects ≥ 140 m [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
- Observational biases favour detecting larger, brighter, higher-albedo objects; space-based infrared surveys (e.g. NEOWISE) overcome bias by measuring thermal emission [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]

### Planetary Defense
- NASA's Planetary Defense Coordination Office (PDCO) and Center for Near-Earth Object Studies (CNEOS) coordinate detection, tracking, and risk assessment in the United States; ESA operates the Near-Earth Object Coordination Centre (NEOCC) [NASA Science; Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
- The Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) mission impacted the moonlet Dimorphos on September 26, 2022, shortening its orbital period by 32 minutes — far exceeding the 73-second success threshold — and proving kinetic impactor deflection is viable [Wikipedia: DART spacecraft]
- Momentum transfer from DART was 3.6× greater than the spacecraft's own impact momentum alone, due to ejected debris [Wikipedia: DART spacecraft]
- 99942 Apophis reached Torino scale level 4 in December 2004 (probability 1-in-37 of 2029 impact) — the highest ever assigned to any object; precovery observations eliminated the threat [Wikipedia: 99942 Apophis]
- Apophis will make a historically close flyby in 2029 at ~31,600 km — closer than geostationary satellites — providing an unprecedented opportunity to study a large NEO at close range; multiple space missions are planned [Wikipedia: 99942 Apophis]

### Scientific Value
- NEOs are among the most accessible small bodies for spacecraft missions, requiring less energy (delta-v) to reach than the Moon in some cases [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
- Sample-return missions have returned pristine Solar System material: OSIRIS-REx from Bennu (2023) and Hayabusa2 from Ryugu (2020) [NASA Science]
- NEOs are time capsules preserving material from the Solar System's first ~10 million years, informing models of planetary formation and the delivery of water and organics to early Earth [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]

## Historical Context

- **1898**: 433 Eros discovered by Witt and Charlois — first known near-Earth asteroid, an Amor-class object [Wikipedia: 433 Eros]
- **1932**: Apollo asteroid 1862 Apollo discovered, defining the Apollo group; Amor asteroid 1221 Amor named the group in the same year
- **1968**: Asteroid 1566 Icarus became the first asteroid observed by radar
- **1994**: Comet Shoemaker–Levy 9 impacted Jupiter, dramatically raising public and scientific awareness of impact hazards [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
- **1998**: US Congress mandated detection of 90% of NEOs ≥ 1 km within 10 years; NASA Spaceguard Survey formally launched [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
- **2000–2001**: NEAR Shoemaker spacecraft orbited and landed on 433 Eros — first spacecraft to orbit and land on a near-Earth asteroid [Wikipedia: 433 Eros]
- **2004**: Apophis discovery and Torino scale 4 alert — highest impact probability ever publicly announced [Wikipedia: 99942 Apophis]
- **2011**: 90% completeness milestone for NEOs ≥ 1 km declared achieved [Wikipedia: Near-Earth object]
- **2013**: Chelyabinsk event — largest NEO atmospheric impact since Tunguska (1908), catalysing international planetary defence coordination [Wikipedia: Chelyabinsk meteor]
- **2022**: DART successfully deflected Dimorphos — first demonstration of asteroid deflection technology [Wikipedia: DART spacecraft]
- **2029**: Apophis 2029 flyby will be the closest known approach by a large NEO in recorded history, visible to naked eye [Wikipedia: 99942 Apophis]

## Sub-types / Classifications

**By object type:**
- **Near-Earth Asteroids (NEAs)**: rocky/metallic bodies, ~99.67% of NEOs; subdivided by orbital family
- **Near-Earth Comets (NECs)**: icy bodies showing or formerly showing cometary activity; ~0.33% of NEOs

**NEA orbital families (ascending semi-major axis order):**
- **Atiras**: a < 1 AU, aphelion < 0.983 AU — orbits entirely inside Earth's; rarest (34 known)
- **Atens**: a < 1 AU, aphelion > 0.983 AU — mostly interior but cross Earth's orbit
- **Apollos**: a > 1 AU, perihelion < 1.017 AU — cross Earth's orbit; most numerous
- **Amors**: 1.017 AU < perihelion < 1.3 AU — orbit between Earth and Mars, currently non-crossing

**Hazard sub-classification:**
- **Potentially Hazardous Objects (PHOs/PHAs)**: MOID < 0.05 AU AND H ≤ 22 (diameter ≥ ~140 m)
- All other NEOs: tracked but not flagged as imminently hazardous
