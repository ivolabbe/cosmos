## Definition
Lepus (the Hare) is a southern constellation located immediately south of Orion, representing the hare being hunted by the celestial hunter and his dogs. Covering 290 square degrees, it is one of the 48 constellations catalogued by Ptolemy in the 2nd century CE and remains one of the 88 modern IAU-designated constellations. It contains the supergiant Arneb, the globular cluster Messier 79, the planetary nebula IC 418 (the Spirograph Nebula), and Hind's Crimson Star (R Leporis).

## Full Content

### Overview

Lepus (Latin for "hare"; IAU abbreviation Lep; genitive Leporis) is a constellation of the southern sky, ranked 51st in size among the 88 modern constellations. It occupies 290 square degrees between right ascensions 04h 55m and 06h 13m and declinations −10.8° and −27.3°, placing it squarely south of the brilliant constellation Orion [Wikipedia: Lepus constellation]. Its bordering constellations are Orion to the north, Eridanus to the west, Caelum and Columba to the south, Canis Major to the southeast, and Monoceros to the east [Wikipedia: Lepus constellation].

The constellation is best seen in January, when it transits the meridian at 21:00 local time. It is visible from latitudes between +63° and −90°, making it accessible to nearly all populated parts of Earth [Wikipedia: Lepus constellation].

### Mythology

Lepus is one of the oldest recognised constellations, listed by the Alexandrian astronomer Ptolemy (c. 100–170 CE) in the Almagest among his catalogue of 48 constellations. The 3rd-century-BCE Greek poet Aratus referred to it as Lagos (the Hare) in his astronomical poem Phaenomena [WebSearch: Lepus mythology Greek Aratus].

In the most widely told Greek account, Lepus represents a hare crouching at the feet of Orion the hunter, which Orion pursues together with his two hunting dogs, the constellations Canis Major and Canis Minor [Wikipedia: Lepus constellation]. The placement neatly reflects Orion's identity as a great hunter in Greek tradition. The mythographer Eratosthenes reported that Hermes (the messenger god) placed the hare in the sky because of its legendary swiftness [WebSearch: Lepus mythology Eratosthenes]. A separate Greek legend connects Lepus to the island of Leros: the islanders introduced hares that proliferated uncontrollably, and the image was set in the sky as a moral lesson on the value of moderation [WebSearch: Lepus mythology Leros hare].

The ancient Egyptians saw the same stars differently, interpreting them as a boat carrying the god Osiris — the Egyptian counterpart of the Greco-Roman Orion [WebSearch: Lepus Egyptian mythology].

Four stars — α, β, γ, and δ Leporis — form a prominent quadrilateral that Arab astronomers knew under several names. One tradition called them al-Jawzāʾ (a throne-like figure associated with Orion), and another called them al-Nihāl, "the camels quenching their thirst" [Wikipedia: Lepus constellation]. This Arabic tradition survives in the star names Arneb and Nihal.

### Notable Stars

#### Alpha Leporis — Arneb

Arneb (from Arabic al-arnab, "the hare") is the brightest star in Lepus, with an apparent magnitude of 2.59. It is classified as a yellow-white supergiant of spectral type F0 Ib and has served since 1943 as one of the anchor stars for stellar spectral classification [Wikipedia: Arneb].

Arneb lies approximately 2,200 light-years (680 pc) away and shines with a luminosity about 27,000 times that of the Sun [Wikipedia: Arneb]. Its surface temperature is approximately 7,290 K. With a mass of 12–13 solar masses and a radius of 74–105 solar radii, it is a massive, evolved star estimated to be only 13 million years old [Wikipedia: Arneb]. Arneb has already completed a red supergiant phase and is now contracting and heating in a blue loop during the late stages of its stellar evolution; it will ultimately end its life as a supernova [Wikipedia: Arneb].

#### Beta Leporis — Nihal

Nihal (from Arabic al-nihāl, "quenching their thirst") is the second-brightest star in Lepus, with an apparent magnitude of 2.84. It is a G-type bright giant (spectral type G5 II) located approximately 160 light-years (49 pc) from Earth [Wikipedia: Beta Leporis].

Nihal has a mass of 3.5 solar masses, a radius of 15.9 solar radii, a surface temperature of 5,434 K, and a luminosity of 160 L☉ [Wikipedia: Beta Leporis]. The system is a binary: a companion star at 2.58 arcsecond separation has an apparent magnitude of 7.34 and is itself a suspected variable (NSV 2008) [Wikipedia: Beta Leporis]. The IAU formally recognised the name Nihal in 2016.

#### Other Stars of Note

**Gamma Leporis** is a wide double star easily resolved in binoculars: the yellow primary has magnitude 3.6 and lies only 29 light-years away, while the orange secondary has magnitude 6.2. At this distance, the primary is among the nearest naked-eye stars in Lepus [Wikipedia: Lepus constellation].

**Epsilon Leporis** is an orange giant (K-type) at magnitude 3.2 and 227 light-years distant [Wikipedia: Lepus constellation].

**Delta Leporis** is a yellow giant at magnitude 3.8 and 112 light-years distant [Wikipedia: Lepus constellation].

**Kappa Leporis** is a double star with a blue-white primary at magnitude 4.4 and a secondary at magnitude 7.4, located 560 light-years away [Wikipedia: Lepus constellation].

### Deep-Sky Objects

#### Messier 79 — Globular Cluster

Messier 79 (M79; NGC 1904; GCl 10) is the most prominent deep-sky object in Lepus and one of the few Milky Way globular clusters visible in the northern winter sky. It was discovered by Pierre Méchain in 1780 and catalogued by Charles Messier in the same year [Wikipedia: Messier 79].

M79 has an apparent magnitude of 7.7 and subtends approximately 8.7 arcminutes on the sky, corresponding to a physical radius of about 53 light-years [Wikipedia: Messier 79]. It lies approximately 42,000 light-years from Earth and about 60,000 light-years from the Galactic Centre (12.9 kpc) — notably far from the Galactic Centre compared with most globular clusters [Wikipedia: Messier 79]. It is classified as Shapley-Sawyer concentration class V, indicating intermediate central concentration.

The cluster is estimated to be approximately 11.7 billion years old, with a metallicity of [Fe/H] = −1.55 dex [Wikipedia: Messier 79]. Research suggests M79 may have an extragalactic origin, possibly associated with the Canis Major Dwarf Galaxy rather than formed within the Milky Way [Wikipedia: Messier 79]. The cluster is also being gradually disrupted by Galactic tidal forces, trailing a long tidal tail [Wikipedia: Messier 79].

#### IC 418 — The Spirograph Nebula

IC 418 is a compact planetary nebula in Lepus, popularly known as the Spirograph Nebula because its intricate filamentary structure resembles the geometric patterns produced by a Spirograph drawing toy [Wikipedia: IC 418]. It has an apparent magnitude of 9.6 and lies approximately 3,600 light-years (1,100 ± 300 pc) from Earth [Wikipedia: IC 418].

The nebula spans about 0.3 light-years across and formed a few thousand years ago when an evolved red giant expelled its outer layers, which are now ionised and made luminous by the hot central star [Wikipedia: IC 418]. The central star HD 35914 is an extremely hot O-type star (spectral type O7fp) whose ultraviolet radiation ionises the surrounding gas to produce the nebula's glow [Wikipedia: IC 418].

### Hind's Crimson Star — R Leporis

R Leporis is one of the most striking variable stars in the sky, nicknamed "Hind's Crimson Star" for its deep blood-red colour. It was discovered by the British astronomer John Russell Hind in 1845, who described it as "like a drop of blood on a black field" [Wikipedia: R Leporis].

R Leporis is a Mira-type long-period variable and a carbon star, classified as spectral type C7,6e(N6e). Its apparent magnitude varies from a faint 11.7 at minimum to as bright as 5.5 at maximum, with a primary period of approximately 427 days (about 14 months) [Wikipedia: R Leporis]. There is evidence of a secondary longer-period variation of about 40 years [Wikipedia: R Leporis]. The star lies approximately 1,490 light-years (460 pc) from Earth, near the constellation's border with Eridanus [Wikipedia: R Leporis].

The intense red colour arises from carbon molecules in the star's outer atmosphere, which absorb blue and green wavelengths preferentially, leaving only red light to escape [Wikipedia: R Leporis]. The colour is most pronounced when R Leporis is near minimum brightness and is significantly less striking near maximum.

### Best Visibility

Lepus is a winter constellation for northern-hemisphere observers. It reaches its highest point in the sky at 21:00 local time in January and remains well placed throughout December, January, and February. At southern latitudes it is a summer constellation and reaches higher altitudes than at northern sites, making Australia, South Africa, and South America particularly good locations to observe it. It is circumpolar from latitudes south of about −63°, and cannot be seen from latitudes north of about +63°. The constellation's proximity to brilliant Orion makes it straightforward to locate: simply drop south from Orion's feet to find the quadrilateral formed by α, β, γ, and δ Leporis.

For visual observers, M79 can be resolved in a small telescope (75–100 mm aperture), appearing as a fuzzy star at low magnification and resolving to individual stars at the cluster periphery under higher magnification. IC 418 requires at least a 150 mm telescope to show any structure beyond a stellar point of light. R Leporis is within binocular reach for several months around maximum and is an excellent target during the red phase near minimum.
