## Definition

Starlight is the electromagnetic radiation emitted by stars, produced through nuclear fusion in their interiors and radiated from their surfaces across a broad range from ultraviolet through infrared, with the visible portion spanning roughly 380–700 nm. [NED/IPAC Glossary; Wikipedia: Starlight]

## Full Content

### What starlight is
- Starlight is electromagnetic radiation produced when nuclear fusion in a star's core generates energy that diffuses outward and is radiated from the photosphere. [NED/IPAC Glossary; Wikipedia: Starlight]
- It spans the electromagnetic spectrum — ultraviolet, visible, and infrared — though the peak wavelength depends on surface temperature following Wien's displacement law: λ_peak = b/T. [Wikipedia: Black-body radiation]
- Stars approximate blackbodies: their spectra follow a Planck distribution. The Sun at ~5,778 K peaks near 500 nm (green-yellow). Hotter O-type stars (≥33,000 K) peak in the ultraviolet; cool M-type stars (2,300–3,900 K) peak in the infrared. [Wikipedia: Stellar classification; HyperPhysics/GSU stellar spectra]
- Total power radiated scales as the fourth power of temperature (Stefan-Boltzmann law: L ∝ R²T⁴). A star twice as hot but the same size radiates 16× more energy. [Wikipedia: Black-body radiation]

### The spectrum of starlight — three types
- **Continuous spectrum**: a smooth, rainbow-like distribution of all wavelengths, produced by a hot dense gas or stellar photosphere. [NASA/GSFC: Spectra and What They Can Tell Us]
- **Emission-line spectrum**: bright lines at specific wavelengths produced by hot low-density gas (e.g. in stellar chromospheres and nebulae). [NASA/GSFC: Spectra and What They Can Tell Us]
- **Absorption-line spectrum**: a continuous spectrum crossed by dark Fraunhofer lines, where cooler overlying atmospheric gases absorb specific wavelengths. Most stellar spectra are of this type. [Wikipedia: Astronomical spectroscopy; NASA/GSFC]
- Each element produces a unique set of lines — a fingerprint. Comparing dark stellar lines against laboratory emission spectra identifies atomic species in the stellar atmosphere. [Wikipedia: Astronomical spectroscopy]

### What starlight reveals
- **Chemical composition**: absorption-line patterns identify elements; hydrogen and helium dominate (>98% of stellar mass). [Payne-Gaposchkin 1925 doctoral thesis]
- **Temperature**: spectral class (OBAFGKM) encodes surface temperature; specific lines appear only within temperature windows where the relevant atomic energy levels are populated. [HyperPhysics/GSU; Wikipedia: Stellar classification]
- **Radial velocity**: the Doppler shift of spectral lines measures motion toward or away from Earth. [Wikipedia: Astronomical spectroscopy]
- **Luminosity and radius**: combining temperature (from spectral type) with distance (parallax) yields luminosity; luminosity + temperature yields radius via the Stefan-Boltzmann relation. [Wikipedia: Astronomical spectroscopy]
- **Magnetic fields**: Zeeman splitting of spectral lines detects stellar magnetic field strength. [Wikipedia: Astronomical spectroscopy]
- **Stellar winds**: P Cygni line profiles (emission + blueshifted absorption) trace outflowing material. [Wikipedia: Astronomical spectroscopy]

### Interstellar effects on starlight
- As starlight travels through the interstellar medium (ISM), dust grains absorb and scatter it — a process called interstellar extinction. [Wikipedia: Extinction (astronomy)]
- Extinction is wavelength-dependent (~1/λ): shorter (blue) wavelengths are scattered more than longer (red) wavelengths, making distant stars appear redder than they are — interstellar reddening. [Wikipedia: Extinction (astronomy); astronomynotes.com]
- Dust grains responsible are ~0.1–1 µm — comparable to visible-light wavelengths — and consist of silicate and carbonaceous cores, often mantled with ices. [Wikipedia: Extinction (astronomy)]
- Polarisation of starlight (~1.5% linear at 1 kpc) arises because elongated dust grains aligned by the Galactic magnetic field preferentially absorb one polarisation direction. [Wikipedia: Starlight]

### Brightness and the naked eye
- Stellar brightness is quantified on the magnitude scale: each 5-magnitude difference equals a factor of 100 in flux. The faintest stars visible to the unaided eye are around magnitude 6–7 (~10⁻⁸ lux). [Wikipedia: Apparent magnitude]
- A 0th-magnitude star delivers ~2.7 × 10⁻⁶ lux at Earth — roughly at the threshold of human photopic (colour) vision. [Wikipedia: Orders of magnitude (illuminance)]
- The combined light of all stars in the night sky (moonless, unpolluted) amounts to roughly 0.002 lux — enough to cast faint shadows but far below moonlight (~0.05–0.3 lux). [Wikipedia: Starlight; NPS Night Skies]

### Average colour of the universe's starlight
- In 2002–2003, Karl Glazebrook and Ivan Baldry measured the combined spectrum of over 200,000 galaxies in the 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey. After correcting a software error, they found the average colour of the universe's starlight is a pale warm beige — dubbed "Cosmic Latte" (hex #FFF8E7). [Wikipedia: Cosmic latte]
- This reflects the mix of stellar populations: hot blue-white O/B stars are luminous but rare; abundant cooler G–M stars tilt the aggregate toward yellow-white. [Wikipedia: Cosmic latte]

## Historical Context

- **Joseph von Fraunhofer (1814)**: using high-purity glass prisms, identified 574 dark lines in the solar spectrum — now called Fraunhofer lines — the first detailed map of starlight's spectral structure. [Wikipedia: Astronomical spectroscopy]
- **Kirchhoff & Bunsen (1850s)**: explained Fraunhofer lines as absorption by cooler gases in the stellar atmosphere, and matched lines to laboratory spectra of known elements — founding stellar chemistry. [Wikipedia: Astronomical spectroscopy]
- **Angelo Secchi (1860s–1870s)**: first classification of stars by their spectral appearance into four broad types. [Wikipedia: Stellar classification]
- **Annie Jump Cannon (1897–1912)**: systematised the Harvard spectral classification (OBAFGKM), classifying hundreds of thousands of stars into temperature-ordered types. [Wikipedia: Stellar classification]
- **Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin (1925)**: in her doctoral thesis, combined Saha's ionization theory with Harvard plate data to show that variations in stellar spectra are primarily due to differing temperatures, not differing elemental abundances — and that stars are composed overwhelmingly of hydrogen and helium. This was the first determination of the true chemical composition of stars, initially doubted by Henry Norris Russell but later confirmed. [Wikipedia: Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin; AMNH profile]
- **Glazebrook & Baldry (2002–2003)**: determined the average colour of the universe's combined starlight (Cosmic Latte) from 200,000+ galaxies. [Wikipedia: Cosmic latte]

## Sub-types / Classifications

Starlight is classified by the spectral type of the source star (the Harvard/Morgan-Keenan system):

| Class | Temperature (K) | Colour | Dominant spectral features |
|-------|-----------------|--------|---------------------------|
| O | ≥33,000 | Blue | Ionized He, N; weak H (ionized away) |
| B | 10,000–33,000 | Blue-white | Neutral He at maximum; H visible |
| A | 7,300–10,000 | White | H lines at maximum (A0); ionized metals |
| F | 6,000–7,300 | Yellow-white | Ca II strengthening; neutral metals |
| G | 5,300–6,000 | Yellow | Ca II H & K strong; weak H (like the Sun) |
| K | 3,900–5,300 | Orange | TiO bands appearing; very weak H |
| M | 2,300–3,900 | Red-orange | TiO dominant; neutral metals; no H |

[Wikipedia: Stellar classification; HyperPhysics/GSU]

Note: additional spectral classes L, T, Y exist for brown dwarfs (not true stars by most definitions) below M.
