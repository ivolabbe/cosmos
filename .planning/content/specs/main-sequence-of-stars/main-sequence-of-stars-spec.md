# Main Sequence of Stars — Article Spec

## Category
Stars

## Definition (1–2 sentences)
The main sequence is the broad diagonal band on a Hertzsprung-Russell diagram where the vast majority of stars spend most of their lives, generating energy by fusing hydrogen into helium in their cores. A star's position along this band is determined almost entirely by its mass, which controls its luminosity, surface temperature, and lifespan.

## Full Content (ALL facts needed for 400–800 word article support)

### The H-R Diagram Band
On a Hertzsprung-Russell (H-R) diagram — a plot of stellar luminosity against surface temperature (or spectral type) — the main sequence appears as a prominent diagonal band running from the upper-left (hot, luminous stars) to the lower-right (cool, dim stars). When Ejnar Hertzsprung (1911) and Henry Norris Russell (1913) independently constructed the first such diagrams, this diagonal concentration of stars immediately stood out as the dominant stellar population. Most stars observed in the solar neighbourhood lie on the main sequence; it is the longest-lived and most populated stage of stellar evolution. [Sources: [Main sequence – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_sequence); [Hertzsprung–Russell diagram – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertzsprung%E2%80%93Russell_diagram)]

### What Makes a Star "Main Sequence"
A star is on the main sequence if it is actively fusing hydrogen into helium in its core and if that fusion energy supports the star against gravitational collapse — a state called hydrostatic equilibrium. This phase begins when gravitational contraction has raised the core temperature high enough to ignite nuclear fusion, and it ends when the core hydrogen is exhausted. Main sequence is the longest phase of a star's life, lasting from millions of years for the most massive stars to trillions of years for the least massive. [Source: [NASA Stars overview](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/stars/)]

### Hydrogen Fusion: Two Mechanisms
Two fusion pathways operate on the main sequence, depending on stellar mass and core temperature:
- **Proton-proton (PP) chain**: dominates in stars of ≤ 1.5 M☉ (including the Sun); directly fuses four protons into one helium-4 nucleus.
- **CNO cycle (carbon-nitrogen-oxygen)**: dominates in stars of > 1.5 M☉; uses carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen as catalysts. At approximately 1.5 M☉, the two processes contribute equally.
[Source: [Main sequence – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_sequence)]

### Zero-Age Main Sequence (ZAMS)
The Zero-Age Main Sequence is the theoretical curve on the H-R diagram marking where stars first arrive when core hydrogen fusion begins and gravitational contraction effectively ceases. As stars age and evolve, their luminosity and temperature slowly increase, so they drift slightly away from the ZAMS over time. The ZAMS can be calculated using computer models of stellar properties at the onset of hydrogen burning. The corresponding endpoint — when core hydrogen is exhausted — is the Terminal Age Main Sequence (TAMS). [Source: [Zero-age main sequence – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-age_main_sequence)]

### Mass-Luminosity Relation
For main-sequence stars in the range 0.1–50 M☉, luminosity scales with mass roughly as:

**L ∝ M^3.5**

This means a star twice as massive as the Sun is approximately 2^3.5 ≈ 11 times more luminous. The steep exponent explains why massive stars are so extraordinarily bright despite having only modestly larger fuel reserves. [Source: [Main sequence – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_sequence)]

### Stellar Lifetime vs Mass
Because fuel supply scales with mass (M) while luminosity (the burn rate) scales as M^3.5, the main-sequence lifetime shrinks rapidly with increasing mass:

**τ_MS ≈ 10^10 yr × (M/M☉)^–2.5**

Key examples:
| Spectral type | Mass (M☉) | τ_MS (approx) |
|---------------|------------|----------------|
| O5 | ~40 | ~1 million yr |
| B0 | ~18 | ~11 million yr |
| A0 | ~3.2 | ~370 million yr |
| G2 (Sun) | ~1.0 | ~10 billion yr |
| K5 | ~0.7 | ~37 billion yr |
| M5 | ~0.2 | > 1 trillion yr |

[Source: [Main sequence – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_sequence)]

[Sources: [Stellar classification – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_classification); [UNLV spectral type table](https://www.physics.unlv.edu/~jeffery/astro/star/spectral_type2.html)]

### The Vogt-Russell Theorem
First proposed by Heinrich Vogt (1926, published 1931 in *Astronomische Nachrichten*) and independently by Henry Norris Russell (1931, *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society*), the Vogt-Russell theorem states that the internal structure of a star in hydrostatic and thermal equilibrium is uniquely determined by its total mass and chemical composition. For main-sequence stars this is an excellent approximation: given mass and composition, all observable properties — radius, luminosity, surface temperature — are fixed. The theorem breaks down near extreme conditions (rapid rotation, strong magnetic fields, electron degeneracy) but remains the conceptual foundation of stellar structure theory. [Source: [Vogt–Russell theorem – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vogt%E2%80%93Russell_theorem)]

### Stellar Interior Structure
Energy transport inside main-sequence stars varies systematically with mass:
- **Very low-mass stars (< 0.4 M☉)**: fully convective throughout.
- **Solar-mass stars (~0.3–1.5 M☉)**: radiative core surrounded by a convective envelope; the Sun's convective zone begins at ~71% of its radius.
- **High-mass stars (> 1.5–2 M☉)**: convective core (driven by the CNO cycle's strong temperature sensitivity) with a radiative outer region.
[Source: [Main sequence – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_sequence)]

### The Main Sequence Turn-Off Point in Star Clusters
In a star cluster all members formed at roughly the same time. More massive stars exhaust their hydrogen first and leave the main sequence (moving toward the red giant region), while lower-mass stars remain. The point on the cluster's H-R diagram where stars are currently departing the main sequence is the **main sequence turn-off point**. Because stellar lifetime scales precisely with mass (via the mass-luminosity relation), the luminosity of the turn-off point directly yields the cluster age — a technique called isochrone fitting. Globular clusters (e.g., M67, NGC 188) show turn-off points corresponding to ages of 10–13 billion years; young open clusters show high-luminosity turn-offs corresponding to ages of only tens of millions of years. [Sources: [Main sequence turnoff – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_sequence_turnoff); [Penn State ASTRO 801](https://courses.ems.psu.edu/astro801/content/l7_p6.html)]

### The Sun's Position
The Sun is a G2V main-sequence star with effective temperature ~5,778 K, mass 1 M☉, luminosity 1 L☉, and radius 1 R☉. It has been on the main sequence for approximately 4.6 billion years and has roughly 5 billion years of hydrogen-burning lifetime remaining — placing it slightly past the midpoint of its main-sequence life. Over that time the Sun has slowly grown ~30% more luminous as helium accumulates in the core and the mean molecular weight increases. [Sources: [Main sequence – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_sequence); [NASA Stars overview](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/stars/)]

## Historical Context

- **1911**: Ejnar Hertzsprung plots absolute magnitude vs colour for nearby stars; main sequence band visible.
- **1913**: Henry Norris Russell independently plots absolute magnitude vs spectral type; publishes the H-R diagram.
- **1926–1931**: Heinrich Vogt formulates and publishes what becomes the Vogt-Russell theorem (*Astronomische Nachrichten*, 1931).
- **1931**: Henry Norris Russell independently publishes the same theorem in *MNRAS*.
- **1950s–1960s**: Stellar evolution models (Henyey, Iben, Schwarzschild) trace evolutionary tracks from ZAMS through turn-off, confirming the mass-luminosity-lifetime framework.
- **Present**: Isochrone fitting of cluster turn-off points is a primary tool for measuring the ages of stellar populations, including the oldest globular clusters at ~13 Gyr.

## Sub-types / Classifications

1. **O-type main sequence (luminosity class V)**: Hottest, most massive, most luminous; rare; O stars drive H II regions.
2. **B-type main sequence**: Hot blue-white stars; many fast rotators; includes Be stars.
3. **A-type main sequence**: White stars with strong hydrogen Balmer lines (Sirius, Vega).
4. **F-type main sequence**: Yellow-white; transitional between radiative and convective interiors.
5. **G-type main sequence**: Yellow dwarfs; Sun is the prototype (G2V).
6. **K-type main sequence**: Orange dwarfs; long-lived, potentially favourable for habitable planets.
7. **M-type main sequence (red dwarfs)**: Most numerous stars in the galaxy; fully convective; lifetimes exceed the current age of the universe.
8. **Lower main sequence (< 0.4 M☉)**: Fully convective; no chemical stratification between core and envelope.
9. **Upper main sequence (> 1.5 M☉)**: CNO-cycle dominated; convective core.
