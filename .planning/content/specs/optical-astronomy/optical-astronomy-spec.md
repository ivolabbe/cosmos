## Definition
Optical astronomy is the branch of astronomy that observes celestial objects in or near the visible-light portion of the electromagnetic spectrum, roughly 380–750 nanometres, using telescopes and associated instruments such as spectrographs, photometers, and cameras. It is distinguished from radio, infrared, ultraviolet, X-ray, and gamma-ray astronomy by the wavelength window it exploits — which also happens to be the window most accessible through Earth's atmosphere without special equipment.

## Full Content

- Optical astronomy covers observations in visible light (~380–750 nm), the spectral window human eyes evolved to detect and the one in which Earth's atmosphere is largely transparent [Wikipedia: Visible-light astronomy].
- For most of human history the naked eye was the sole detector; telescopes were introduced in the early 17th century, transforming the field entirely [Wikipedia: Telescope].
- Three telescope designs dominate: refractors (lenses), reflectors (mirrors), and catadioptric systems (mirrors + corrector lenses); reflecting telescopes are preferred for research because lenses impose a practical aperture ceiling of ~1 m and suffer from chromatic aberration [Wikipedia: Telescope; Wikipedia: Visible-light astronomy].
- The atmosphere imposes "seeing" — image blurring caused by turbulent air cells of varying refractive index. Typical seeing at a good mountaintop site is ~1 arcsecond; exceptional sites achieve < 0.4 arcseconds [Wikipedia: Atmospheric seeing].
- Strategies to overcome seeing: building at high-altitude sites (Mauna Kea at 4,205 m, Cerro Paranal at 2,635 m, Canary Islands), adaptive optics (deformable mirrors driven by wavefront sensors; best current systems reach ~90% Strehl ratio at 2.2 µm), lucky imaging, speckle imaging, and space-based telescopes above the atmosphere entirely [Wikipedia: Atmospheric seeing].
- Light pollution from artificial sources competes with faint celestial signals; remote mountain sites are chosen partly for darkness [Wikipedia: Visible-light astronomy].
- Detectors evolved from the naked eye → photographic plates (capturing ~2% of incident photons) → charge-coupled devices (CCDs, ~70% quantum efficiency), first applied astronomically in the 1970s–80s [Wikipedia: Charge-coupled device].
- CCDs enabled digital survey astronomy; the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) used 30 CCDs in drift-scanning mode to map > 25% of the sky [Wikipedia: Charge-coupled device].
- Spectroscopy is a core optical technique: splitting starlight with prisms or diffraction gratings reveals absorption and emission line patterns that act as "fingerprints" to identify chemical composition, surface temperature (Wien's displacement law: λ_max · T = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K), radial velocity via Doppler shifts, and cosmological redshift [Wikipedia: Astronomical spectroscopy].
- Over 20,000 solar absorption lines have been catalogued, with ~75% linked to known elements [Wikipedia: Astronomical spectroscopy].
- Helium was identified in the Sun's spectrum in 1868, predating its discovery on Earth — an iconic demonstration of optical spectroscopy's power [Wikipedia: Astronomical spectroscopy].
- Photometry (measuring brightness in standardised filter bands such as Johnson UBV and SDSS ugriz) gives stellar colours, distances via standard candles, and light curves of variable and transient sources [Wikipedia: Visible-light astronomy].
- Stellar colours reveal temperatures: our Sun (~5,500°C) appears yellow; cool stars like Betelgeuse (~3,000°C) appear red; hot stars like Rigel (~12,000°C) appear blue [NASA Science: Visible light].
- The Hubble Space Telescope (launched 1990, 2.4-m primary mirror) observes from near-UV through visible to near-IR, producing diffraction-limited images about 25 times more sensitive than the dimmest objects visible from the ground; it measured the Hubble constant and produced deep-field images of the earliest galaxies [Wikipedia: Visible-light astronomy; NASA images].
- Modern ground-based optical facilities are dominated by segmented-mirror reflectors: the Gran Telescopio Canarias (10.4 m, Canary Islands), twin Keck telescopes (10 m each, Mauna Kea), ESO's four VLT Unit Telescopes (8.2 m each, Cerro Paranal), and the Large Binocular Telescope (effective 11.9 m, two 8.4-m mirrors, Arizona) [Wikipedia: Largest reflecting telescopes].
- The next generation — ESO's Extremely Large Telescope (39.5 m, Chile, first light ~2029), Giant Magellan Telescope (24.5 m, Chile, 2030s), and Thirty Meter Telescope (30 m, Mauna Kea, status uncertain) — will gather far more light, enabling direct imaging of exoplanet atmospheres and study of the first galaxies [Wikipedia: ELT].
- Optical interferometry (e.g. CHARA array, VLTI optical mode) combines beams from separated telescopes to achieve angular resolutions far beyond a single mirror, resolving stellar surfaces [Wikipedia: Telescope].
- Stars appear to twinkle (scintillate) to the naked eye due to atmospheric seeing; planets show a steadier disc because their finite angular size averages out rapid fluctuations — a useful rule of thumb for amateur observers [Wikipedia: Atmospheric seeing].

## Historical Context

- Naked-eye astronomy spans millennia: ancient civilisations named and tracked constellations and planets visible to the unaided eye, using the sky for navigation and timekeeping.
- 1608: Hans Lipperhey (Dutch spectacle maker) filed the first patent for an optical refracting telescope; Galileo independently built and improved the design in 1609, achieving 30× magnification [Wikipedia: Telescope].
- 1609–1610: Galileo turned his telescope skyward, discovering Jupiter's four largest moons, lunar craters, the phases of Venus, and that the Milky Way resolved into individual stars; he published *Sidereus Nuncius* (Starry Messenger) in March 1610 [Wikipedia: Visible-light astronomy].
- 1668: Isaac Newton built the first practical reflecting telescope, sidestepping chromatic aberration intrinsic to lenses [Wikipedia: Telescope].
- 1733: Chester Moor Hall invented the achromatic lens, reducing colour aberration in refractors and enabling shorter, more practical designs [Wikipedia: Telescope].
- 1814–1815: Joseph von Fraunhofer systematically catalogued 574 dark absorption lines in the solar spectrum (now called Fraunhofer lines), laying foundations for optical stellar spectroscopy [Wikipedia: Astronomical spectroscopy].
- 1840: John William Draper made the first successful photograph of the Moon (20-minute daguerreotype exposure with a 5-inch reflector) [Wikipedia: Astrophotography].
- 1850: William Bond and John Whipple photographed Vega using Harvard College Observatory's 15-inch refractor — the first photograph of a star other than the Sun [Wikipedia: Astrophotography].
- 1857: Silver-coated glass mirrors introduced; aluminised coatings followed in 1932, greatly improving reflectivity and durability [Wikipedia: Telescope].
- 1868: Helium identified in the Sun's spectrum by Norman Lockyer and Jules Janssen via spectroscopy — 27 years before its discovery on Earth [Wikipedia: Astronomical spectroscopy].
- 1880: Henry Draper photographed the Orion Nebula — the first nebula captured on a photographic plate [Wikipedia: Astrophotography].
- 1883: Amateur astronomer Andrew Ainslie Common demonstrated that long exposures revealed stars invisible to the human eye, using 60-minute exposures with a homemade 36-inch reflector [Wikipedia: Astrophotography].
- Early 20th century: Large reflectors displaced refractors for professional research. Edwin Hubble used the 100-inch Hooker Telescope at Mount Wilson (1917) to resolve Cepheid variables in M31, establishing that "spiral nebulae" are external galaxies; his 1929 discovery of the velocity–distance relation (via spectroscopic redshifts) demonstrated the expanding universe.
- 1970s–1980s: CCDs replaced photographic plates in professional optical astronomy, increasing photon-capture efficiency from ~2% to ~70% and enabling the digital survey era [Wikipedia: Charge-coupled device].
- 1990: Hubble Space Telescope launched, providing the first large-aperture telescope free of atmospheric seeing [Wikipedia: Visible-light astronomy].
- 1993–present: Era of 8–10-metre segmented-mirror ground-based telescopes begins with Keck 1 first light [Wikipedia: Largest reflecting telescopes].
- 2029 (expected): ESO's Extremely Large Telescope (39.5 m primary) will be the largest optical telescope ever built [Wikipedia: ELT].

## Sub-types / Classifications

By technique:

- **Imaging / direct photography**: capturing 2D images of objects on CCD arrays; modern sky surveys (SDSS, Rubin/LSST) image vast areas systematically and repeatedly.
- **Photometry**: precise measurement of brightness in standardised filter bands (e.g. Johnson UBV, SDSS ugriz); used for variable stars, planetary transits, supernova light curves, and standard candles.
- **Spectroscopy**: dispersing light with gratings or prisms to measure spectral lines; yields composition, temperature, velocity, and redshift. Subdivides into long-slit, multi-object (fibre-fed, e.g. 2dF, DESI), and integral-field spectroscopy.
- **Astrometry**: precise measurement of position and proper motion; Hipparcos (1989–1993) and Gaia (2013–present) are pre-eminent astrometric optical missions.
- **Polarimetry**: measuring the polarisation state of light; probes magnetic fields (via aligned interstellar dust) and scattering geometry.
- **Optical interferometry**: combining beams from separated apertures (e.g. CHARA, VLTI optical arm) to achieve milliarcsecond angular resolution, resolving stellar discs.

By telescope type:

- **Refractors** (lens-based): historically dominant, now mainly used by amateurs; limited to ~1 m aperture.
- **Reflectors** (Newtonian, Cassegrain, Ritchey-Chrétien, etc.): all major research telescopes; no fundamental aperture limit.
- **Catadioptric** (Schmidt-Cassegrain, Maksutov): hybrid lens-mirror designs; popular for compact amateur and wide-field survey instruments.
- **Space optical telescopes**: outside the atmosphere; access near-UV to near-IR beyond ground-based windows; Hubble Space Telescope is the prime example; Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is planned.
