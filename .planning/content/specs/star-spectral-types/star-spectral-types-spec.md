## Definition
A stellar spectral type is a classification of a star based on the absorption and emission features in its spectrum, which reflect the star's surface temperature; the standard Harvard sequence O, B, A, F, G, K, M orders stars from hottest to coolest, and each class is further subdivided by a number (0–9) and a luminosity class (I–V) in the Morgan-Keenan (MK) system.

## Full Content

- **What spectral type means**: A stellar spectral type encodes a star's surface temperature through the pattern of dark absorption lines in its spectrum. At different temperatures, atoms are ionised to different degrees, causing different elements to absorb light — so the spectrum acts as a thermometer. [Wikipedia; Ohio State Ast162]
- **Physical basis — Saha ionisation**: The connection between spectrum and temperature was placed on a quantitative footing by Indian physicist Meghnad Saha in the 1920s. His ionisation equation shows that the fraction of atoms in a given ionisation state depends exponentially on temperature, explaining why each spectral class has its characteristic line signature. [Wikipedia; Britannica]
- **The OBAFGKM sequence**: Stars are divided into seven main classes — O, B, A, F, G, K, M — running hottest to coolest. A common mnemonic is "Oh, Be A Fine Girl/Guy, Kiss Me!" Each class is subdivided 0–9, where 0 is hottest within that class (e.g. B0 is hotter than B9). [Wikipedia; NAAP]
- **Class O** (≥33,000 K, blue): Dominated by ionised helium (He II), plus highly ionised Si IV, O III, N III, C III. Hydrogen lines are weak because most hydrogen is ionised. Rarest main-sequence class: ~0.00003% of nearby stars. Examples: S Monocerotis (O7V), Theta¹ Orionis C. [Wikipedia]
- **Class B** (10,000–33,000 K, blue-white): Neutral helium lines are strongest at B2; hydrogen lines moderate and strengthening toward A. He II absent. About 0.12% of local main-sequence stars. Examples: Rigel (B8Ia), Spica (B1V). [Wikipedia; CfA spectral atlas]
- **Class A** (7,300–10,000 K, white): Hydrogen Balmer lines reach maximum strength at A0. Ionised calcium (Ca II H and K) starts to appear; ionised metals (Fe II, Mg II, Si II) peak around A5. About 0.61% of local main-sequence stars. Examples: Vega (A0Va — reference standard), Sirius A (A1V). [Wikipedia; CfA spectral atlas]
- **Class F** (6,000–7,300 K, yellow-white): Hydrogen lines weaken; Ca II H and K lines strengthen; neutral metals (Fe I, Cr I, Ca I) begin to dominate. About 3% of local main-sequence stars. Examples: Procyon A (F5IV-V), Canopus (F0II). [Wikipedia; CfA spectral atlas]
- **Class G** (5,300–6,000 K, yellow): Ca II H and K lines very prominent; neutral metals (Fe, Ca, Na, Mg, Ti) strong; hydrogen lines only weak. About 7.6% of local main-sequence stars. The Sun is G2V. Examples: Sun (G2V), Capella Aa (G8III). [Wikipedia; Britannica]
- **Class K** (3,900–5,300 K, orange): Hydrogen lines nearly absent; neutral metals dominant; late-K stars begin to show titanium oxide (TiO) bands; cyanogen (CN) and hydroxyl (OH) fragments persist. About 12% of local main-sequence stars. Examples: Arcturus (K0III), Alpha Centauri B (K1V). [Wikipedia; Britannica]
- **Class M** (2,300–3,900 K, red): TiO and other metal-oxide molecular bands are the defining spectral features; hydrogen lines typically absent. By far the most common class: ~76% of nearby main-sequence stars are M dwarfs. Examples: Betelgeuse (M1-2Ia), Proxima Centauri (M5.5Ve). [Wikipedia]
- **Extended cool classes (L, T, Y)**: Introduced in the late 1990s–2000s for brown dwarfs cooler than M. Class L (1,300–2,500 K) shows metal-hydride bands (CrH, FeH) and alkali metals; class T (550–1,300 K) is defined by methane (CH₄) absorption — similar to Jupiter's spectrum; class Y (<550 K) shows ammonia and water. These objects emit mostly infrared light. [Wikipedia; Ohio State Ast162]
- **Luminosity classes (MK system)**: Giant and supergiant stars at the same temperature as a dwarf have lower surface gravity, so their spectral lines are narrower (less pressure broadening). The 1943 Morgan-Keenan (Yerkes) system adds Roman-numeral luminosity classes: Ia/Ib (supergiants), II (bright giants), III (giants), IV (subgiants), V (main-sequence dwarfs), VI/sd (subdwarfs), VII/D (white dwarfs). This gives a full classification like "G2 V" (Sun) or "B8 Ia" (Rigel). [Wikipedia; NED/IPAC]
- **Peculiar and special types**: Be stars (B with Balmer emission from a circumstellar disc); Ap/Bp (magnetically enhanced rare-earth abundances); Am (metallic-line A stars with Ca underabundance); Wolf-Rayet stars (WR — strong broad emission lines, hydrogen-stripped massive stars); Carbon stars (C0–C6 — cool red giants with carbon/oxygen > 1); S-type (zirconium and s-process elements, intermediate between M and C). [Wikipedia]
- **Significance**: Spectral type is the single most important label astronomers assign a star — combined with luminosity class, it constrains temperature, radius, mass, evolutionary state, and enables spectroscopic parallax distance estimation. [Wikipedia; Britannica]

## Historical Context

- **Fraunhofer lines (1814)**: Josef von Fraunhofer mapped hundreds of dark absorption lines in the solar spectrum, including the Ca II H and K lines at 396.8/393.4 nm and Na D at 589 nm, providing the observational foundation for stellar spectroscopy. [NED/IPAC]
- **Angelo Secchi (1860s)**: The Jesuit astronomer at the Pontifical Gregorian University in Rome was the first to attempt systematic stellar classification. Examining over 4,000 stars, he defined four (later five) spectral types I–IV based on dominant features (hydrogen lines, metallic lines, TiO bands, carbon bands). [NED/IPAC; WebSearch]
- **Henry Draper (1872)**: First to photograph a stellar spectrum. His widow funded the Henry Draper Memorial project at Harvard, which ultimately produced the definitive catalogue of stellar spectra. [WebSearch / astronomy.com]
- **Harvard Computers — Fleming, Maury, Cannon (1880s–1920s)**: Pickering directed the Henry Draper Catalogue. Williamina Fleming devised the initial A–Q alphabetic system, classifying over 10,000 stars. Antonia Maury introduced a 22-group scheme with line-width notation. Annie Jump Cannon simplified the system to the temperature-ordered OBAFGKM sequence; she classified ~325,000 spectra, published in nine volumes (1918–1924). [NED/IPAC; Wikipedia; WebSearch]
- **Cecilia Payne (1925)**: In her Harvard doctoral thesis, Payne demonstrated that stellar spectra form a temperature sequence — the dominant stellar constituent is hydrogen and helium, and varying line strengths reflect ionisation state, not composition changes. This was the theoretical breakthrough behind the sequence. [Wikipedia]
- **Meghnad Saha (1920s)**: Derived the ionisation equation relating ionisation degree to temperature and pressure, providing the physical theory that Payne applied to stars. [Wikipedia; Britannica]
- **Morgan-Keenan system (1943)**: William W. Morgan (Yerkes Observatory) and Philip C. Keenan published the two-dimensional MK system in the *Atlas of Stellar Spectra*, adding luminosity classes using standard comparison stars. The standards-based approach keeps the system empirically stable. [NED/IPAC]
- **Brown dwarf extensions (1990s–2010s)**: Classes L and T were proposed in 1997–1999 following cool-object discoveries by 2MASS and SDSS surveys. Class Y was added after the WISE satellite revealed ultracool brown dwarfs in 2011. [Wikipedia]

**Peculiar spectral sub-types:**
- **Be** — B-type with Balmer emission lines from equatorial decretion disc
- **Ap / Bp** — chemically peculiar with magnetically enhanced rare-earth abundances
- **Am** — A-type with enhanced metals and depleted calcium (atomic diffusion)
- **WR** — Wolf-Rayet: broad emission, hydrogen-poor, evolved massive star
- **C (C0–C6)** — Carbon star: C/O > 1, TiO absent, C₂ and CN prominent
- **S** — zirconium and other s-process elements; transitional between M and C
