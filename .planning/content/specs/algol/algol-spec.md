## Definition

Algol (Beta Persei, β Per) is a triple star system in the constellation Perseus, located 94 light-years from Earth, best known as the prototype of eclipsing binary variable stars — a class in which the apparent brightness drops periodically as one star passes in front of the other as seen from Earth. Its periodic dimming, visible to the naked eye every 2.87 days, earned it the name "Demon Star" and made it one of the most historically significant variable stars in astronomy.

## Full Content

### What Algol is
- Algol is one of the best-known variable stars in the night sky — it changes brightness noticeably every 2.87 days, a cycle easily tracked with the naked eye [Wikipedia]
- It is a triple star system: two stars (Aa1 and Aa2) form a close eclipsing pair with a 2.87-day orbit; a third star (Ab) orbits this inner pair with a period of about 680 days [Wikipedia; Britannica]
- The primary eclipse, when the cooler K-type subgiant (Aa2) passes in front of the hotter B-type main-sequence star (Aa1), dims the system from magnitude 2.12 to 3.4 over roughly 10 hours [Wikipedia]
- A secondary, shallower eclipse occurs when the B star passes in front of the K star — the drop is much smaller and harder to detect visually [Wikipedia]

### Physical properties
- The primary star, Algol Aa1, is a hot blue-white B8 main-sequence star with 3.17 solar masses, 2.73 solar radii, and a surface temperature of about 13,000 K [Wikipedia]
- The secondary, Algol Aa2, is a cool orange K0 subgiant with only 0.70 solar masses but a larger radius (3.48 R☉), bloated by its evolution off the main sequence [Wikipedia]
- The tertiary companion, Algol Ab, is an F1 main-sequence star of 1.76 solar masses; it does not participate in the eclipses seen from Earth because its orbital plane is misaligned [Wikipedia; Britannica]
- Algol is the second-brightest star in Perseus and, at magnitude 2.12 at maximum, is a prominent naked-eye star [Britannica]

### The Algol paradox
- The Algol paradox is the apparent contradiction that the less massive star (Aa2, 0.70 M☉) has already evolved into a subgiant, while the more massive star (Aa1, 3.17 M☉) is still on the main sequence [Wikipedia — Algol paradox; Tidjma.tn]
- In standard stellar evolution, more massive stars evolve faster, so a higher-mass star should leave the main sequence before a lower-mass one [Wikipedia — Algol paradox]
- The resolution is mass transfer: when the originally more massive star expanded to fill its Roche lobe (the gravitational boundary within which material is bound to it), gas streamed across to its companion [Wikipedia — Algol paradox]
- This reversed the mass ratio: the star that was originally heavier donated so much material that it is now the lighter one, yet it had already evolved to the subgiant stage [Wikipedia — Algol paradox]
- The Algol system is an ongoing mass-transfer binary; Aa2 is still losing material to Aa1 today [Wikipedia]
- Algol-type binaries (EA variables) are a whole class of semi-detached binaries defined by this same scenario [Wikipedia — Algol variable]

### Observation
- Algol's eclipse minimum is predicted with high precision; online calculators and the AAVSO publish schedules in advance [AAVSO; EarthSky]
- Observing an eclipse requires only the naked eye or binoculars; compare Algol against nearby reference stars Almach (γ And, magnitude 2.1) and Mirfak (α Per, magnitude 1.8) [EarthSky]
- The CHARA interferometer has spatially resolved the inner pair, producing direct images of both stars as separate discs, confirming the sizes inferred from spectroscopy [Wikimedia Commons — CHARA image, Fabien Baron 2012]
- NASA's TESS satellite has recorded a high-precision light curve showing both primary and secondary eclipses in detail [Wikimedia Commons — TESS lightcurve]

### Ancient Egyptian connection
- Research published in 2012 and expanded in a 2015 PLOS ONE study by Finnish scholars (Lauri Jetsu et al.) identified a 2.85-day cycle in the Cairo Calendar, an ancient Egyptian astronomical document dated to approximately 1244–1163 BCE [phys.org / PLOS ONE doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0144140]
- The calendar records lucky and unlucky periods for daily activity; Jetsu's team argued that the 2.85-day period corresponds to Algol's eclipsing cycle, making this potentially the earliest known record of Algol's variability — predating Goodricke by over 3,000 years [phys.org]
- The slight difference from the modern period (2.85 vs. 2.867 days) is consistent with a slow change in Algol's orbital period over millennia, possibly linked to the three-body dynamics and ongoing mass transfer [phys.org]

## Historical Context

- **1667**: Italian astronomer Geminiano Montanari first recorded Algol's light variation in writing [Wikipedia; Britannica notes 1670]
- **1782–1783**: English amateur astronomer John Goodricke (age 17) measured the period systematically and proposed, before the Royal Society in May 1783, that an unseen dark companion was eclipsing the star — the correct explanation. He was awarded the Copley Medal for this work in 1783 [Wikipedia]
- **1881**: Edward Charles Pickering at Harvard College Observatory presented evidence consistent with an eclipsing binary interpretation [Wikipedia]
- **1889**: Hermann Carl Vogel at Potsdam Observatory detected periodic Doppler shifts in Algol's spectrum, confirming that the two components were orbiting each other — one of the first spectroscopic binaries ever identified [Wikipedia]
- **2009**: The CHARA interferometer at Mount Wilson Observatory produced the first direct resolved image of the Algol system, showing the three components as separate objects [Wikimedia Commons; Fabien Baron]
- **c. 1244–1163 BCE (proposed)**: The Cairo Calendar may encode Algol's eclipsing period, suggesting ancient Egyptians observed it [PLOS ONE 2015]
- **Name**: "Algol" derives from the Arabic _raʾs al-ghūl_ meaning "head of the ogre/demon"; the star represents the severed Gorgon's head (Medusa) held by Perseus in Greek mythology. In Latin astronomical tradition it was _Caput Medusae_ or _Caput Larvae_ [Wikipedia; EarthSky]

## Sub-types / Classifications

- **Algol (EA) variables**: Semi-detached eclipsing binaries where the secondary fills its Roche lobe and transfers mass to the primary. Algol is the prototype; tens of thousands of such systems are catalogued [Wikipedia — Algol variable]
- **Semi-detached vs. detached binaries**: In a detached binary neither star fills its Roche lobe; in a semi-detached one (like Algol), the secondary does. A contact binary has both stars filling their lobes (W UMa type) [Wikipedia]
- **Spectroscopic binary**: Algol was among the earliest confirmed spectroscopic binaries (1889), where the binary nature is revealed by periodic Doppler shifts in spectral lines rather than resolved visual components [Wikipedia]
