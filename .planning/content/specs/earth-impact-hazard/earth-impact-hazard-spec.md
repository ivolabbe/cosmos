## Overview

The Earth impact hazard is the risk posed by near-Earth objects (NEOs) — asteroids and comets whose orbits bring them close to Earth's path — striking the planet and causing local to global-scale damage. While large impacts are rare on human timescales, they are statistically inevitable on geological ones, and constitute a uniquely preventable natural catastrophe because detection and deflection are both technically feasible.

---

## Near-Earth Objects: Definitions and Counts

A **near-Earth object (NEO)** is any small Solar System body with a perihelion (closest approach to the Sun) of less than 1.3 AU. The two main classes are: [Wikipedia, Near-Earth object]

- **Near-Earth asteroids (NEAs):** 37,255 known (as of December 2024)
- **Near-Earth comets (NECs):** 123 known (as of December 2024)

NEAs are subdivided by orbital characteristics: [Wikipedia, Near-Earth object]
- **Apollos** (21,132 known): semi-major axis >1 AU; cross Earth's orbit
- **Amors** (13,137 known): orbit strictly outside Earth's orbit; do not currently cross it
- **Atens** (2,952 known): semi-major axis <1 AU; cross Earth's orbit
- **Atiras** (34 known): orbits entirely inside Earth's orbit

**Potentially hazardous asteroids (PHAs)** are NEAs larger than ~140 m in diameter whose orbit passes within 0.05 AU of Earth's orbit. As of December 2024, 2,465 known NEAs meet this classification. [Wikipedia, Near-Earth object]

**Current NEO discovery statistics (December 2024):** [Wikipedia, Earth impact hazard]
- Total NEOs discovered: 37,378
- NEAs >1 km diameter: 867 (approximately 93% of the estimated ~981 total)
- NEAs >140 m diameter: 11,167 (approximately 44% of estimated ~35,000 total)
- Potentially hazardous asteroids: 2,465

The first near-Earth asteroid, 433 Eros, was discovered in 1898. Systematic discovery accelerated dramatically in the 1990s following Congressional mandates and the establishment of dedicated sky surveys. [Wikipedia, Near-Earth object]

## Impact Frequency by Size

Statistical models predict the following average recurrence intervals for stony asteroid impacts: [Wikipedia, Earth impact hazard]

| Impactor diameter | Approximate recurrence | Approximate energy |
|---|---|---|
| ~4 m | ~1 year | sub-kiloton |
| ~7 m | ~5 years | ~15 kt TNT (Hiroshima-scale) |
| ~20 m | decades | ~400–500 kt TNT (Chelyabinsk scale) |
| ~50–60 m | ~few hundred to ~1,300 years | 10–30 Mt TNT (Tunguska scale) |
| ~1 km | ~440,000 years | global effects |
| ~5 km | ~18 million years | mass extinction possible |
| ~10 km | tens of millions of years | major mass extinction |

Note: estimates for Tunguska-sized events range widely — from once every 300 years to once every 2,000–3,000 years depending on the model — reflecting uncertainty in the size distribution of small NEOs. [Wikipedia, Earth impact hazard]

## Risk Assessment Scales

### The Torino Scale

The **Torino scale** is a public-communication tool that categorises the impact hazard from a specific NEO. It was developed by Professor Richard P. Binzel (MIT), first presented in 1995, and formally adopted at an international conference in Turin, Italy, in June 1999. [Wikipedia, Torino scale]

The scale combines collision probability and estimated kinetic energy into a single integer value from 0 to 10:

| Level | Colour | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | White | No hazard; negligible probability or too small to reach surface |
| 1 | Green | Routine discovery; no cause for concern |
| 2–4 | Yellow | Deserves attention by astronomers; regional to global effects possible |
| 5–7 | Orange | Close encounters with serious to unprecedented threat potential |
| 8–10 | Red | Certain collision with regional, hemispheric, or global effects |

The highest Torino rating ever assigned was **4**, given briefly to asteroid 99942 Apophis in December 2004 when an initial 2.7% impact probability was calculated for 2029. Subsequent observations eliminated that risk. [Wikipedia, Torino scale]

### The Palermo Technical Impact Hazard Scale

The **Palermo scale** is a logarithmic, technical tool used by astronomers for more precise risk comparison. It was adopted at the Working Group on Near-Earth Objects meeting in Palermo, Italy, in June 2001. [Wikipedia, Palermo Technical Impact Hazard Scale]

The Palermo scale value P is defined as:

**P = log₁₀(pᵢ / fᵦT)**

where pᵢ is the impact probability, T is the time interval until potential impact, and fᵦ is the background impact frequency (expected rate for objects of similar energy). [Wikipedia, Palermo Technical Impact Hazard Scale]

Interpretation:
- **P = 0:** Hazard equals the average background risk from similarly sized objects
- **P > 0:** Hazard exceeds background; serious concern
- **−2 < P < 0:** Warrants monitoring
- **P < −2:** Negligible consequence

As of April 2026, no known asteroid has a cumulative Palermo rating above 0. The two closest are (29075) 1950 DA and 101955 Bennu, both rated between −2 and 0. [Wikipedia, Palermo Technical Impact Hazard Scale]

## Notable Historical Impacts

### Chicxulub (66 million years ago)

The most consequential impact in the geological record struck what is now the Yucatán Peninsula of Mexico approximately 66.043 ± 0.043 million years ago. [Wikipedia, Chicxulub crater]

- **Impactor diameter:** ~10 km
- **Crater dimensions:** ~180–200 km diameter, buried ~1 km beneath younger sediments
- **Energy released:** estimated 72 teratonnes of TNT (300 ZJ)
- **Extinction consequences:** triggered the Cretaceous–Paleogene (K–Pg) mass extinction, killing approximately 75% of all species on Earth, including all non-avian dinosaurs
- **Evidence:** shocked quartz, global iridium anomaly at the K–Pg boundary, tektite fields, and a gravity anomaly over the buried crater structure [Wikipedia, Chicxulub crater]

### Tunguska (1908)

On 30 June 1908, a stony asteroid estimated at 50–60 m in diameter exploded as an **air burst** at an altitude of 5–10 km above the Podkamennaya Tunguska River in Siberia, Russia. [Wikipedia, Tunguska event]

- **Energy released:** 10–30 megatons of TNT equivalent (some estimates range 3–50 Mt)
- **Forest devastated:** approximately 2,150 km² flattened in a distinctive butterfly-shaped pattern
- **Casualties:** essentially zero, due to the extreme remoteness of the region
- **Cause:** scientific consensus favours an asteroidal origin; a 2001 study found 83% probability of an asteroidal orbital path [Wikipedia, Tunguska event]

Tunguska remains the largest impact event in recorded history. Because it produced no crater — only an air burst — it went unscientifically investigated for over a decade. It defines a key benchmark in hazard assessments: a Tunguska-scale event over a modern city would cause catastrophic casualties.

### Chelyabinsk (2013)

On 15 February 2013, an 18-metre asteroid entered Earth's atmosphere at approximately 19.2 km/s and exploded at ~30 km altitude above Chelyabinsk Oblast, Russia. [Wikipedia, Chelyabinsk meteor]

- **Mass:** approximately 10,000 tonnes
- **Energy released:** 400–500 kilotonnes of TNT, roughly 30 times the yield of the Hiroshima bomb
- **Injuries:** 1,491 people sought medical treatment
- **Damage:** over 7,200 buildings across six cities; estimated $33 million (2013 USD)
- **Deaths:** none

Chelyabinsk was the largest bolide to affect a populated region since Tunguska. It arrived from the direction of the Sun and was not detected in advance, demonstrating the detection challenge posed by small, Sun-approaching objects. [Wikipedia, Chelyabinsk meteor]

## Asteroid Apophis: A Case Study in Risk Assessment

Asteroid **99942 Apophis** (diameter ~340 m) was discovered in June 2004. Initial calculations in December 2004 yielded a 2.7% probability of impact on 13 April 2029, the highest impact probability ever computed for a sizeable object, earning it a Torino scale rating of 4. [Wikipedia, Torino scale; NASA science.nasa.gov/apophis]

Subsequent radar observations and refined orbital solutions progressively eliminated the 2029 risk, and by March 2021, Apophis was formally removed from all impact risk lists for at least the next 100 years. [NASA/JPL via WebSearch]

On 13 April 2029, Apophis will nonetheless pass just ~31,600 km from Earth's surface — closer than geostationary satellites and easily visible to the naked eye. This will be the closest known approach by an asteroid of this size in recorded history. NASA has redirected the OSIRIS-REx spacecraft (renamed OSIRIS-APEX) to rendezvous with Apophis shortly after this flyby. [WebSearch, NASA Science]

## Planetary Defense Strategies

Multiple techniques have been proposed and, in one case, successfully tested to deflect threatening NEOs. All viable methods aim to **deflect**, not destroy, the NEO — fragmentation would still cause widespread destruction from the resulting debris cloud. [Wikipedia, Earth impact hazard]

### Kinetic Impactor (demonstrated)

A spacecraft impacts the asteroid at high velocity, transferring momentum to alter its orbit. This is the most technically mature approach, demonstrated in practice by NASA's DART mission (see below). [Wikipedia, Planetary defense]

### Gravity Tractor

A spacecraft stations itself near the asteroid and uses its own gravitational attraction to slowly pull the object off its collision course. Proposed by Edward T. Lu and Stanley G. Love, this method is composition-independent and spin-rate-independent but requires years of operation. [Wikipedia, Planetary defense]

### Nuclear Detonation

Nuclear devices offer 10–100 times more energy than non-nuclear alternatives and can operate via stand-off detonation (above the surface) or subsurface burial. Stand-off detonation is preferred for loosely aggregated "rubble pile" asteroids to avoid fragmentation. [Wikipedia, Planetary defense]

### Laser Ablation and Mass Driver

Focused laser energy vaporises surface material, producing thrust via the reaction force (the proposed DE-STAR system). Mass drivers eject material from the asteroid's own surface as propellant. Both require extended proximity to the target. [Wikipedia, Planetary defense]

## The DART Mission

NASA's **Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART)** was the first full-scale planetary defense experiment. DART targeted Dimorphos, the moonlet of the binary asteroid Didymos, and impacted it on **26 September 2022 at 23:14 UTC** at approximately 6.1 km/s. [NASA/JPL via WebSearch]

**Results:** [NASA news release; Nature, doi:10.1038/s41586-023-05805-2]
- Dimorphos's orbital period around Didymos was shortened by **32 minutes** (from 11 h 55 min to 11 h 23 min), with an uncertainty of ±2 minutes
- NASA's pre-mission success threshold was a change of ≥73 seconds; the actual result exceeded this by more than 25 times
- Ejecta recoil from material blasted off Dimorphos contributed substantially to the momentum transfer, amplifying DART's direct push

DART confirmed that the kinetic impactor approach can work in principle. The European Space Agency's **Hera** mission, launched in 2024, will travel to the Didymos system to characterise the impact crater and measure Dimorphos's internal structure, providing ground-truth data to refine deflection models. [Wikipedia, Planetary defense]

## The Spaceguard Survey

**Spaceguard** is the collective term for systematic programmes to discover and catalogue NEOs. The name was coined by Arthur C. Clarke in his 1973 novel *Rendezvous with Rama* and later adopted for real-world efforts. [Wikipedia, Spaceguard]

The **1992 U.S. Congressional Spaceguard Survey Report** established the "Spaceguard Goal": NASA must discover 90% of near-Earth asteroids larger than 1 km in diameter within 10 years. A later Congressional mandate extended this to objects ≥140 m. [Wikipedia, Spaceguard]

The 1 km goal was achieved by 2011. Key contributing surveys include:
- **Lincoln Near-Earth Asteroid Research (LINEAR)**
- **Catalina Sky Survey** — detected 2008 TC3, the first asteroid predicted to hit Earth before atmospheric entry
- **Pan-STARRS** — also discovered interstellar object ʻOumuamua in 2017
- **Asteroid Terrestrial-impact Last Alert System (ATLAS)**
- **NASA Scout** — operational since 2016, for rapid assessment of newly discovered objects

**Forthcoming surveys:**
- **Vera C. Rubin Observatory** (first light ~2025): expected to increase known asteroid count by a factor of 10–100
- **NEO Surveyor** (NASA, planned launch ~2027): infrared space telescope projected to identify 76% of NEOs ≥140 m in diameter [Wikipedia, Earth impact hazard]

## International Coordination

International response frameworks include: [Wikipedia, Planetary defense]

- **International Asteroid Warning Network (IAWN):** established via UN Committee approval in October 2013; acts as a clearinghouse for shared observational data. Activation threshold: impact probability >1% within 20 years for an object >10 m.
- **Space Missions Planning Advisory Group (SMPAG):** coordinates joint deflection mission studies and manages the diplomatic complexity of shifting an impact footprint across national territories.
- **UN Office for Outer Space Affairs (UNOOSA):** oversees notification protocols and public disclosure.

The United Nations has designated 2029 the International Year of Asteroid Awareness and Planetary Defence, timed to coincide with the Apophis flyby. [United Nations observances via WebSearch]
