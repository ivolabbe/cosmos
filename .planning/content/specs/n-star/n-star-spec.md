## Definition

An N star (also written N-type star, or C-N star in the modern classification) is a cool, carbon-rich red giant whose atmosphere contains more carbon than oxygen, producing strong molecular bands of C₂, CN, and CH rather than the titanium oxide (TiO) bands seen in ordinary M-type giants; it is the classic, cool division of the carbon star family, now placed on the asymptotic giant branch of stellar evolution.

## Full Content

### What an N star is

- N-type stars are the coolest and most deeply red class of carbon stars, with effective temperatures of 2,600–3,100 K. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
- Their defining chemical signature is a carbon-to-oxygen (C/O) ratio exceeding 1. In most stars (like the Sun), C/O < 1, so oxygen outstrips carbon; the excess oxygen locks up carbon as CO, and remaining oxygen forms metal oxides like TiO. When C/O > 1, the reverse occurs: free carbon atoms survive to form C₂, CN, CH, C₃, SiC₂, and other carbon molecules, completely changing the spectrum. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
- The atmosphere absorbs so strongly in the blue and violet (via C₂ Swan bands and diffuse blue absorption) that N stars appear strikingly red — one of the reddest stellar colour classes known. [Wikipedia, Carbon star; NED/IPAC Glossary]
- N stars are luminous giants: MV ≈ −2.2, making them detectable in nearby galaxies and usable as distance indicators. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
- They concentrate toward the Galactic plane, consistent with thin disc (Population I) membership and intermediate to low stellar masses. [NED/IPAC Glossary; Wikipedia, Carbon star]

### Spectral classification — from Harvard N to modern C-N

- The Harvard classification system (late 19th century) placed carbon stars in two classes: R (less red, hotter) and N (more red, cooler). Both showed carbon bands rather than TiO. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
- In the 1993 revised Morgan–Keenan (MK) system, the old Harvard N class became **C-N** — one of five carbon star sub-types. The C-N designation retains the physics of the N stars while situating them within a broader framework. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
- The five modern carbon star sub-types are:
  - **C-R**: "old Harvard class R reborn"; visible at the blue end; strong isotopic bands; no enhanced barium; 2,800–5,100 K; ~25 known. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
  - **C-N** (= N stars): heavy blue absorption; enhanced s-process elements (Ba, Tc, Zr); 2,600–3,100 K; MV ≈ −2.2; ~90 known. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
  - **C-J**: very strong ¹³C isotopic bands of C₂ and CN; 2,800–3,900 K; ~20 known; e.g. Y CVn (La Superba). [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
  - **C-H**: strong CH absorption; found in binary systems where mass transfer occurred from a former AGB companion (now a white dwarf); 4,100–5,000 K; MV ≈ −1.8; ~20 known. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
  - **C-Hd**: hydrogen lines and CH bands weak or absent; ~7 known; MV ≈ −3.5. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
- Before 1993 the MK system used a two-digit notation: C[temperature class][C₂ strength], e.g. C5,4 for Y CVn. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]

### Physical mechanism — AGB evolution and the third dredge-up

- N stars are asymptotic giant branch (AGB) stars — low- to intermediate-mass stars (~0.5–8 M☉) that have exhausted hydrogen and helium core burning and are now burning shells around a degenerate carbon-oxygen core. [Wikipedia, AGB]
- During the AGB phase, the helium shell undergoes periodic **thermal pulses** (shell flashes) — sudden ignitions that peak at thousands of times the star's normal luminosity, then decay over a few years. Pulses recur every 10⁴–10⁵ years. [Wikipedia, AGB]
- After each thermal pulse, convection dips inward in what is called the **third dredge-up**, bringing carbon produced by the triple-alpha process (3 ⁴He → ¹²C) up from the deep interior to the stellar surface. [Wikipedia, Carbon star; Wikipedia, AGB]
- After enough dredge-up events, the surface C/O ratio exceeds 1 and the star becomes a carbon star. A star that was initially an oxygen-rich giant (M-type) can thus transform into an N-type carbon star through this process. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
- Alongside carbon, **s-process elements** — barium (Ba), technetium (Tc), zirconium (Zr) — are synthesised in the shell flashes and also dredged to the surface, appearing as enhanced absorption features in the spectrum; their presence is a hallmark of C-N stars. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
- The transient presence of technetium (half-life ~4 Myr) in N star spectra confirms that nucleosynthesis is actively occurring in the star — it is not a relic from the past. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]

### Mass loss and dust production

- As much as half (or more) of a carbon star's total mass can be lost through powerful stellar winds during the AGB phase. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
- The outflowing carbon-rich material condenses into dust grains — silicon carbide (SiC) and graphite-like carbon — forming a circumstellar dust shell that further reddens the star's appearance. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
- This dusty outflow is a major source of carbon-rich interstellar dust; presolar SiC grains from carbon star outflows have been found preserved in primitive meteorites, providing direct laboratory samples of AGB nucleosynthesis products. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
- R Leporis, imaged by ALMA at the highest resolution ever achieved, shows a ring-like gas structure surrounding the stellar surface with material actively escaping — a direct view of this mass-loss process. [ESO/ALMA press release, ann23019a]

### Notable examples

- **R Leporis** (Hind's Crimson Star): the archetypal N star. Spectral type C7,6e(N6e). Discovered by J. R. Hind in 1845, who described it as "like a drop of blood on a black field." Temperature 2,290 K; luminosity 13,200 L☉; radius 710–910 R☉; distance 1,490 ly. A Mira-type variable with period ~427 days and magnitude range +5.5 to +11.7. Its extreme red colour results from heavy carbon absorption of blue light. [Wikipedia, R Leporis]
- **Y Canum Venaticorum** (La Superba): classified C54J(N3), technically a C-J star (carbon-13 rich). Named "La Superba" by Angelo Secchi for its magnificent red colour. Temperature ~2,760 K; distance ~1,010 ly; radius ~315 R☉. One of the coolest and reddest known stars. [Wikipedia, Y CVn]

### Astrophysical significance

- Carbon stars are among the most luminous AGB stars, especially in the near-infrared, enabling detection in nearby galaxies such as the Magellanic Clouds. A large sample has a near-uniform median luminosity, making them useful as extragalactic distance indicators. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
- They are major contributors to the enrichment of the interstellar medium with carbon and s-process elements, playing a key role in Galactic chemical evolution. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]

## Historical Context

- Angelo Secchi observed the first carbon stars in the 1860s and established **Secchi class IV** for carbon-rich red stars — one of his original four stellar spectral categories. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
- In the late 1890s, as the Harvard classification system (led by Williamina Fleming, Annie Jump Cannon, et al.) was developed, Secchi class IV stars were reclassified as **N-class stars**. A companion class **R** was added for the less-red, hotter carbon stars. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
- The R–N sequence spanned what would now correspond roughly to G7 through M10 in conventional spectral classification (by temperature). [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
- In 1960 Morgan and Keenan proposed a two-digit C-number scheme (C0–C7 for temperature, followed by C₂ band strength). This was superseded in **1993** by the current five-subtype C system (C-R, C-N, C-J, C-H, C-Hd). [Wikipedia, Carbon star]
- The recognition that R and N types represent physically distinct astrophysical classes (R stars are Population II or Galactic-thick disc; N stars are thin disc AGB stars) motivated the 1993 revision. [Wikipedia, Carbon star]

## Sub-types / Classifications

The term "N star" historically encompasses all of the Harvard N class, but in the modern MK system it maps most directly to **C-N** stars. The broader carbon star family includes:

| MK Type | Old Name | T (K) | Key Feature | ~Count |
|---------|----------|--------|-------------|--------|
| C-R | Harvard R | 2,800–5,100 | Blue end visible; strong isotopic bands; no enhanced Ba | ~25 |
| C-N | Harvard N | 2,600–3,100 | Heavy blue absorption; enhanced s-process (Ba, Tc, Zr) | ~90 |
| C-J | — | 2,800–3,900 | Very strong ¹³C₂ and ¹³CN isotopic bands | ~20 |
| C-H | — | 4,100–5,000 | Strong CH; binary mass-transfer origin | ~20 |
| C-Hd | — | — | Weak H lines and CH bands | ~7 |

N stars (C-N) are distinguished from C-R by their lower temperature, the absence of a visible blue spectral end, and the presence of enhanced s-process elements indicating active AGB nucleosynthesis.
