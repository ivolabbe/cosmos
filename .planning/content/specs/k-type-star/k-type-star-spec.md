# K-type Star — Article Spec

## Category
Stars

## Definition (1–2 sentences)
A K-type star is a star with an orange colour and a surface temperature between approximately 3,900 and 5,300 K, cooler than the Sun (a G-type star) but hotter than M-type red dwarfs. Their spectra are dominated by neutral metallic absorption lines with extremely weak hydrogen lines, and K-type main-sequence stars — often called "orange dwarfs" or "Goldilocks stars" — are considered among the most promising hosts for life-bearing exoplanets.

## Full Content (ALL facts needed for 200–400 words)

### Spectral Classification and Physical Properties
K-type stars occupy class K in the Harvard spectral classification scheme, which orders stars by decreasing surface temperature: O, B, A, F, G, K, M. Their effective surface temperatures span 3,900–5,300 K [Wikipedia: Stellar classification]. Main-sequence K dwarfs (luminosity class V) have masses of 0.45–0.86 solar masses, radii of 0.70–0.96 solar radii, and luminosities of 0.08–0.60 solar luminosities [Wikipedia: K-type main-sequence star]. Their conventional colour is "light orange" against a white reference, appearing "pale yellowish orange" under daylight conditions [Wikipedia: K-type star]. The colour index (B−V) ranges from approximately 0.82 to 1.40 [Wikipedia: K-type main-sequence star].

### Spectral Features
K-type spectra are distinguished by extremely weak hydrogen Balmer absorption lines — far weaker than in the hotter G-type stars — and by numerous neutral metal lines from iron (Fe I), manganese (Mn I), silicon (Si I), calcium, and sodium [Wikipedia: K-type star; astro4edu.org]. Cyanogen (CN) and the hydroxyl radical (OH) molecular bands persist throughout the class. In the late subtypes (K7–K9), faint bands of titanium oxide (TiO) begin to appear, the dominant feature in cooler M-type stars [Wikipedia: Stellar classification]. The dense forest of metallic lines gives K-type spectra a "choppy, ragged appearance" [astro4edu.org].

### Subtypes K0–K9
K-type stars are subdivided into ten subtypes from K0 (hottest, ~5,290 K) to K9 (coolest, ~3,930 K) [Wikipedia: K-type main-sequence star]. Standard spectral classification anchors include Sigma Draconis (K0 V), Alpha Centauri B (K1 V), Epsilon Eridani (K2 V), and 61 Cygni A (K5 V) [Wikipedia: Stellar classification; Wikipedia: K-type main-sequence star]. In practice, the subdivisions K8 and K9 are rarely used.

### K Dwarfs vs. K Giants
K-type stars exist across multiple luminosity classes. K giants (luminosity class III) are evolved post-main-sequence stars; Arcturus (α Boötis, K1.5 III) is the fourth-brightest star in the night sky at a distance of 36.7 light-years, with a radius ~25 times solar and a luminosity ~170 times solar [Wikipedia: Arcturus]. Aldebaran (α Tauri, K5 III) and Pollux (β Geminorum, K0 III) are other prominent K giants visible to the naked eye [astro4edu.org]. Even hypergiant K-type stars exist; RW Cephei is an extreme example [Wikipedia: K-type star]. The main-sequence K dwarfs (orange dwarfs) are, however, the most scientifically significant subclass for exoplanet habitability research.

### Prevalence in the Galaxy
Approximately 12–13% of main-sequence stars in the solar neighbourhood are K-type [Wikipedia: K-type star; Wikipedia: Habitability of K-type main-sequence star systems]. This makes K dwarfs roughly three to four times more abundant than G-type (Sun-like) stars and about three times as numerous overall [NASA Science / Hubble; Wikipedia: K-type main-sequence star]. Roughly 1,000 K-type stars lie within 100 light-years of Earth [NASA Science / Hubble].

### The "Goldilocks Stars" for Life
K dwarfs are widely considered prime targets for the search for life because they occupy a "sweet spot" between the rarer, shorter-lived G stars and the more numerous but potentially hazardous M red dwarfs [NASA Science / Hubble, quoting Edward Guinan, Villanova University]. Key advantages are:

1. **Longevity**: K dwarfs remain on the main sequence for 17–70 billion years, compared to ~10 billion years for the Sun, providing far more time for biological evolution [Wikipedia: Habitability of K-type main-sequence star systems; NASA Science / Hubble].
2. **Reduced high-energy radiation**: Planets in K-dwarf habitable zones receive approximately 1/100th as much X-ray radiation as planets in the close-in habitable zones of active M-dwarf stars [NASA Science / Hubble].
3. **Lower flare activity**: K dwarfs do not sustain the intense magnetic fields that drive the frequent, powerful stellar flares that can strip planetary atmospheres around M dwarfs [NASA Science / Hubble].
4. **Tidal locking avoidance**: Habitable-zone planets around K dwarfs are typically far enough from their star to escape tidal locking, unlike the inner-orbit planets around M dwarfs [Wikipedia: Habitability of K-type main-sequence star systems].
5. **Stable habitable zones**: The circumstellar habitable zone ranges from roughly 0.1–0.4 AU to 0.3–1.3 AU (depending on stellar mass) and remains stable through most of the star's main-sequence lifetime [Wikipedia: Habitability of K-type main-sequence star systems].

One important caveat: recent research shows that K dwarfs emit "dangerously high levels of X-rays and far ultraviolet (FUV) radiation for considerably longer into their early main-sequence phase" than either G or early-M stars, which may delay or inhibit the emergence of life on young planets [Wikipedia: Habitability of K-type main-sequence star systems; Wikipedia: K-type main-sequence star].

## Historical Context

- **1866–1890s**: The Harvard spectral classification system develops under Edward Pickering and Williamina Fleming, later systematised by Annie Jump Cannon; class K is assigned to stars intermediate between G and M in the temperature sequence.
- **1901**: Annie Jump Cannon introduces the modern O–B–A–F–G–K–M ordering in her revised classification, with K as the penultimate class before the coolest M stars.
- **1838**: Friedrich Bessel measures the parallax of 61 Cygni, a K5 binary system, becoming the first to determine the distance to a star other than the Sun — though the spectral classification did not exist yet.
- **1943**: The MK (Morgan–Keenan) two-dimensional classification adds luminosity classes (I–V) to spectral types, allowing K dwarfs and K giants to be formally distinguished.
- **2000**: Detection of exoplanet Epsilon Eridani b (Aegir) around a K2 V star; one of the first confirmed exoplanets around a nearby star.
- **2010**: Todd Henry and colleagues (AAS) formally propose calling K dwarfs "Goldilocks stars" for habitability research.
- **2019 onwards**: NASA Habitable Exoplanet Observatory (HabEx) and LUVOIR mission studies prioritise K dwarfs as optimal targets.

## Sub-types / Classifications

1. **K0–K9 (temperature subtypes)**: Hottest (K0, ~5,290 K) to coolest (K9, ~3,930 K); K8 and K9 rarely used in practice.
2. **K V (orange dwarfs / main-sequence)**: Long-lived hydrogen-burning stars, 0.45–0.86 M☉; the "Goldilocks" subclass for habitability.
3. **K III (K giants)**: Evolved post-main-sequence stars with expanded radii; Arcturus and Aldebaran are canonical examples.
4. **K I/II (K supergiants/bright giants)**: Rare massive evolved stars; often variable.
5. **K Ia/Ib/0 (K hypergiants)**: Extremely rare; RW Cephei is the outstanding example.
6. **K subdwarfs (sdK)**: Low-metallicity stars, slightly underluminous compared to main-sequence K stars.
