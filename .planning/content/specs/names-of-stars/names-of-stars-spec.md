## Definition
Stars acquire names through a layered system of proper names (mostly derived from Arabic and Latin), systematic letter-and-number designations tied to their constellation, and catalogue numbers — with the International Astronomical Union (IAU) serving as the sole authority for official approval of proper names.

## Full Content

### Proper Names

- Of the roughly 200–400 billion stars in the Milky Way, only ~10,000 are visible to the naked eye; of these, fewer than 600 have IAU-approved proper names [Wikipedia, List of proper names of stars; Star name].
- Most proper names originate from **Arabic**, inherited from medieval Islamic astronomy, which itself drew from Ptolemy's 2nd-century *Almagest* (translated into Arabic in the 8th–9th centuries) [Wikipedia, Arabic star names].
- The Arabic scholar **Abd al-Rahman al-Sufi** (*Book of Fixed Stars*, 10th century) standardised many of these names and is a key transmission node [Wikipedia, Arabic star names].
- During medieval European translation of Arabic texts, names were frequently mis-transcribed by scholars who did not know Arabic, creating inconsistent spellings that persisted for centuries — e.g. Almach / Almaach / Almak for the same star [Wikipedia, Star name].
- Other proper names derive from **Latin** (Capella = "small female goat"), **Greek** (Arcturus = "Guardian of the Bear"), or from **modern languages** introduced through the IAU's NameExoWorlds campaigns (Hungarian, Thai, etc.) [Wikipedia, List of proper names of stars; Star name].
- A small subset of stars carry names honouring astronomers: **Barnard's Star** (E.E. Barnard), **Kapteyn's Star** (Jacobus Kapteyn), **Tabby's Star** (Tabetha Boyajian) [Wikipedia, Star name].
- Some modern English names were assigned for practical purposes: **Peacock** (α Pavonis) and **Avior** (ε Carinae) were named in 1937 to give British aviators navigational reference stars with pronounceable English names [Wikipedia, Star name].
- Stars frequently carry multiple names from different traditions; the IAU WGSN has standardised one official proper name per star since 2016 [Wikipedia, IAU Working Group on Star Names].
- Commercial companies sell "star naming rights" as gifts, but the IAU explicitly does not recognise these and notes the names appear only in the selling company's private catalogue [Wikipedia, Star name].

### Bayer Designations

- German astronomer **Johann Bayer** introduced the first systematic designation scheme in his 1603 star atlas *Uranometria*, assigning Greek letters (α through ω) to stars within each constellation, roughly in order of decreasing brightness [Wikipedia, Bayer designation].
- The genitive form of the constellation's Latin name follows the letter: e.g. α Tauri = Alpha Tauri (Aldebaran) [Wikipedia, Bayer designation].
- When the 24 Greek letters ran out, Bayer continued with uppercase A, then lowercase b–z (omitting j and v), giving up to 48 designations per constellation [Wikipedia, Bayer designation].
- The brightness ordering is imperfect: **Pollux** (β Geminorum) is brighter than **Castor** (α Geminorum) — Bayer grouped stars by magnitude class and sometimes assigned letters arbitrarily within a class [Wikipedia, Bayer designation].
- At least 30 constellations lack a star designated α (brightest); four have no α at all [Wikipedia, Bayer designation].
- Numeric superscripts distinguish multiple stars sharing one letter: e.g. π¹ through π⁶ Orionis [Wikipedia, Bayer designation].

### Flamsteed Designations

- French astronomer **J.-J. Lalande** added sequential numbers (by increasing right ascension within each constellation) to John Flamsteed's 1725 star catalogue *Historia Coelestis Britannica* for his 1783 almanac [Wikipedia, Flamsteed designation].
- 2,554 stars received Flamsteed numbers, covering the northern sky as observable from southern England [Wikipedia, Flamsteed designation].
- Flamsteed numbers are generally preferred over Bayer's Roman-letter designations when no Greek-letter Bayer designation exists [Wikipedia, Flamsteed designation].
- Example: 61 Cygni (no Bayer Greek letter) — the first star whose parallax (distance) was measured (Bessel, 1838).

### Variable Star Designations

- Variable stars discovered in each constellation receive uppercase Latin letter designations starting at **R** (to avoid clashing with Bayer letters) through Z, then double-letter pairs RR–RZ, SS–SZ, … ZZ, then AA–AZ … QQ–QZ (J omitted throughout) — 334 combinations total [Wikipedia, Variable star designation].
- After 334, variables are numbered V335, V336, and so on [Wikipedia, Variable star designation].
- This scheme was devised by **Friedrich W. Argelander** in the early 19th century [Wikipedia, Variable star designation].
- Administration of the variable-star catalogue has been delegated by the IAU to Moscow's **Sternberg Astronomical Institute** since 1946 [Wikipedia, Variable star designation].

### Multiple-Star and Exoplanet Designations

- Stars in multiple systems append uppercase Latin letters to the system name: Mizar (in the Big Dipper, apparently a single star) comprises four components labelled Mizar Aa, Ab, Ba, and Bb [Astro4Edu glossary].
- Exoplanets receive lowercase letters appended to the host-star name, starting with **b** (a = the star itself): e.g. 51 Pegasi b, Kepler-452b [Astro4Edu glossary; Wikipedia, Star name].

### Major Catalogues

- **Hipparchus** (~150 BC): ~850 naked-eye stars — the first systematic enumeration [Wikipedia, Star name].
- **Bayer's *Uranometria*** (1603): ~1,700 stars, introduced Greek-letter designations [Wikipedia, Star name].
- **Flamsteed's *Historia Coelestis Britannica*** (1725): northern sky to ~6th magnitude [Wikipedia, Flamsteed designation].
- **Bonner Durchmusterung** (1859): 320,000 northern stars [Wikipedia, Star name].
- **Henry Draper Catalogue** (1918–1924): 225,300 stars with spectral classifications; funded by Mary Anna Palmer Draper, classification led by **Annie Jump Cannon** at Harvard [Wikipedia, Henry Draper Catalogue].
- **Bright Star Catalogue** (1930, multiple editions): all stars brighter than magnitude 6.5 (9,096 stars) [Wikipedia, Star name].
- **Hipparcos satellite catalogue** (ESA, 1997): ~118,000 stars with precise parallaxes — named after Hipparchus.
- **USNO-B1.0**: over 1 billion objects [Wikipedia, Star name].
- **Guide Star Catalog II** (2008): 945 million stars to magnitude 21 [Wikipedia, Star name].

### IAU Standardisation

- The IAU established the **Working Group on Star Names (WGSN)** in May 2016 to catalogue and standardise proper names for the international astronomical community [Wikipedia, IAU Working Group on Star Names].
- The WGSN approved 125 names in its first two batches (July 2016), 102 more in November 2016, and continued approvals through 2017–2018 and beyond [Wikipedia, IAU Working Group on Star Names].
- As of March 2026, **543 proper names** have been officially approved [Wikipedia, List of proper names of stars].
- The IAU's **NameExoWorlds** campaigns (2015, 2019, 2022) invited public submissions to name exoplanets and their host stars, adding culturally diverse names such as Cervantes, Copernicus, and Rosalíadecastro [Wikipedia, Star name; IAU Working Group on Star Names].

## Historical Context

- **2nd century BC**: Hipparchus catalogs ~850 naked-eye stars — the first systematic star list in Western astronomy.
- **2nd century AD**: Ptolemy's *Almagest* extends the catalogue to ~1,025 stars and uses descriptive positions within constellations that Arabic astronomers later formalised as names.
- **8th–9th century**: Arabic translations of the *Almagest* circulate; Muslim astronomers adapt and expand star nomenclature.
- **10th century**: Abd al-Rahman al-Sufi publishes *Book of Fixed Stars*, fixing Arabic names for Ptolemy's stars and adding native Arabian star lore.
- **12th century**: Latin translations of Arabic astronomical texts (often via al-Andalus) transmit Arabic star names to Europe, with frequent transcription errors.
- **1603**: Johann Bayer publishes *Uranometria*, introducing the systematic Greek-letter designation scheme that endures today.
- **1725**: Flamsteed's *Historia Coelestis Britannica* published posthumously; Lalande adds sequential numbers in 1783.
- **1801**: *Histoire céleste française* extends coverage to 47,390 stars.
- **1859**: Bonner Durchmusterung reaches 320,000 stars.
- **1899**: Richard Hinckley Allen publishes *Star Names: Their Lore and Meaning*, recovering Assyrian, Babylonian, and Sumerian star name origins.
- **1918–1924**: Henry Draper Catalogue published; Annie Jump Cannon leads the classification of 225,300 stars.
- **1937**: "Peacock" and "Avior" assigned as English proper names for navigational use.
- **2016**: IAU establishes WGSN; 543 names approved by March 2026.
- **2015, 2019, 2022**: NameExoWorlds campaigns introduce culturally diverse proper names for exoplanet host stars.

## Sub-types / Classifications

The main naming systems in use (not mutually exclusive — a single star may have entries in all):

1. **Proper name** — informal or IAU-approved traditional name (e.g. Sirius, Rigel, Vega). Mostly Arabic or Latin origin.
2. **Bayer designation** — Greek letter (or Roman letter) + constellation genitive (e.g. α Canis Majoris). Introduced 1603, covers ~1,564 stars.
3. **Flamsteed designation** — number + constellation genitive (e.g. 61 Cygni). Covers 2,554 northern-sky stars; preferred when no Greek Bayer letter exists.
4. **Variable star designation** — letter sequence R–Z, then double-letter pairs, then V-number (e.g. RR Lyrae, V404 Cygni). Administered by Sternberg Astronomical Institute.
5. **Catalogue designation** — sequential number in a specific catalogue: HD (Henry Draper), HIP (Hipparcos), SAO, TYC (Tycho-2), etc. Essential for faint stars.
6. **Multiple-system designation** — uppercase Latin letter appended for components in a resolved multiple star (e.g. Mizar Aa, Mizar Ab).
7. **Exoplanet host designation** — mission-specific numbering for planet-hosting stars discovered by surveys (TRAPPIST-1, Kepler-452, HAT-P-7, WASP-1).

**Target length:** 200–400 words

**Key decisions:**
- Article is structured around the three main audiences (general public, students, educators) with the overarching theme that every star carries multiple "identities" from different naming systems.
- Emphasis on Arabic-origin names as the dominant tradition keeps the article culturally rich without becoming a pure catalogue description.
- The IAU WGSN is presented as the contemporary authority, grounding the article in current scientific practice.
- Variable star designations and multiple-system/exoplanet suffixes are included as brief but important completions of the picture.

**Rejected alternatives:**
- A pure historical narrative: would lose the practical utility of knowing how designation systems work today.
- Focusing only on proper names: ignores Flamsteed and catalogue designations that dominate modern professional usage.

**Risks:**
- The star-name count (543 as of March 2026) will change as WGSN continues approvals — writer should include the "as of [date]" qualifier.
- Commercial star-naming is a perennial public confusion point; worth a brief clear statement that IAU does not sell naming rights.

**Open questions:**
- The writer may wish to include one or two more culturally diverse examples from NameExoWorlds (e.g. a South American or African name) to reflect the IAU's broadening of the naming tradition.

**Watch for:** Do not conflate proper names (traditional/official) with catalogue designations; the article must keep both levels clear. "Data" are plural.
