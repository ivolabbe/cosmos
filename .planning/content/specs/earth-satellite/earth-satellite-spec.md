## Definition
An Earth satellite is any object — natural or artificial — that orbits Earth under the influence of gravity. Earth has one natural satellite, the Moon. Artificial satellites are human-made spacecraft placed intentionally into orbit for scientific, communications, navigation, weather, or military purposes.

## Full Content

### What Is a Satellite?

A satellite is any object in a stable orbit around a larger body. Earth has one natural satellite — the Moon — and thousands of artificial satellites. In everyday usage, "satellite" most often refers to the artificial, human-made variety.

The concept of an artificial satellite in orbital flight was first outlined theoretically by Isaac Newton in *Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica* (1687). Newton described a thought experiment in which a cannonball fired horizontally from a mountaintop at sufficient velocity would travel all the way around Earth before falling — an orbit.

### The Moon: Earth's Natural Satellite

Earth's only natural satellite is the **Moon**, orbiting at a mean distance of **384,399 km** (238,854 miles). The Moon completes one orbit of Earth every **27.3 days** (sidereal period), with a synodic period (Moon phase cycle) of **29.5 days**. Its diameter is approximately **3,500 km** — more than one-quarter of Earth's diameter — making it the fifth-largest natural satellite in the Solar System.

The Moon most likely formed from debris ejected when a Mars-sized body (often called Theia) collided with the young Earth roughly 4.5 billion years ago. This giant impact hypothesis is supported by the Moon's composition being similar to Earth's mantle.

[Source: NASA Moon Facts; https://science.nasa.gov/moon/facts/]
[Source: Wikipedia — Moon; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon]

### History of Artificial Satellites

#### Sputnik 1 (1957)
The first artificial Earth satellite was **Sputnik 1**, launched by the Soviet Union on **4 October 1957**. It was approximately 58 cm (22.8 inches) in diameter, weighed 83.6 kg (184 lb), and orbited Earth every 96 minutes on an elliptical path with a perigee of 230 km and an apogee of 940 km. Its sole payload was a low-power radio transmitter that broadcast a repeating beep audible to radio operators worldwide. The transmitter batteries depleted after 22 days, and Sputnik 1 re-entered Earth's atmosphere on 4 January 1958, having completed approximately 1,440 orbits and travelled roughly 70 million km.

[Source: Wikipedia — Sputnik 1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sputnik_1]
[Source: NASA NSSDCA — Sputnik 1; https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/nmc/spacecraft/display.action?id=1957-001B]

#### Explorer 1 and the Space Race
The United States responded with **Explorer 1**, launched on **31 January 1958**. The launch of Sputnik 1 had shocked the Western world, intensified Cold War tensions, and ultimately catalysed the creation of NASA (1958) and the modern era of space exploration.

[Source: Britannica — Earth satellite; https://www.britannica.com/technology/Earth-satellite]

#### Growth of the Satellite Population
By 2017, more than 5,000 satellites had been launched by over 70 nations. As of June 2025, approximately **12,952 satellites** were in Earth orbit, of which 8,530 belonged to the United States, 1,559 to Russia, and 908 to China. The rapid proliferation of commercial mega-constellations — particularly SpaceX's Starlink — has driven most of the recent growth.

[Source: Wikipedia — Satellite; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite]

### Orbit Types

Satellites occupy several families of orbits, each selected to suit the mission's purpose.

#### Low Earth Orbit (LEO)
**Altitude:** ~160–2,000 km above Earth's surface
**Orbital period:** ~88–127 minutes

LEO is the most densely populated orbital regime and hosts the majority of Earth observation satellites, weather satellites, the International Space Station (ISS; at ~400 km), and commercial mega-constellations such as Starlink. Satellites at these altitudes complete roughly 15–16 orbits per day and experience enough residual atmospheric drag to re-enter naturally over years to decades, depending on altitude. Most scientific satellites and Earth-observing instruments reside in LEO.

[Source: NASA — Catalog of Earth Satellite Orbits; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/catalog-of-earth-satellite-orbits]

#### Medium Earth Orbit (MEO)
**Altitude:** ~2,000–35,500 km
**Orbital period:** ~2–24 hours

MEO is primarily used for navigation satellite constellations. GPS satellites orbit at approximately **20,200 km** (26,560 km from Earth's centre) with a period of **12 hours**. Russia's GLONASS, Europe's Galileo, and China's BeiDou systems occupy similar MEO altitudes. The Molniya orbit (highly elliptical, inclination ~63.4°, period 12 hours) provides extended coverage of high-latitude regions and is used by Russian communications satellites.

[Source: NASA — Catalog of Earth Satellite Orbits; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/catalog-of-earth-satellite-orbits]

#### Geostationary Orbit (GEO)
**Altitude:** ~35,786 km above the equator (42,164 km from Earth's centre)
**Orbital period:** 23 hours 56 minutes 4 seconds (matching Earth's rotation)

A satellite in a circular equatorial orbit at this altitude appears stationary over a fixed point on Earth's surface. GEO is used for telecommunications, weather monitoring (e.g., GOES, Meteosat), and search-and-rescue relay. Three satellites in GEO spaced 120° apart can provide near-global coverage (excluding polar regions). The concept of geostationary communications satellites was first proposed by Arthur C. Clarke in 1945.

[Source: NASA — Catalog of Earth Satellite Orbits; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/catalog-of-earth-satellite-orbits]
[Source: Wikipedia — Geostationary orbit; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostationary_orbit]

#### Sun-Synchronous Orbit (SSO)
**Altitude:** typically 600–800 km
**Inclination:** ~96–98° (slightly retrograde)

An SSO is a special class of near-polar LEO in which the orbital plane precesses at the same rate that Earth revolves around the Sun — approximately 1° per day. This means the satellite crosses the equator at the same local solar time on every pass, ensuring consistent solar illumination for imaging. SSO is widely used for Earth observation, climate monitoring, and land/sea imaging satellites such as Landsat and Sentinel.

[Source: NASA — Catalog of Earth Satellite Orbits; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/catalog-of-earth-satellite-orbits]
[Source: ESA — Types of orbits; https://www.esa.int/Enabling_Support/Space_Transportation/Types_of_orbits]

### Applications

Satellites serve a broad range of civil, commercial, and military purposes:

- **Communications:** Relay telephone, internet, television, and radio signals across continents and oceans. GEO communications satellites have formed the backbone of global telecommunications since the 1960s.
- **Weather and climate:** Polar-orbiting and geostationary weather satellites provide cloud imagery, atmospheric temperature profiles, and storm tracking data that underpin modern meteorology.
- **Navigation:** GPS (United States), GLONASS (Russia), Galileo (Europe), and BeiDou (China) are satellite navigation constellations that enable sub-metre positioning for transportation, agriculture, mapping, and emergency services.
- **Earth observation:** Remote-sensing satellites monitor land use, deforestation, ocean colour, sea surface temperature, polar ice extent, and agricultural yields.
- **Scientific research:** Space telescopes (e.g., Hubble, James Webb) and heliophysics satellites (e.g., SOHO, ACE) study the universe and the Sun–Earth environment. The ISS serves as a platform for microgravity research.
- **Military and reconnaissance:** Surveillance, signals intelligence, and missile early-warning satellites have played strategic roles since the earliest days of the Space Age.

### Space Debris and Kessler Syndrome

#### The Debris Problem
As of 2024, Earth's orbital environment contains far more objects than active satellites. According to the European Space Agency (ESA):

- ~**54,000** objects larger than 10 cm in orbit (including ~9,300 active payloads)
- ~**1.2 million** debris objects between 1 cm and 10 cm
- ~**140 million** objects between 1 mm and 1 cm

Only objects larger than ~10 cm are routinely tracked by ground-based surveillance networks. Even a 1 cm fragment travelling at orbital speeds (~7–8 km/s in LEO) carries enough kinetic energy to damage a spacecraft.

[Source: ESA Space Environment Report 2025; https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_Debris/ESA_Space_Environment_Report_2025]

#### Notable Collision Events
In February 2009, the operational **Iridium 33** communications satellite collided with the defunct Russian **Cosmos 2251** at ~789 km altitude and a closing speed of approximately 42,120 km/h, creating thousands of debris fragments. In 2007, China deliberately destroyed its own Fengyun-1C weather satellite in an anti-satellite (ASAT) test, creating more than 3,000 tracked fragments — the largest single debris-generating event in history. A 2024 fragmentation of a Chinese Long March 6A rocket stage generated at least 700 additional tracked fragments.

[Source: Wikipedia — Kessler syndrome; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kessler_syndrome]
[Source: ESA Space Environment Report 2025; https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_Debris/ESA_Space_Environment_Report_2025]

#### Kessler Syndrome
In 1978, NASA scientists **Donald J. Kessler** and **Burton G. Cour-Palais** published a seminal paper describing a cascading collision scenario, later termed the **Kessler syndrome**: if the density of objects in LEO exceeds a critical threshold, each collision generates more debris, which triggers further collisions, in a self-sustaining chain reaction. The result could be an orbital debris belt that renders certain altitudes permanently unusable for satellites.

As of 2025, ESA's debris modelling indicates that at approximately **550 km altitude**, the density of tracked debris objects is now comparable to the density of active satellites — a warning sign that this critical regime may already be approaching.

[Source: Kessler & Cour-Palais 1978 — Collision Frequency of Artificial Satellites; via Wikipedia — Kessler syndrome; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kessler_syndrome]
[Source: ESA Space Environment Report 2025; https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_Debris/ESA_Space_Environment_Report_2025]

Mitigation measures include designing satellites to de-orbit within 25 years of end of life, active debris-removal missions (under development by ESA's ClearSpace-1 and others), and improved collision avoidance manoeuvres by satellite operators.
