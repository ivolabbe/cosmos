## Definition

A lunar calendar is a dating system whose months track the Moon's phases — that is, each month corresponds to one complete cycle of the Moon's phases (a synodic month or lunation) [Britannica: lunar-calendar]. Lunar calendars are among the oldest timekeeping systems in human history, predating solar and lunisolar calendars.

---

## The Synodic Month

The fundamental unit of a lunar calendar is the **synodic month** (from Greek *synodos*, meaning "meeting" or "conjunction"), defined as the time between successive new moons as seen from Earth. Its mean duration is **29.530588 days** (29 days, 12 hours, 44 minutes, 3 seconds) [Britannica: synodic-month; Wikipedia: Lunar_month].

The synodic month is longer than the Moon's true orbital period (the sidereal month, ~27.3 days) because Earth is simultaneously orbiting the Sun: after the Moon completes one orbit, it must travel an extra ~2.2 days to "catch up" to the same Sun–Earth–Moon geometry [Wikipedia: Lunar_month].

Because 12 synodic months sum to only ~354.37 days — roughly **10.88 days shorter** than a solar (tropical) year of 365.24 days — a purely lunar year drifts through all seasons over ~33 years [USNO: calendars; Wikipedia: Islamic_calendar].

---

## Pure Lunar vs. Lunisolar Calendars

**Pure lunar calendars** make no attempt to align months with the solar year. Their months drift backward through the seasons at ~11 days per year, completing a full seasonal regression in 33–34 years. The **Islamic (Hijri) calendar** is the primary modern example [Wikipedia: Lunar_calendar].

**Lunisolar calendars** add intercalary (leap) months periodically to keep months broadly aligned with the seasons. Examples include the Hebrew, Chinese, Vietnamese, Hindu, Korean, and Thai calendars [Wikipedia: Lunisolar_calendar; USNO: calendars].

---

## Key Examples

### Islamic (Hijri) Calendar
The Hijri calendar is the most widely used pure lunar calendar. It has 12 months totalling 354 or 355 days per year. Month beginnings are traditionally determined by observation of the first visible lunar crescent (*hilal*) at sunset [Wikipedia: Islamic_calendar]. The calendar's epoch is **622 CE**, marking the Hijrah (Muhammad's migration from Mecca to Medina); the year numbering system was formally introduced by Caliph Umar around **638 CE** [Wikipedia: Islamic_calendar]. Because it is purely lunar, Ramadan and other Islamic observances cycle through all seasons over ~33 years [USNO: calendars].

### Hebrew Calendar
The Hebrew calendar is lunisolar, formally codified by Hillel II around **359 CE** [USNO: calendars]. It is based on calculation rather than observation. It employs the **Metonic cycle** (see below): a 19-year cycle containing 12 common years of 12 months and 7 leap years of 13 months. The intercalary month (Adar Aleph) is inserted so that Passover always falls in spring [Wikipedia: Lunisolar_calendar; UniversalTimeDate].

### Chinese Calendar
The Chinese lunisolar calendar is based on precise astronomical calculations of Sun and Moon positions [USNO: calendars]. A leap month is added every 2–3 years, governed by the system of 24 solar terms. The calendar has been central to Chinese agricultural and ceremonial life for millennia [Wikipedia: Lunisolar_calendar; UniversalTimeDate].

---

## Intercalation

Intercalation is the insertion of extra days or months to keep a calendar synchronized with the astronomical cycle of choice (seasons or lunar phases). For lunisolar calendars, the most common approach adds a 13th month every 2–3 years [Wikipedia: Lunar_calendar]. Without intercalation, a lunar year falls ~10.88 days short of a solar year, and festivals tied to agricultural seasons would drift unacceptably.

The Babylonians were probably the first to systematize intercalation: before ~380 BCE, decisions were made ad hoc by royal astrologers; after that date, fixed rules were established [Britannica: lunar-calendar].

---

## The Metonic Cycle

The **Metonic cycle** is the observation that **235 synodic months = 19 solar years** (to within ~2 hours). After 19 years, the Moon's phases recur on the same calendar dates, making the Metonic period a powerful tool for intercalation [Wikipedia: Metonic_cycle; USNO: calendars].

The cycle is named after **Meton of Athens** (5th century BCE), who recognized it and counted 6,940 days in the period [Wikipedia: Metonic_cycle]. The underlying arithmetic had already been used by Babylonian astronomers from the late 6th century BCE [Wikipedia: Metonic_cycle]. In a Metonic lunisolar structure, **12 years have 12 lunar months** and **7 years have 13 lunar months**, giving the correct long-run average.

The Metonic cycle underlies the Hebrew calendar and historically governed Easter computation in Christian liturgy. It is off by ~2 hours, 5 minutes, 16 seconds per 19-year cycle — negligible for most practical purposes [Wikipedia: Metonic_cycle].

---

## Historical Importance

Lunar calendars are the earliest known systematic timekeeping devices. Key evidence:

- **Abri Blanchard bone** (France, ~28,000 years ago): a bone shard with a pattern of pits interpreted by Alexander Marshack as a record of lunar phase cycles — possibly the earliest known lunar tally [NASA SSERVI; Wikipedia: Lunar_calendar].
- **Venus of Laussel** (Dordogne, France, ~22,000–27,000 years ago): a carved figure holding a bison horn with 13 notches, possibly representing 13 lunations in a year [NASA SSERVI].
- **Mesolithic pit alignment, Aberdeenshire, Scotland** (~8,000 BCE): a 50-metre arc of pits shaped to depict waxing, waning, crescent, gibbous, and full moon phases — described as the world's oldest known formal lunar calendar monument [National Geographic; Science News].
- **Engraved pebble, Velletri, Alban Hills, Italy** (~10,000 years ago): three sets of 7, 9–10, and 11 notches totalling 27–28, consistent with a lunar cycle record [Sapienza Università di Roma].

The Sumero-Babylonian calendar, the first historically documented lunar calendar, began each month on the first day of the new crescent Moon's visibility [Britannica: lunar-calendar].

## Comparison with the Solar Calendar

| Feature | Pure Lunar | Lunisolar | Solar (Gregorian) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Month basis | Synodic month (~29.5 d) | Synodic month | Fixed month (28–31 d) |
| Year length | ~354 days | ~365 days (with leap months) | ~365.25 days |
| Seasonal drift | Yes (~11 d/yr) | Minimal | None |
| Examples | Hijri | Hebrew, Chinese, Hindu | Gregorian, Julian |

Solar calendars abandon lunar months entirely, keeping months as administrative units of fixed length to track the seasons. The Gregorian calendar, now the international civil standard, is purely solar [Britannica: lunar-calendar].
