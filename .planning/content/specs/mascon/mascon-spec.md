## Definition
A mascon (mass concentration) is a region of a planet's or moon's crust containing a large positive gravitational anomaly, typically associated with large circular impact basins where dense mare basaltic lava flooded the depression or where the crust-mantle boundary was uplifted by the ancient impact. Unlike ordinary topographic depressions, which produce negative gravitational anomalies as isostasy would predict, mascons produce anomalies that are strongly positive — a sign they are "super-isostatic," meaning they are held above their equilibrium positions by the rigid lithosphere.

## Full Content

### Definition and basic concept
- A mascon is a region of a planet or moon's crust with a large positive gravity anomaly [Wikipedia]
- The word "mascon" is a contraction of "mass concentration" [Britannica]
- Unlike a typical topographic depression (which would exhibit a slight negative gravity anomaly under isostasy), a mascon shows a positive anomaly — implying excess mass [Wikipedia]
- All known lunar mascons are "super-isostatic": they are held above their isostatic equilibrium positions by the stiffness of the lunar lithosphere [Wikipedia]
- The Moon is described as "the most gravitationally 'lumpy' major body known in the Solar System" [Wikipedia]

### Physical mechanism of lunar mascon formation
- Mascons form as a consequence of large asteroid impacts during the Late Heavy Bombardment (~4 billion years ago), when the Moon's interior was much hotter and its crust was thinner than today [Wikipedia; Melosh et al. 2013, Science]
- Two mechanisms jointly produce the positive gravity anomaly:
  1. **Dense mare basaltic lavas** that flooded the impact basin, up to 6 km thick [Wikipedia]
  2. **Uplift of the crust-mantle boundary** (mantle rebound), bringing denser mantle material closer to the surface [Wikipedia; Britannica]
- Not all mascon basins are associated with volcanic activity: some show no mare volcanism yet still exhibit positive anomalies, implying mantle rebound alone can create a mascon [Wikipedia]
- Notably, Oceanus Procellarum — the Moon's largest mare volcanic region — has no associated positive gravity anomaly, demonstrating that lava alone is insufficient [Wikipedia]
- The GRAIL-confirmed gravity signature of a mascon basin follows a characteristic ring pattern: central gravity surplus (bulls-eye), surrounded by a ring of gravity deficit, surrounded by an outer ring of surplus [Melosh et al. 2013; ScienceDaily 2013]

### Stability and survival over geological time
- Mascons have persisted for over 3 billion years, indicating the Moon's crust is thick and rigid (the lithosphere has been "frozen" since shortly after the impacts) and the internal heat source has been extinct for a long time [Britannica]

### Orbital effects — spacecraft and satellites
- Mascons perturb the local gravitational field sufficiently that low-altitude, uncorrected lunar orbits are unstable on timescales of months to years [Wikipedia]
- NASA 2006 summary: "Lunar mascons make most low lunar orbits unstable ... the mascons pull it forward, back, left, right, or down" [Wikipedia/Frozen orbit article]
- Apollo 16 subsatellite PFS-2: expected to orbit for 1.5 years but crashed after only 35 days because it was deployed in a lower orbit than planned [Wikipedia]
- Only four "frozen orbit" inclination zones allow indefinitely stable low orbits around the Moon: 27°, 50°, 76°, and 86° [Wikipedia/Frozen orbit article, discovered 2001]
- Apollo 15 subsatellite PFS-1 operated near 28° inclination, placing it close to the stable zone [Wikipedia/Frozen orbit article]

### Impact on Apollo missions and navigation
- Before mascons were understood, positional errors in Lunar Orbiter tracking data exceeded mission specifications by a factor of 100 (100 km² instead of required 1 km² landing zones) [Wikipedia]
- NASA formed a dedicated "tiger team" to resolve the discrepancy [Wikipedia]
- William Wollenhaupt and Emil Schiesser (NASA Manned Spacecraft Center, Houston) developed correction methodology [Wikipedia]
- Applied first for Apollo 12: landed within 163 m of the pre-landed Surveyor 3 spacecraft — previously errors would have been on the order of kilometres [Wikipedia]

### Earth mascons
- Earth also has mascons, detected by satellite gravimetry via the GRACE satellite mission [Wikipedia]
- Earth mascons are reported in units of "equivalent water thickness" (surface mass density redistribution divided by water density) [Wikipedia]
- A prominent example: the Hawaiian island chain creates a positive gravity anomaly [Wikipedia]

### Mars mascons
- Three confirmed mascon basins on Mars: Argyre Planitia, Isidis Planitia, and Utopia Planitia [Wikipedia]
- Same pattern as lunar mascons: topographic depressions associated with positive gravitational anomalies [Wikipedia]

### Mercury mascons
- Mascons mapped on Mercury by the MESSENGER spacecraft (2011–2015) [Wikipedia]
- Two examples: Caloris Planitia and Sobkou Planitia [Wikipedia]

### Mapping missions
- **Lunar Orbiter program (1966–1967)**: All five spacecraft contributed Doppler tracking data; led to mascon discovery by Muller & Sjogren [Wikipedia]
- **Luna 10 (1966)**: First artificial lunar orbiter; returned the first evidence of gravitational perturbations now attributed to mascons (460 lunar orbits) [Wikipedia/Luna 10]
- **GRAIL mission (2011–2012)**: Twin spacecraft (Ebb and Flow), periselene 25 km, able to detect 1-micrometre changes in spacecraft separation; produced the highest-resolution gravity map of any celestial body; identified 74 circular impact basins; resolved the origin of mascons definitively [Wikipedia/GRAIL; NASA JPL]
- **Chang'e 5T1 (China)**: Also contributed to mascon mapping [Wikipedia]
- **MESSENGER (2011–2015)**: Mapped Mercury mascons [Wikipedia]
- **GRACE satellites**: Map Earth mascons continuously [Wikipedia]

## Historical Context

- **April 1966**: Luna 10 becomes the first artificial lunar orbiter; its Doppler tracking data provide the first evidence of unexpected gravitational perturbations over the lunar surface [Wikipedia/Luna 10]
- **1966–1967**: NASA's five Lunar Orbiter spacecraft collect precise Doppler tracking data enabling the first detailed lunar gravity mapping [Wikipedia]
- **August 16, 1968**: Paul M. Muller and William L. Sjogren of NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory publish "Mascons: Lunar Mass Concentrations" in *Science*, vol. 161, issue 3842, pp. 680–684 (DOI: 10.1126/science.161.3842.680). They identified consistent 1:1 correlation between large positive gravity anomalies and depressed circular basins [Muller & Sjogren 1968; PubMed 17801458]
- **Discovery method**: Doppler-shifted radio signals tracked the orbiters; spacecraft dipped and accelerated over mascons as the stronger gravity pulled them closer and then released them [Britannica]
- **Apollo era (1969–1972)**: Mascons posed a navigation crisis for Apollo landers. The "tiger team" correction (Wollenhaupt & Schiesser) enabled Apollo 12 to land within 163 m of target [Wikipedia]
- **2001**: Four stable "frozen orbit" inclinations identified, decades after mascon discovery [Wikipedia/Frozen orbit]
- **May 2013**: NASA publishes GRAIL findings resolving the origin of mascons; Melosh et al. (2013, *Science*) confirm the Late Heavy Bombardment impact + mantle rebound model; 74 basins catalogued [Wikipedia/GRAIL; ScienceDaily 2013]
- **Etymology**: "Mascon" is a portmanteau of "mass concentration," coined by Muller and Sjogren in their 1968 paper [Britannica]

## Sub-types / Classifications

Mascons are generally classified by the body on which they occur and by the mechanism producing the anomaly:

1. **Volcanic mascons** — positive anomaly primarily from dense mare basaltic lava infill (e.g., Mare Imbrium, Mare Serenitatis). The lava column reaches up to 6 km thick on the Moon.
2. **Rebound mascons** — positive anomaly primarily from uplift of the denser crust-mantle boundary following impact, without significant volcanic infill (some lunar mascons show no associated volcanism).
3. **Combined mascons** — both mechanisms contribute; most major lunar mascons fall in this category.

All lunar mascons are characterized as **super-isostatic** (held above their isostatic equilibrium by the frozen lithosphere), distinguishing them from simply "dense regions" that might be in gravitational equilibrium.

On Earth, mascons refer to any region of excess surface mass detected by satellite gravimetry and are often associated with post-glacial rebound, ocean mass redistribution, or crustal density variations rather than impact processes.
