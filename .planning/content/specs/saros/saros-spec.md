# Saros — Article Spec

## Definition
The saros is an astronomical period of approximately 6,585.3 days (18 years, 11 days, and 8 hours) after which the Sun, Earth, and Moon return to nearly the same relative geometry, causing a nearly identical eclipse to recur. Because this interval coincides with very nearly integer multiples of the synodic, draconic, and anomalistic lunar months, it has served as the principal tool for eclipse prediction from antiquity to the present.

## Full Content

- The saros equals 6,585.3211 days (~18 years, 11 days, 8 hours), during which the Sun, Earth, and Moon return to nearly identical relative positions, reproducing a nearly identical eclipse [NASA GSFC / Wikipedia]
- The cycle emerges from the near-simultaneous commensurability of three lunar periods: 223 synodic months (6,585.3223 d), 242 draconic months (6,585.3575 d), and 239 anomalistic months (6,585.5375 d) [NASA GSFC]
- The synodic month governs lunar phase — the Moon must be new (solar eclipse) or full (lunar eclipse) for an eclipse to occur [Astronomical League]
- The draconic month governs nodal position — the Moon must be near an ascending or descending node (where its orbit crosses the ecliptic) for the Sun, Earth, and Moon to align closely enough for an eclipse [Astronomical League]
- The anomalistic month governs the Moon's distance from Earth — its value determines the apparent angular size of the Moon and thus whether a solar eclipse is total, annular, or hybrid [NASA GSFC]
- Because all three periodicities nearly coincide within one saros, the Moon returns to the same phase, the same node, and approximately the same orbital distance after each saros, recreating nearly the same eclipse type and duration [NASA GSFC]
- The critical imperfection: the saros period contains a fractional day of ~8 hours (~1/3 day), so each successive eclipse in a series occurs approximately 120° westward in longitude (Earth rotates an additional ~8 hours between events) [NASA GSFC]
- Successive eclipses within a saros series also drift slowly in latitude: a series begins with partial eclipses near one polar region, progresses through decades of central eclipses (total, annular, or hybrid) across mid-latitudes, and finally ends with partial eclipses near the opposite pole [NASA GSFC / Britannica]
- A complete saros series spans 1,226 to 1,550 years and contains 69 to 87 eclipses, of which approximately 40 to 60 are central [NASA GSFC / Britannica]
- Solar saros series currently active are numbered approximately 117–156 (~40 series); lunar saros series currently active are numbered approximately 110–150 (~41 series) [Wikipedia]
- As one saros series ends, another begins: the system maintains a nearly constant number of active eclipse families at any given time [Britannica]
- The exeligmos is a triple saros (~54 years, 34 days; 19,755.96 days): after three saros periods, the eclipse returns to approximately the same longitude and same local time of day, restoring geographic visibility [Wikipedia]
- The inex (358 synodic months ≈ 29 years minus 20 days; 10,571.95 days) is a complementary cycle that links eclipses across different saros series at the same node; together, saros and inex can map all solar eclipses in a grid called the Saros–Inex Panorama [NASA GSFC]
- Eclipse prediction using the saros requires no understanding of gravitational mechanics — ancient astronomers could identify the cycle purely from systematic observations of eclipse recurrence [Astronomical League]
- The saros applies equally to solar and lunar eclipses; for lunar eclipses, visibility is less longitude-restricted (any location on the night-side of Earth can observe the event), so geographic displacement affects series visibility less dramatically [Wikipedia]

## Historical Context

- Ancient Babylonian (Chaldean) astronomers identified the saros empirically in the last several centuries BCE, recognising the ~18-year repetition of eclipses from systematic tabulated observations; Chinese astronomers may have independently identified the cycle before 575 BCE [Astronomical League / Wikipedia]
- The saros was encoded in the Antikythera Mechanism (c. 150–100 BCE), an ancient Greek analogue computing device whose gearing incorporated 223 lunar months (inscribed in Greek numerals ΣΚΓ′) to predict eclipse occurrences; this demonstrates that the cycle was well understood in the Hellenistic world [Wikipedia]
- The term "saros" was applied to the eclipse cycle by English astronomer Edmond Halley in 1686, drawing on the Suda — an 11th-century Byzantine encyclopaedia — which defined "saros" as "a measure and a number among Chaldeans" [Wikipedia]
- The etymology of "saros" is disputed: it may derive from the Babylonian *sāru* (meaning "3600") or from the Greek *saro* (σαρῶ, meaning "to sweep"), as in sweeping the sky with successive eclipses; the Babylonian *sāru* actually referred to a period of 3,600 years, not the eclipse cycle, making Halley's usage a partial misnomer now universally accepted [Wikipedia]
- Halley used the saros to successfully predict the total solar eclipse of 3 May 1715 visible from London, accurate to within 4 minutes and a distance of about 30 km (19 miles) — a landmark demonstration of the cycle's predictive power [Astronomical League]
- The discovery of the cycle exemplifies how systematic empirical record-keeping, rather than theoretical physics, can reveal celestial periodicities; the Babylonians discovered the saros centuries before any understanding of gravitation [Astronomical League]

## Sub-types / Classifications

- **Solar saros series** — tracks recurrence of solar eclipses sharing a common node and geometric origin; each is assigned a series number (~117–156 currently active). Within a series, eclipses evolve from polar partial → mid-latitude central → opposite-pole partial over ~1,200–1,550 years.
- **Lunar saros series** — tracks recurrence of lunar eclipses; currently active series ~110–150. Less geographically restricted than solar series because lunar eclipses are visible from any point on Earth's night side.
- **Central vs. partial members** — early and late members of a saros series are partial eclipses; intermediate members are central (total, annular, or hybrid for solar; total for lunar).
- **Exeligmos** — a triple saros (~54 years, 34 days); restores geographic and local-time similarity of eclipses, since three 8-hour shifts ≈ 24 hours (one full Earth rotation).
- **Inex** — a complementary eclipse cycle of 358 synodic months (~29 years); the inex shifts eclipse prediction to a different saros series at the same node. Combined with the saros, all solar eclipses can be mapped in a Saros–Inex Panorama grid.
