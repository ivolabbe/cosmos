## Definition
Absolute space is Isaac Newton's concept of a fixed, immovable three-dimensional framework that exists independently of all matter and energy, providing a universal reference against which true motion can be measured. The concept was rejected by Einstein's theory of relativity, which replaced it with spacetime whose geometry is shaped by matter and energy.

## Full Content

- Newton introduced absolute space in his *Principia Mathematica* (1687), defining it as space that "remains always similar and immovable without relation to anything external" — an independent backdrop for all physical events [Wikipedia: Absolute space and time; NED/IPAC Glossary]
- Newton distinguished absolute space from **relative space**: relative space is the measurable position of objects with respect to each other, while absolute space is the underlying fixed framework [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Newton's Views on Space, Time, and Motion]
- The concept arose from a physical necessity within Newton's mechanics: his laws of motion — especially the law of inertia — require a preferred reference frame to define states of uniform motion and rest [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy]
- **Newton's bucket argument**: Newton described a spinning bucket of water. Initially the water surface is flat despite the bucket rotating around it; as the water picks up rotation and co-rotates with the bucket, the surface becomes concave (parabolic). Newton argued the concavity proves the water rotates relative to absolute space, not relative to the bucket — since the relative rotation between water and bucket is zero when the concavity is greatest [Wikipedia: Bucket argument]
- **Revolving spheres argument**: Two spheres connected by a cord and rotating about their common centre will exhibit cord tension regardless of any surrounding reference bodies. Newton used this to argue that rotation can be detected in completely empty space — implying absolute space as the reference [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy]
- Absolute space was associated with the **luminiferous aether**: the 19th-century hypothesis that light waves require a medium to propagate through, which would also serve as the physical carrier of Newton's absolute frame [NED/IPAC Glossary]
- The **Michelson–Morley experiment** (1887) attempted to detect Earth's motion through the aether by measuring differences in the speed of light in perpendicular directions. The null result — a measured fringe shift of ≤ 0.02 fringes against an expected 0.40 — dealt a critical blow to aether theory and the notion of an absolute reference frame [Wikipedia: Michelson–Morley experiment]
- **Einstein's special relativity** (1905) eliminated the need for absolute space by postulating that the speed of light is constant in all inertial frames. There is no privileged rest frame; motion is purely relative between observers [Universe Today; Wikipedia: Absolute space and time]
- **General relativity** (1915) went further: spacetime geometry is determined by the distribution of matter and energy, meaning space itself is dynamic, not fixed [Wikipedia: Absolute space and time; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Absolute and Relational Space]
- The philosophical debate between **absolutism** (Newton) and **relationalism** (Leibniz, Mach) remains active in philosophy of physics: absolutists hold that space/time are real entities independent of matter; relationalists hold that spatial and temporal facts are entirely constituted by relations among material bodies [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Absolute and Relational Space and Motion]
- **Mach's principle** (formulated by Ernst Mach, ~1883): inertial effects such as centrifugal force arise not from rotation relative to absolute space but from rotation relative to the mass distribution of the entire universe. Einstein acknowledged Mach's principle as an inspiration for general relativity [Wikipedia: Absolute space and time]
- Modern physics uses **inertial frames of reference** rather than absolute space: a preferred class of reference frames in which Newton's laws hold and objects in uniform motion remain so, without positing a fixed background space [Universe Today]

## Historical Context

- **Aristotle** (384–322 BCE): early notion that the heavens define an absolute background; the concept of a "place" for each object implies a cosmic reference structure [Wikipedia: Absolute space and time]
- **Copernicus** (*De Revolutionibus*, 1543): reference to an immobile stellar sphere gestured toward proto-absolute-space thinking [Wikipedia: Absolute space and time]
- **René Descartes** (1644, *Principia Philosophiae*): denied empty space; held that motion is defined purely relative to neighbouring bodies — an early relational position that Newton explicitly opposed [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy]
- **Isaac Newton** (1687, *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, the "Scholium"): formally defined absolute space and absolute time as the conceptual foundations of his mechanics. Newton was aware these were theoretical posits, not directly observable [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy; NED/IPAC Glossary]
- **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** (1715–1716, Leibniz–Clarke correspondence, conducted via Samuel Clarke as Newton's proxy): argued that absolute space is metaphysically incoherent — if the entire universe were shifted through absolute space, no observable consequence would follow, violating the principle of sufficient reason [Wikipedia: Leibniz–Clarke correspondence]
- **George Berkeley** (1710, *Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge*): argued that isolated objects cannot meaningfully be said to rotate without reference to surrounding bodies [Wikipedia: Absolute space and time]
- **Ernst Mach** (1883, *The Science of Mechanics*): mounted the most sustained attack on absolute space; reinterpreted Newton's bucket experiment relationally; Mach's critique became Mach's principle [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy]
- **Albert Michelson and Edward Morley** (1887): their null result for aether-drift provided the key experimental foundation for rejecting absolute space [Wikipedia: Michelson–Morley experiment]
- **Albert Einstein** (1905, special relativity; 1915, general relativity): formally replaced absolute space and time with the unified, dynamic spacetime framework [Wikipedia: Absolute space and time]
- The term "absolute space" comes from Newton's Latin *spatium absolutum*, translating to "absolute space" in English editions of the *Principia* [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy]

## Sub-types / Classifications

Absolute space is a single concept, but the debate divides into two broad positions:

- **Substantivalism (absolutism)**: space exists as a real, independently existing entity — a "container" that would exist even if entirely empty. Newton held this view. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy]
- **Relationalism**: space has no existence beyond the spatial relations among material bodies. Leibniz and Mach held this view. General relativity is sometimes interpreted as relational, though the debate continues. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy]

Modern physics uses a middle path:
- **Inertial frames**: a preferred class of reference frames in which Newton's first law holds, replacing the need for a single absolute space while retaining a notion of privileged motion states [Universe Today]
