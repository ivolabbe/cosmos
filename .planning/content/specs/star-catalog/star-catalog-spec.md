## Definition
A star catalog is a systematic list of stars recording their positions in the sky and other measured properties such as brightness, spectral type, parallax, and proper motion. Catalogs allow astronomers to identify, reference, and study individual stars consistently across observations, time, and different instruments.

## Full Content

### What a Star Catalog Is
- A star catalog is an astronomical database listing stars by their sky position (right ascension and declination) along with measured properties [Wikipedia: Star catalogue]
- Properties recorded can include apparent magnitude, absolute magnitude, spectral type, proper motion, parallax (and hence distance), radial velocity, color indices, and variability classification [Wikipedia: Star catalogue]
- Completeness is described by the limiting magnitude — the faintest stars included — with deeper surveys requiring more observing time or larger telescopes [Wikipedia: Star catalogue]
- Most modern catalogs are available in electronic form and freely downloadable from space agencies and data centers such as VizieR (CDS, Strasbourg) and SIMBAD [Wikipedia: Star catalogue]

### How Stars Are Designated
- Johann Bayer (1603, *Uranometria*) introduced Greek letter designations for stars within each constellation, still in daily use (e.g., Alpha Centauri, Beta Persei) [Wikipedia: Star catalogue]
- John Flamsteed (1725, *Historia coelestis Britannica*) numbered stars within constellations from west to east; Flamsteed numbers such as 61 Cygni remain standard references [Wikipedia: Star catalogue]
- Modern catalogs use prefixes tied to catalog name: HD (Henry Draper), SAO (Smithsonian Astrophysical Observatory), HIP (Hipparcos), GJ (Gliese-Jahreiß for nearby stars) [Wikipedia: Star catalogue]
- A star can appear in dozens of catalogs under different designations; SIMBAD links these cross-identifications [Wikipedia: Star catalogue]

### Types of Star Catalogs
- **Astrometric catalogs**: specialize in precise positions and proper motions; example: Hipparcos Catalogue, USNO-B1.0 [Wikipedia: Star catalogue]
- **Photometric catalogs**: record stellar brightness in multiple color bands (Johnson B, V; 2MASS J, H, K); used to determine stellar temperatures and luminosities [Wikipedia: Star catalogue]
- **Spectroscopic catalogs**: classify stellar spectra; the Henry Draper Catalogue established the Harvard classification scheme (O, B, A, F, G, K, M) still in use today [Wikipedia: Henry Draper Catalogue]
- **Proper motion catalogs**: focus on high-velocity stars (Luyten, Ross, Wolf series) — useful for identifying nearby stars [Wikipedia: Star catalogue]
- **Specialty catalogs**: Gliese-Jahreiß nearby stars; Struve double star catalog (17,180 objects); carbon star catalog (7,000+ stars) [Wikipedia: Star catalogue]
- **All-sky survey catalogs**: Bonner Durchmusterung, Hipparcos, Gaia — attempt complete coverage to a limiting magnitude across the whole sky [Wikipedia: Star catalogue]

### Space-Based Revolution
- Ground-based astrometry is limited by atmospheric seeing; ESA's Hipparcos satellite (launched 1989) provided the first practical all-sky absolute parallax measurements from space [Wikipedia: Hipparcos catalogue]
- Hipparcos yielded distances accurate to better than 10% for ~20,000 stars within ~1,000 light-years, transforming the stellar distance scale [Wikipedia: Hipparcos catalogue]
- ESA's Gaia mission (launched 2013, science operations ended March 2025) is the definitive modern star catalog: Gaia DR3 (2022) contains 1.812 billion sources — the largest and most precise stellar census ever compiled [ESA Gaia; WebSearch Gaia DR3]
- Gaia provided six-dimensional phase-space data (3D positions, 3D velocities) for hundreds of millions of stars, enabling a full kinematic map of the Milky Way [ESA Gaia DR2 page]
- Gaia also cataloged 14,000+ Solar System asteroids and anchored the celestial reference frame using 1.6 million extragalactic sources [Wikipedia: Gaia catalogues]

## Historical Context

- **~1500 BCE**: Babylonian scholars compiled the earliest known star lists on clay tablets, naming 36 stars organized by celestial region [Wikipedia: Star catalogue]
- **129 BCE**: Greek astronomer Hipparchus compiled the first systematic attempt to map the entire sky with ~850 stellar positions; this also led to his discovery of the precession of the equinoxes [Britannica; Wikipedia]
- **~140 CE**: Claudius Ptolemy incorporated and extended Hipparchus's work in the *Almagest*, cataloguing 1,022 stars grouped into 48 constellations; this catalog remained the Western standard for over 800 years [Wikipedia: Star catalogue]
- **964**: Persian astronomer Abd al-Rahman al-Sufi published *Book of Fixed Stars*, revising Ptolemy's positions and magnitudes with original observations [Wikipedia: Star catalogue]
- **1437**: Ulugh Beg completed *Zij-i Sultani* at Samarkand, the last major pre-telescopic catalog, introduced to Europe by 1665 [Britannica]
- **1598**: Tycho Brahe completed his ~1,000-star catalog to unprecedented naked-eye precision, finally superseding Ptolemy [Wikipedia: Star catalogue]
- **1603**: Johann Bayer published *Uranometria* introducing Greek-letter stellar designations still in everyday use [Wikipedia: Star catalogue]
- **1725**: John Flamsteed's *Historia coelestis Britannica* introduced numbered designations and set the template for modern numbering systems [Britannica]
- **1859–1863**: Friedrich Argelander's *Bonner Durchmusterung* catalogued 342,198 stars — the first systematic survey of comparable scale to modern catalogs — laying groundwork for 20th-century photographic surveys [Wikipedia: Bonner Durchmusterung]
- **1918–1924**: Annie Jump Cannon and colleagues at Harvard published the Henry Draper Catalogue, classifying 225,300 stellar spectra and establishing the Harvard spectral classification (OBAFGKM) used universally today [Wikipedia: Henry Draper Catalogue]
- **1989–1997**: ESA's Hipparcos satellite operated, producing the first high-precision space-based astrometric catalog (published 1997) [Wikipedia: Hipparcos catalogue]
- **2013–2025**: ESA's Gaia mission surveyed the sky, producing its landmark Gaia DR3 catalog in 2022 with 1.812 billion sources [Wikipedia: Gaia catalogues]

## Sub-types / Classifications

Star catalogs are classified by their primary measured quantity and scope:

| Type | What It Records | Key Example |
|------|----------------|-------------|
| Astrometric | Positions, proper motions, parallaxes | Hipparcos, USNO-B1.0, Gaia |
| Photometric | Stellar brightness in color bands | Tycho-2, 2MASS, Guide Star Catalog |
| Spectroscopic | Spectral type, radial velocity | Henry Draper Catalogue, Gaia RVS |
| Proper motion | High-velocity stars | Luyten Half-Second (LHS), Ross Catalog |
| Proximity | Nearby stars within fixed distance | Gliese-Jahreiß, GCNS (100 pc) |
| Double/multiple | Binary and multiple star systems | Struve Catalog, Washington Double Star |
| Variable | Stars with time-variable brightness | GCVS (General Catalog of Variable Stars) |
| All-sky survey | Full-sky coverage to limiting mag | Bonner Durchmusterung, Gaia DR3 |
