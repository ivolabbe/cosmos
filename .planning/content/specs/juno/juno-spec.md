# Juno (spacecraft) — Article Spec

## Category
solar-system

## Definition
Juno is a NASA spacecraft in polar orbit around Jupiter, studying the planet's origin, interior structure, atmospheric dynamics, and magnetic field. Launched in August 2011 and arriving at Jupiter in July 2016, it is the first solar-powered spacecraft to operate in the outer Solar System.

---

## Full Content

### Mission Overview

Juno is a NASA New Frontiers mission to Jupiter, built by Lockheed Martin and operated by the Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) in Pasadena, California. The mission's primary goal is to understand Jupiter's origin and evolution — key to understanding how the Solar System itself formed. Juno investigates whether Jupiter has a solid core, maps its intense magnetic field, measures water and ammonia abundance deep in the atmosphere, and observes the planet's auroras.

### Launch and Journey

Juno launched on **5 August 2011** from Cape Canaveral Air Force Station aboard an **Atlas V 551 rocket**. The spacecraft travelled approximately **1.7 billion miles (2.8 billion kilometres)** over a five-year journey before reaching Jupiter. To gain the velocity needed without carrying excessive fuel, Juno performed an **Earth gravity assist flyby on 9 October 2013**, passing within 559 km of Earth's surface — essentially using Earth as a slingshot.

### Arrival at Jupiter

Juno fired its main engine on **4 July 2016** in a critical 35-minute burn — the Jupiter Orbit Insertion (JOI) — slowing the spacecraft sufficiently to be captured by Jupiter's gravity. This manoeuvre placed Juno into a **53.5-day elliptical polar orbit**, with closest approach (perijove) bringing the spacecraft to within approximately **4,200 km of Jupiter's cloud tops** while the far end of the orbit extends to about **8.1 million kilometres** from the planet.

A planned engine burn to reduce the orbital period to 14 days was cancelled after engineers identified concerns with helium check valves in the fuel pressurisation system. Rather than risk the spacecraft, mission managers chose to conduct science from the longer 53.5-day orbit.

### Orbit Design

Juno's polar orbit was specifically designed to:

1. **Fly over all latitudes of Jupiter** from pole to pole, mapping the entire planet over many orbits.
2. **Avoid the most intense radiation belts**, which concentrate near Jupiter's equatorial plane. Jupiter's radiation environment is the most intense of any planet in the Solar System; to survive it, Juno's electronics are housed in a **1-centimetre-thick titanium radiation vault** that reduces radiation exposure by a factor of approximately 800.
3. **Exploit a radiation shadow** near Jupiter where particle flux is lower, during the brief window of closest approach.

The spacecraft rotates at **2 revolutions per minute** (a spin-stabilised design), which simplifies attitude control and ensures instruments scan across Jupiter during each perijove pass.

### Solar-Powered Design

Juno is notable as **the first mission to Jupiter to use solar panels rather than radioisotope thermoelectric generators (RTGs)**. Three solar panel wings, each approximately **8.9 metres (29.5 feet) long**, deploy to give a total solar panel area of roughly **50 square metres**. At Jupiter's distance from the Sun — about 5.2 astronomical units — sunlight is approximately 25 times weaker than at Earth. The solar panels generate approximately **486 watts** at Jupiter (compared to ~14 kilowatts at Earth). This design was adopted partly because supplies of plutonium-238 (the fuel for RTGs) were limited during the mission's development phase.

### Science Instruments

Juno carries nine science instruments:

**Microwave Radiometer (MWR)**
Six antennas passively detect microwave emissions from six depth levels in Jupiter's atmosphere, measuring the abundance of water and ammonia from the cloud tops down to pressures of approximately 1,000 atmospheres (roughly 550 km depth). This instrument produced the first direct measurements of Jupiter's deep atmospheric structure.

**Magnetometer (MAG)**
Two magnetometers mounted on one of the solar panels map Jupiter's magnetic field in three dimensions. Unlike the magnetometers on previous Jupiter missions, MAG also carries a star tracker to precisely measure the spacecraft's orientation, enabling the most accurate magnetic field maps ever produced for any planet.

**Gravity Science**
Juno's radio link with NASA's Deep Space Network (DSN) on Earth is used as a precision instrument. Tiny Doppler shifts in the radio signal reveal variations in Jupiter's gravitational field, probing the distribution of mass in the planet's interior.

**JunoCam**
A visible-light colour camera included primarily for public engagement and education. JunoCam takes images at each perijove pass; raw data are made freely available online for citizen scientists to process. JunoCam has also produced important scientific results, particularly of polar cloud structures that were not visible from earlier missions.

**Jovian Infrared Auroral Mapper (JIRAM)**
A near-infrared spectrometer and imager that maps auroral emissions and atmospheric composition. JIRAM provided the first detailed infrared images of Jupiter's polar regions and the cyclone clusters there.

**Ultraviolet Spectrograph (UVS)**
Records ultraviolet auroral emissions, characterising the energy and structure of Jupiter's powerful auroral zones.

**Jovian Auroral Distributions Experiment (JADE)**
Measures the energy and direction of low-energy electrons and ions (below 100 keV) in the Jovian magnetosphere.

**Jupiter Energetic-Particle Detector Instrument (JEDI)**
Measures higher-energy ions and electrons (above 100 keV), complementing JADE to characterise the full particle environment.

**Radio and Plasma Wave Science (Waves)**
Detects radio and plasma waves in Jupiter's magnetosphere and auroral regions, identifying the regions where auroral currents flow.

### Key Scientific Discoveries

#### Jupiter's Interior: A "Fuzzy" Core

One of Juno's most significant findings overturned the long-held idea that Jupiter has a compact, solid rocky core. Gravity science data revealed that Jupiter instead has a large, **diffuse or "fuzzy" core** composed of a dilute mixture of rocky material and metallic hydrogen, possibly extending to roughly half the planet's radius. This may be the remnant of a large primordial body that Jupiter engulfed early in its history, with the core material gradually mixing outward over billions of years.

**Source:** NASA Science — Juno Mission; https://science.nasa.gov/mission/juno/

#### Atmospheric Bands Penetrate Deep

The coloured bands visible on Jupiter's surface are not merely a cloud-top phenomenon. MWR measurements demonstrated that these bands and the associated jet streams extend **hundreds of kilometres below the cloud tops** — to depths of roughly 3,000 km (1,860 miles) in some analyses. Yet the interior is not evenly mixed; composition varies with depth, suggesting complex dynamics. Near the base of the water-cloud layer, the banded pattern inverts.

**Source:** NASA Science — Juno Mission; https://science.nasa.gov/mission/juno/; Wikipedia: Juno (spacecraft); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juno_(spacecraft)

#### Polar Cyclones

Juno returned the first detailed images of Jupiter's poles, revealing a dramatically different world from the equatorial bands. At the **north pole**, a central cyclone is surrounded by **eight circumpolar cyclones**, each roughly 4,000 km across. At the **south pole**, a central cyclone is encircled by **five circumpolar cyclones** arranged in a pentagonal pattern (a sixth was later observed joining the south polar cluster). These storm systems are stable features that have persisted throughout Juno's mission.

Multi-year tracking of the cyclones revealed they undergo **"beta drift"** — a slow pole-ward migration driven by the interaction of the Coriolis force with the cyclone's circular wind field. As cyclones approach one another they interact like springs in a mechanical system, bouncing off each other and preventing merging, which explains their long-term stability.

**Sources:**
- NASA Science — Juno Mission; https://science.nasa.gov/mission/juno/
- NASA Juno navigators enable Jupiter cyclone discovery; https://www.nasa.gov/missions/juno/nasas-juno-navigators-enable-jupiter-cyclone-discovery/

#### Magnetic Field: Intense and Secular Variation

Juno's magnetometer revealed that Jupiter's magnetic field is **far more intense and irregular than expected**. It is more than 30 times stronger than Earth's in some regions and shows significant asymmetry — it is stronger in the northern hemisphere than the southern. 

In a landmark 2019 result, Juno made **the first definitive detection of secular variation (temporal change) in a magnetic field beyond Earth**. The changes are driven by deep atmospheric winds that extend over 3,000 km into the planet, where they shear and carry magnetic field lines. Much of the secular variation is concentrated near the **Great Blue Spot** — an intense magnetic anomaly near Jupiter's equator — making it the dominant site of magnetic change on the planet.

**Sources:**
- NASA — Juno Finds Changes in Jupiter's Magnetic Field; https://www.nasa.gov/missions/juno/nasas-juno-finds-changes-in-jupiters-magnetic-field/
- Wikipedia: Juno (spacecraft); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juno_(spacecraft)

#### The Great Red Spot

During Perijove 7 on **10 July 2017**, Juno flew directly over the **Great Red Spot** at a distance of approximately 13,917 km from the cloud tops — the closest any spacecraft had passed. Gravity science subsequently constrained the depth of the Great Red Spot to approximately **500 km below the cloud tops**, resolving a long-standing question about whether the storm was a shallow surface feature or a deep structure.

**Sources:**
- Wikipedia: Juno (spacecraft); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juno_(spacecraft)
- NASA Science — Great Red Spot; https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/close-up-of-jupiters-great-red-spot/

#### Auroras and Lightning

Juno's UVS instrument and JADE/JEDI particle detectors revealed that Jupiter's auroras reach energies of **400,000 electron volts** — far exceeding Earth's auroral energies of a few thousand electron volts. Juno also revealed that Jupiter's lightning, unlike Earth's, is concentrated at the **poles rather than the equator**, driven by convection in the deep water-cloud layer near the poles where solar heating is weakest.

**Source:** NASA Science — Juno Mission; https://science.nasa.gov/mission/juno/

### Extended Mission and Moon Flybys

Juno's original primary mission was scheduled to conclude in **February 2018** after 37 science orbits, after which the spacecraft was to be deorbit-burned into Jupiter's atmosphere to comply with NASA planetary protection requirements (preventing biological contamination of Europa or other potentially habitable moons).

NASA approved multiple mission extensions. In the extended mission Juno executed close flybys of Jupiter's large moons:

- **Ganymede** — 7 June 2021: closest approach since the Galileo spacecraft in 2000 (at about 1,038 km). The flyby compressed Juno's orbital period from 53 to 43 days.
- **Europa** — 29 September 2022: flyby at 352 km altitude, returning the highest-resolution images of Europa's surface since Galileo. The flyby further reduced the orbital period to 38 days.
- **Io** — December 2023 and February 2024: two close passes revealing active volcanism and detecting the most energetic eruption in Io's observed history.

By 2025 the orbital period had been reduced to approximately 33 days through gravitational assists from these flybys. The extended mission was approved through **September 2025**, with further flybys of inner moons Thebe, Amalthea, Adrastea, and Metis planned. Funding for fiscal year 2026 operations was subject to budget negotiations.

### End of Mission

At the conclusion of its mission, Juno is planned to perform a **deorbit burn** that will send it into Jupiter's atmosphere, where it will be destroyed. This planetary protection measure prevents the spacecraft from accidentally crashing into Europa or other icy moons that might harbour liquid water — and potentially life.
