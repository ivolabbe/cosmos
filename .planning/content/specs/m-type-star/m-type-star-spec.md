## Definition (1–2 sentences)
An M-type star is the coolest and most numerous class of star in the Harvard spectral classification system, with surface temperatures below about 3,900 K and a distinctive red colour caused by molecular absorption — especially titanium oxide (TiO) bands — dominating their spectra. M-type stars range from tiny, dim red dwarfs on the main sequence (including the nearest star to the Sun, Proxima Centauri) to vast, luminous red giants and supergiants such as Betelgeuse.

## Full Content (ALL facts needed for 200–400 words)

### Spectral Classification and Physical Properties
The Harvard spectral sequence orders stars O–B–A–F–G–K–M by decreasing surface temperature. M-type stars occupy the cool end of this sequence, with effective temperatures from about 2,300 K (late M9) to 3,900 K (early M0) [Wikipedia – Stellar Classification]. Their characteristic red-orange colour reflects the dominance of low-energy, long-wavelength photons. The full spectral type combines the letter M, a numeric subtype (0 through 9, where M0 is hottest and M9 is coolest), and a luminosity class Roman numeral (e.g., M2V for a main-sequence dwarf, M1Ia for a supergiant).

The defining spectral signature is strong absorption by titanium oxide (TiO) molecules, which form in the relatively cool stellar atmosphere; TiO bands are detectable by about M0 and dominate by M5 [Wikipedia – Stellar Classification]. Vanadium oxide (VO) bands appear at late subtypes. Water (H₂O) and diatomic hydrogen (H₂) absorption also become important in cooler M dwarfs [STScI M Dwarf Newsletter 2020].

### Red Dwarfs: The Most Common Stars in the Galaxy
Main-sequence M-type stars, known as red dwarfs, account for approximately 76% of all main-sequence stars in the solar neighbourhood and roughly 70% of all stars in the Milky Way by number [Wikipedia – Red Dwarf; STScI 2020]. Despite their dominance by number, their intrinsic faintness means none is visible to the naked eye.

Red dwarf masses span 0.08–0.6 M☉ (solar masses), the lower limit being the hydrogen-burning minimum [Wikipedia – Red Dwarf]. Luminosities range from about 0.0003 to 0.07 L☉ [Wikipedia – Red Dwarf]. Radii fall between roughly 9% and 60% of the Sun's radius. Stars below 0.35 M☉ are fully convective — heat is transported throughout by convection rather than radiation — preventing helium from accumulating at the core and allowing the entire hydrogen supply to be available as fuel [Wikipedia – Red Dwarf].

This fully convective structure produces extraordinary stellar lifetimes: a 0.1 M☉ M dwarf can continue fusing hydrogen for approximately 10 trillion years, far exceeding the current age of the universe (13.8 Gyr) [Wikipedia – Red Dwarf; Britannica – Red Dwarf Star]. The most massive red dwarfs still have lifetimes of tens of billions of years.

M dwarfs span nearly an order of magnitude in mass and two orders of magnitude in luminosity [STScI 2020]. The "active" flare phase lasts 1 Gyr for early M0 dwarfs but extends to 8 Gyr or more for late M8 stars [whillyard.com Type M Stars].

### Flare Activity
Many M-type stars are classified as flare stars, capable of doubling their brightness in minutes due to intense magnetic activity [Wikipedia – Red Dwarf]. Proxima Centauri, a prototypical late M dwarf (spectral type M5.5Ve), has a magnetic field roughly 600 times stronger than the Sun's [Reiners & Basri 2008, cited in A&A 2017 Proxima flare paper]. Between January 2016 and March 2018, 24 flares were recorded from Proxima Centauri with bolometric energies from 10^30.6 to 10^33.5 erg [Wikipedia – Proxima Centauri]. In 2019, Proxima Centauri emitted a flare 14,000 times brighter than average in ultraviolet wavelengths [Wikipedia – Proxima Centauri].

### Habitability Debates
The habitable zone around an M dwarf lies very close to the star — Proxima Centauri b, the nearest known exoplanet, orbits at only ~0.05 AU with a period of ~11.2 days [Wikipedia – Proxima Centauri]. This proximity creates several challenges:

1. **Tidal locking** — planets are likely to keep one face permanently toward the star, creating extreme temperature contrasts between day and night hemispheres [Wikipedia – Habitability of Red Dwarf Systems].
2. **Stellar flares and UV/X-ray radiation** — M dwarf habitable-zone planets receive extreme X-ray and UV flux, potentially hundreds of thousands of times Earth's dose; flare-driven proton events can deplete a planet's ozone column by up to 94% [Wikipedia – Habitability of Red Dwarf Systems; A&A 2017 Proxima Centauri b habitability].
3. **Atmospheric erosion** — stellar winds and coronal mass ejections strip planetary atmospheres; only about 5 of 49 small planets orbiting red dwarfs within 50 parsecs may retain an atmosphere [Wikipedia – Habitability of Red Dwarf Systems].
4. **Photosynthesis constraints** — M dwarf light is heavily infrared-shifted, complicating photosynthesis based on visible-wavelength chlorophyll [Habitability of Red Dwarf Systems Wikipedia].

However, the question is unresolved: cloud formation on tidally locked planets may moderate temperature extremes, some hotter M dwarfs emit enough near-UV to support prebiotic chemistry in early epochs [MNRAS Letters 2024], and rocky planets are extremely common around M dwarfs — TRAPPIST-1, an ultra-cool M8 dwarf, hosts seven roughly Earth-sized planets, three in its habitable zone [NASA Exoplanet Archive].

### M Giants and Supergiants
Not all M-type stars are dim dwarfs. Post-main-sequence stars that have expanded into giants or supergiants also fall in the M spectral class due to their cool surface temperatures.

**Betelgeuse** (α Orionis, spectral type M1–M2 Ia–Iab) is the prototypical M supergiant: a red supergiant with a surface temperature of ~3,600–3,800 K [Wikipedia – Betelgeuse], a luminosity of approximately 65,000–87,000 L☉ [Wikipedia – Betelgeuse], and a radius 640–764 times that of the Sun — large enough to engulf the orbit of Mars if placed at the centre of the Solar System [Wikipedia – Betelgeuse]. It is a semiregular variable with a main period near 400 days.

**Mira** (o Ceti, spectral class M5–M9 IIIe) is a long-period pulsating variable (Mira variable) on the asymptotic giant branch (AGB). Mira variables show pulsation periods exceeding 100 days and visual amplitude changes greater than 2.5 magnitudes [Wikipedia – Mira variable]. They undergo mass loss of ~10⁻⁷ to 10⁻⁶ M☉ per year and are in the penultimate phase before shedding planetary nebulae and becoming white dwarfs [Wikipedia – Mira variable].

### Proxima Centauri: The Nearest Star
Proxima Centauri (spectral type M5.5Ve) is the Sun's nearest stellar neighbour at 4.2465 light-years (1.302 pc) [Wikipedia – Proxima Centauri]. Its properties exemplify the late M dwarf class:
- Mass: 0.1221 M☉ (~12.2% of the Sun) [Wikipedia – Proxima Centauri]
- Radius: 0.1542 R☉ (~15.4% of the Sun, roughly 1.5 times Jupiter's radius) [Wikipedia – Proxima Centauri]
- Luminosity: 0.0016 L☉, of which more than 85% is emitted at infrared wavelengths [Wikipedia – Proxima Centauri]
- Surface temperature: 2,992 ± ~50 K [Wikipedia – Proxima Centauri]
- Rotation period: 83.2 ± 1.6 days [Wikipedia – Proxima Centauri]
- Age: ~4.85 Gyr [Wikipedia – Proxima Centauri]

Proxima Centauri b was announced in 2016 and has a minimum mass of ~1.06 Earth masses, orbiting at ~0.05 AU in the star's habitable zone [Wikipedia – Proxima Centauri b].

## Historical Context

- **1867**: Angelo Secchi classifies red stars as "Type IV" in his early spectral taxonomy, later merged into the M class.
- **1890s**: The Harvard classification system (Fleming, Cannon) assigns M to the coolest red stars based on TiO absorption bands.
- **1943**: The Morgan-Keenan-Kellman (MKK) system formalises luminosity classes, distinguishing M dwarfs (V), giants (III), and supergiants (I).
- **1960**: Proxima Centauri confirmed as the nearest star by Robert Innes (discovery 1915).
- **1984**: The low-mass end of M-type stars (0.08 M☉) identified as the hydrogen-burning minimum; below this are brown dwarfs.
- **2016**: Proxima Centauri b announced as the closest known exoplanet — an Earth-mass world in the habitable zone of an M dwarf.
- **2017**: Seven-planet TRAPPIST-1 system (M8 dwarf) discovered; three planets in the habitable zone. [NASA TRAPPIST-1]
- **2019**: Proxima Centauri emits the largest UV flare ever recorded for this star, 14,000× brighter than average. [Wikipedia – Proxima Centauri]

## Sub-types / Classifications

1. **M Dwarfs (M V)** — Main-sequence red dwarfs, masses 0.08–0.6 M☉, temperature 2,300–3,900 K. Most common stellar type. Fully convective below 0.35 M☉. Examples: Proxima Centauri (M5.5Ve), Barnard's Star (M4Ve), TRAPPIST-1 (M8V).
2. **Early M Dwarfs (M0–M2)** — Partially convective interiors; higher mass and temperature end; active but with shorter flare phase (~1 Gyr).
3. **Mid M Dwarfs (M3–M5)** — Transition to full convection; typically active flare stars.
4. **Late M Dwarfs (M6–M9)** — Fully convective, extremely low luminosity and mass, approaching the brown-dwarf boundary at M9; long flare-active phase (>8 Gyr).
5. **M Giants (M III)** — Post-main-sequence red giants with cool surfaces. Include Mira variables (pulsating AGB stars). Example: Mira (M5–M9 IIIe).
6. **M Supergiants (M I)** — Rare, massive evolved stars with enormous radii and luminosities. Example: Betelgeuse (M1–M2 Ia–Iab), Mu Cephei (M2 Ia).
7. **Flare Stars (UV Ceti type)** — Many late M dwarfs are classified as flare stars; intense magnetic activity drives dramatic brightness outbursts.
