## Definition
A supermoon (formally a perigee-syzygy) is a full Moon — or occasionally a new Moon — that occurs when the Moon is at or near perigee, the closest point in its elliptical orbit around Earth, making it appear slightly larger and brighter than average. The term has no official astronomical definition and was coined by an astrologer in 1979.

## Full Content

### Physical mechanism
- The Moon orbits Earth in an ellipse, not a circle. The closest point is perigee (~356,400–363,300 km); the farthest is apogee (~404,000–406,700 km). [NASA Science, Wikipedia]
- When a full Moon coincides with or falls near perigee, the Moon subtends a larger angle on the sky — up to ~14% more diameter than at apogee, and up to ~30% brighter. [NASA Science]
- The difference in angular diameter between a supermoon and an average full Moon is only a few percent — subtle and difficult to notice without side-by-side comparison. [Wikipedia, RMG Greenwich]
- Apparent brightness scales with the square of angular size (inverse-square law), amplifying the brightness increase relative to the size increase. [Wikipedia]
- The "Moon illusion" — the Moon appearing larger near the horizon — is a perceptual/psychological effect unrelated to supermoon geometry. [Wikipedia]

### Definition and terminology
- No official IAU definition for "supermoon" exists. Different sources use different thresholds (e.g., timeanddate.com: < 360,000 km centre-to-centre; Nolle: within 90% of the year's closest perigee). [Wikipedia]
- The technical term is perigee-syzygy or perigee full Moon. Syzygy describes the co-linear alignment of Sun, Earth, and Moon that defines a full or new Moon phase. [Wikipedia]
- Most media usage restricts "supermoon" to full Moons; Nolle's original 1979 definition included both full and new Moons. [Wikipedia]
- New Moons at perigee are not directly visible (dark phase) but produce the same gravitational and tidal effects. [Wikipedia]

### Tidal effects
- Supermoons strengthen tidal forces slightly; the Moon's gravitational pull on Earth's oceans follows an inverse-cube law, so perigee amplifies tides more than apogee diminishes them. [Wikipedia]
- The combined effect of perigee + syzygy is called a perigean spring tide; this adds roughly 5 cm (2 in) to normal spring tide ranges. [timeanddate.com]
- Research shows slightly increased beach erosion in upper swash zones during perigean spring tides. [Wikipedia]
- Claims of supermoon-triggered earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, or severe storms have not been supported by scientific evidence. [Wikipedia, RMG Greenwich]

### Frequency and occurrence
- Supermoons occur 3–4 times per year and tend to come in consecutive runs (since the anomalistic month — the time for the Moon to return to perigee — is ~27.55 days, close to the synodic month of ~29.53 days). [NASA Science, Wikipedia]
- Perigee and full Moon align approximately every 14 synodic months (the lunar perigee cycle). [Wikipedia]

### Observational notes
- The difference is most apparent when comparing a supermoon with a micromoon (full Moon at apogee) in adjacent months. [NASA Science]
- Photographs often exaggerate apparent size using telephoto lenses and nearby foreground objects. [Wikipedia]
- Moonrise and moonset provide dramatic viewing opportunities due to the horizon perspective, though the Moon is not physically larger at that time. [RMG Greenwich]

### Connection to lunar eclipses
- When a supermoon passes through Earth's shadow, the result is a "super blood Moon" total lunar eclipse; the Moon appears larger and red from atmospheric refraction. The most recent such event was January 21, 2019. [RMG Greenwich]

## Historical Context

- The term "supermoon" was coined in 1979 by American astrologer Richard Nolle in Dell Horoscope magazine, defined as "a new or full moon which occurs with the Moon at or near (within 90% of) its closest approach to Earth in a given orbit." [Wikipedia; Nolle, astropro.com]
- Nolle developed the concept after reading hydrologist Fergus Wood's 1976 NOAA work "Strategic Role of Perigean Spring Tides in Nautical History and Coastal Flooding." [Wikipedia]
- In 2011, Nolle clarified that his threshold is calculated as 90% of the difference in the year's perigee extremes. [Wikipedia]
- The term entered mainstream science media around 2011–2012 and attracted wide public attention with the March 19, 2011 supermoon (356,577 km).
- The November 14, 2016 supermoon (356,511 km) was the closest full Moon since January 26, 1948, and became the most-publicised supermoon of the 2010s; the next comparable event will occur November 25, 2034. [EarthSky, NASA]
- The closest full supermoon of the 21st century will occur on December 6, 2052, at 356,429 km. [astropixels.com]
- The technical description "perigee syzygy" predates Nolle; his contribution was only the popular label. [Wikipedia]

## Sub-types / Classifications

- **Super full Moon** — a full Moon occurring near perigee; visible as an enlarged, brighter disc. Produces perigean spring tides.
- **Super new Moon** — a new Moon at perigee per Nolle's definition; gravitationally equivalent to a super full Moon but invisible (dark phase).
- **Micromoon (mini-Moon)** — the opposite: a full or new Moon near apogee; appears ~14% smaller in diameter than a super full Moon.
- **Super blood Moon** — a total lunar eclipse coinciding with a supermoon; the Moon appears enlarged and red.
- **Blue supermoon** — a supermoon that is also a "blue moon" by the seasonal definition (third full Moon of four in one season). August 2024 was an example.
- The umbrella technical term for all these events is perigee-syzygy.
