## Definition

An O-type star is the hottest and most luminous class of star in the standard OBAFGKM spectral sequence, with surface temperatures exceeding 30,000 K, masses typically above 16 solar masses, and luminosities reaching millions of times that of the Sun. They are extraordinarily rare — roughly 1 in 3,000,000 nearby main-sequence stars — yet they dominate the ultraviolet radiation field of galaxies and power the formation of HII regions. [Wikipedia: O-type star]

## Full Content

### Physical Description

- O-type stars are the hottest, most luminous, and most massive stars on the main sequence; they sit at the extreme blue end of the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram [Wikipedia: stellar classification]
- Surface temperatures range from ~30,000 K (late O-types such as O9) to over 52,000 K (earliest O2 sub-types) [Wikipedia: O-type star]
- Luminosities span 10,000–1,000,000 L☉ on the main sequence; giant and supergiant O-types can exceed several million L☉ [Wikipedia: O-type star]
- Masses run from ~16 M☉ at the cool end to potentially 200 M☉ for the most extreme objects; the theoretical upper stellar mass limit at solar metallicity is ~120–150 M☉ [Wikipedia: O-type star]
- Typical radii are ≥6.6 R☉, with a representative main-sequence O star near 10 R☉ [Wikipedia: O-type MS star]
- Their color is deep blue-white; color index B−V ≈ −0.33 [Wikipedia: O-type MS star]
- They emit the bulk of their luminosity in the ultraviolet [Wikipedia: stellar classification]

### Spectral Features

- The defining spectral feature is strong absorption (and sometimes emission) lines of singly ionized helium (He II), particularly He II λ4686 [Wikipedia: stellar classification]
- Ionized metal lines — Si IV, O III, N III, C III — are prominent in the blue-ultraviolet [Wikipedia: stellar classification]
- Neutral helium (He I) lines are present and strengthen from O2 to O9, while H Balmer lines are weaker than in B-type stars [Wikipedia: stellar classification]
- Modern classification uses the ratio of N IV λ4058 to N III λλ4634–42 to anchor the sub-type sequence [Wikipedia: stellar classification]
- "Of" stars display nitrogen and helium emission lines in their spectra and represent evolved O-type objects with enhanced mass loss; these grade continuously into Wolf–Rayet spectra [Wikipedia: Wolf–Rayet star]
- The "Vz" suffix flags extremely young O dwarfs at the zero-age main sequence [Wikipedia: O-type star]

### Sub-types and Luminosity Classes

- Sub-types run O2 through O9.7, with O2 being hottest and O9.7 coolest; O7 is the anchor point where He II λ4541 and He I λ4471 lines are equal in strength [Wikipedia: O-type star]
- Luminosity classes: V (main sequence / dwarf), IV (subgiant), III (giant), II (bright giant), I (supergiant); class Ia indicates the most luminous supergiants [Wikipedia: O-type star]
- Special designations include "f" (N III and He II emission), "n" (rapid rotation, broad lines), "p" (peculiar), and "e" (Hα emission) [Wikipedia: O-type star]
- The coolest O-types (O9–O9.7) overlap with the hottest B-type stars; the boundary is defined spectroscopically, not by temperature alone [Wikipedia: stellar classification]

### Rarity and Distribution

- Approximately 20,000 O-type stars exist in the entire Milky Way; only ~1 in 3,000,000 nearby main-sequence stars is an O-type [Wikipedia: O-type MS star; stellar classification]
- Despite their rarity, 4 of the 90 brightest stars visible from Earth are O-type, because their extreme luminosity makes them visible across kiloparsec distances [Wikipedia: O-type star]
- O-type stars are almost exclusively found in the spiral arms of the Galaxy, concentrated in giant molecular clouds and HII regions [Wikipedia: HII region]
- They aggregate into OB associations: loose, gravitationally unbound groups of 10–100+ massive O and B stars spanning 700–1,500 light-years; examples include Orion OB1, Cygnus OB2, and Scorpius–Centaurus [Wikipedia: OB association]

### Role in the Interstellar Medium

- The intense UV flux from O-type stars photoionizes the surrounding hydrogen gas, creating HII regions (emission nebulae); a single O star can maintain an ionized Strömgren sphere many parsecs across [Wikipedia: HII region]
- Their powerful stellar winds (up to 2,500 km/s; mass-loss rates of 10−6–10−5 M☉/yr) inject kinetic energy and momentum into the ISM, sweep up cavities, and may trigger further star formation by compressing adjacent molecular gas [Wikipedia: HII region; Zeta Puppis]
- Wind mass-loss can strip away a significant fraction of the star's envelope over its lifetime, fundamentally affecting its evolutionary path [Wikipedia: Wolf–Rayet star]
- O-type stars dominate the UV radiation field of their host galaxy and are the primary producers of ionizing photons used in estimating galaxy star-formation rates [Wikipedia: O-type star]

### Evolutionary Path

- Because they burn hydrogen so rapidly via the CNO cycle, O-type stars have the shortest lifetimes of any main-sequence stars: <1 Myr for the most massive, up to ~10 Myr for the least massive O-types [Wikipedia: O-type star]
- After exhausting core hydrogen, low-mass O stars (~16–30 M☉) expand into red supergiants before exploding as core-collapse supernovae, leaving neutron stars [Wikipedia: O-type star]
- Very massive O stars (>60 M☉) lose their hydrogen envelopes through stellar winds and become luminous blue variables (LBVs) and then Wolf–Rayet stars before undergoing supernova; the remnant is often a black hole [Wikipedia: Wolf–Rayet star]
- Intermediate-mass O stars develop "Of" spectral features as they evolve off the main sequence, transitioning through blue supergiant and possible red supergiant phases [Wikipedia: Wolf–Rayet star]
- All O-type stars are geologically young — the oldest are only ~10 Myr old — so they serve as tracers of current and recent star formation [Wikipedia: O-type MS star]
- No exoplanets have been detected around O-type stars; their brief lives and powerful radiation fields make planet formation and survival unlikely [Wikipedia: O-type star; O-type MS star]

### Notable Examples

- **Theta1 Orionis C** (O6Vp): the most luminous star of the Trapezium cluster in the Orion Nebula; 33 M☉, 39,000 K, 204,000 L☉; ionizes most of the Orion Nebula; the first O-type star found to have a magnetic field (1.1 kG, detected 2002) [Wikipedia: Theta1 Orionis C]
- **Zeta Puppis** (O4Ifn): one of the most luminous stars known; 25.3 M☉, ~40,000 K, ~447,000 L☉; stellar wind at 2,500 km/s losing >10−6 M☉/yr; a classic O supergiant [Wikipedia: Zeta Puppis]
- **10 Lacertae** (O9V): a standard star for the O9V sub-type anchor [Wikipedia: stellar classification]
- **S Monocerotis** (O7V): another MK standard [Wikipedia: stellar classification]
- **ζ Ophiuchi** (O9.5V): runaway O-type star, possibly ejected from a binary after a supernova [Wikipedia: O-type MS star]
- **Plaskett's Star**: binary with two O-class components, one of the most massive binary systems known [Wikipedia: O-type MS star]

## Historical Context

- The OBAFGKM classification system grew out of the Harvard spectral survey, principally through the work of Annie Jump Cannon, who by 1912 established the modern lettered sub-type system with numerical subdivisions [Wikipedia: stellar classification]
- Williamina Fleming classified the initial large catalogue of spectra (>10,000 stars) using the original Draper lettered system [Wikipedia: stellar classification]
- The two-dimensional Morgan–Keenan (MK) system, introduced in 1943 by Morgan, Keenan, and Kellman, added luminosity classes to the temperature classification, allowing O-type stars to be distinguished as dwarfs, giants, or supergiants [Wikipedia: stellar classification]
- The original 1943 MKK scheme covered only subtypes O5–O9.5; the sequence was extended to O9.7 in 1971, O4 in 1978, and to O2 and O3 in more recent decades as the hottest objects were better characterised [Wikipedia: stellar classification]
- The traditional mnemonic is "Oh, Be A Fine Girl: Kiss Me!" (OBAFGKM), placing O at the hot end [Wikipedia: stellar classification]

| O9.5–O9.7 | ~30,000–33,000 K | Coolest O-types; blend into B0 territory |

**Luminosity classes:**
- **V** (dwarf/main sequence): narrow, weak or absent emission; classic examples: 10 Lac (O9V), S Mon (O7V)
- **III** (giant): stronger winds and emission features than dwarfs
- **I / Ia** (supergiant): prominent "Of" emission lines; strongest winds; examples: Zeta Puppis (O4Ifn)
- **"f" suffix**: N III λλ4634–42 and He II λ4686 in emission — hallmark of evolved O supergiants
- **"Vz" suffix**: extreme luminosity for age; young, near zero-age main sequence
- **"n" suffix**: rapid rotation causing line broadening
- **"p" suffix**: spectral peculiarities

**Evolutionary connections:**
- O dwarf → Of star → Wolf–Rayet (WN → WC) → supernova (for M > 20–30 M☉)
- Intermediate "slash stars" (e.g. Of/WN) exist at the O–WR boundary
