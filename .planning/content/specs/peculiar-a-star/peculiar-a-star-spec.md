## Definition

A peculiar A star (Ap star) is a main-sequence star of spectral type A or late B whose spectrum shows large overabundances of certain heavy metals — including strontium, chromium, and europium — combined with an unusually strong global magnetic field, setting it apart from normal A-type stars, which have unremarkable surface chemistries. [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]

## Full Content

### Physical Description and Key Properties
- Ap stars belong to the Preston CP2 class: chemically peculiar main-sequence stars with spectral types A and late B [Wikipedia: Chemically Peculiar Stars]
- They display overabundances of strontium (Sr), chromium (Cr), europium (Eu), praseodymium (Pr), neodymium (Nd), and in some subtypes silicon (Si) [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]
- Many simultaneously show underabundances of helium and light elements such as carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen [Wikipedia: Alpha Circini; Wikipedia: Przybylski's Star]
- Rotation is much slower than typical A-type stars; most rotate at well under 100 km/s [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]
- Strong, organised dipolar magnetic fields of 1–30 kG, far stronger than those of normal A-type stars [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]
- The extreme case is HD 215441 (Babcock's Star), measured at 33.5 kG — the largest magnetic field directly measured in a main-sequence star at the time of discovery [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]

### Physical Mechanism — Atomic Diffusion
- Chemical peculiarities are not primordial: they arise from **atomic diffusion** in the outer stellar layers after formation [Wikipedia: Chemically Peculiar Stars]
- Two competing forces act on individual atoms: **radiative levitation** (radiation pressure pushes elements with many absorption lines toward the surface) and **gravitational settling** (gravity pulls heavier or less-absorbing atoms downward) [Wikipedia: Chemically Peculiar Stars]
- The magnetic field suppresses convective mixing, allowing a stable, nearly quiescent outer envelope in which this slow diffusion can operate over millions of years [Wikipedia: Chemically Peculiar Stars]
- Elements that absorb efficiently (Sr, Cr, Eu, Mn) levitate; those that absorb less (He, C, N, O) sink — producing spectroscopic signatures wildly out of step with the bulk stellar composition [Wikipedia: Chemically Peculiar Stars]

### Oblique Rotator Model and Variability
- Ap stars are **spectrum variables**: their spectral line strengths and radial velocities change periodically with the stellar rotation period [Wikipedia: Oblique Rotator Model]
- The **oblique rotator model** explains this: the magnetic dipole axis is tilted relative to the rotation axis, so as the star rotates, an observer sees different magnetic hemispheres, hence varying apparent field strength and varying abundances [Wikipedia: Oblique Rotator Model]
- Chemical overabundances are concentrated in patches ("abundance spots") tied to the magnetic geometry; these are mapped using **Doppler imaging** and **high-resolution spectroscopy** [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]
- Photometric variability (typically millimagnitude-level) follows the same rotation period [Wikipedia: Rapidly Oscillating Ap Star]

### Sub-types
- **SrCrEu Ap stars**: the classic cool Ap subtype; enhanced Sr, Cr, Eu; temperatures ~8,000–10,000 K. Example: Gamma Equulei (A9VpSrCrEu) [Wikipedia: Gamma Equulei]
- **Silicon Ap stars (Si/He-weak)**: hotter Ap stars (~10,000–15,000 K) with overabundant silicon and weak helium lines (CP2/CP4 boundary) [Wikipedia: Chemically Peculiar Stars]
- **Rapidly Oscillating Ap stars (roAp)**: a subset that pulsates in high-overtone non-radial pressure modes; pulsation axis aligned with the magnetic axis (oblique pulsator model); periods 5–23.6 minutes [Wikipedia: Rapidly Oscillating Ap Star]
- **HgMn stars (CP3)**: sometimes historically grouped with Ap stars but are non-magnetic; overabundant mercury and manganese; no strong global magnetic field [Wikipedia: Chemically Peculiar Stars]
- **Am stars (CP1)**: also non-magnetic; underabundant Ca/Sc, overabundant Fe-group and heavy metals; slow rotation from tidal braking in binaries; the brightest example is Sirius [Wikipedia: Am Stars]

### Notable Examples
- **HD 101065 (Przybylski's Star)**: most chemically extreme known Ap star; F-type temperature (~6,131 K) but enormous overabundances of rare-earth elements (Z = 35–82), a factor ~1,000× solar; 12.15-min pulsation period; prototype roAp star [Wikipedia: Przybylski's Star]
- **Alpha Circini (α Cir)**: brightest roAp star in the sky (apparent magnitude 3.18–3.21); A7 Vp SrCrEu; dominant pulsation 6.8 min; 54 light-years distant [Wikipedia: Alpha Circini]
- **Gamma Equulei (γ Equ)**: A9VpSrCrEu; multiple pulsation modes near 12 minutes; ~118 light-years [Wikipedia: Gamma Equulei]
- **HD 222925**: extremely metal-rich Ap star with the widest detected range of heavy elements (detected up to Z = 96), studied with the Gemini GHOST spectrograph [Roederer et al. 2022, ApJS 260, 27]
- **Babcock's Star (HD 215441)**: field star with highest historically measured Ap magnetic field: 33.5 kG [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]
- **Alpheratz (α And)**: the brightest HgMn (CP3) star (V = +2.06); B8IV-VHgMn; overabundant Hg, Mn, Ga, Xe; historically classified with Ap stars [Wikipedia: Alpha Andromedae]

### Observational Evidence and Significance
- Ap star spectra were first noted in the 19th century but systematic study began with Maury and Cannon's Harvard spectral survey [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]
- The Preston (1974) classification formalised four CP classes; Ap stars correspond to CP2 [Wikipedia: Chemically Peculiar Stars]
- Magnetic field discovery: Horace Babcock measured the first Ap star magnetic field in 1947, establishing the connection between magnetic fields and chemical peculiarity [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]
- Donald Kurtz discovered roAp pulsations in HD 101065 in 1978, opening up Ap star asteroseismology [Wikipedia: Rapidly Oscillating Ap Star]
- roAp asteroseismology probes deep stellar structure and magnetic-pulsation coupling unobtainable from surface spectroscopy alone [Wikipedia: Rapidly Oscillating Ap Star]
- The fossil field hypothesis — that strong magnetic fields are relics from the pre-main-sequence phase trapped during star formation — is favoured for the dipolar field geometry, though details remain debated [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]

## Historical Context

- Late 19th / early 20th century: Peculiar A-type spectra first identified in Harvard spectral surveys; Antonia Maury flagged "peculiar" spectra in the 1890s [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]
- 1947: Horace Babcock measured the first Ap star magnetic field (78 Virginis); linked chemical peculiarity and magnetism [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]
- 1950s: The oblique rotator model proposed to explain periodic spectral/photometric variations as a geometric effect of rotation with an inclined dipole field [Wikipedia: Oblique Rotator Model]
- 1961: Antoni Przybylski identifies HD 101065 as an exceptionally unusual Ap star [Wikipedia: Przybylski's Star]
- 1974: George Preston formalises the four-class CP classification scheme [Wikipedia: Chemically Peculiar Stars]
- 1978: Donald Kurtz discovers rapid oscillations in HD 101065, founding the roAp class [Wikipedia: Rapidly Oscillating Ap Star]
- 1980s–present: Doppler imaging applied to Ap stars; abundance spot maps produced for dozens of systems [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]
- 2022: Roederer et al. use the Gemini GHOST spectrograph to detect 65 chemical elements in HD 222925, the widest elemental census of any star other than the Sun [Roederer et al. 2022]

## Sub-types / Classifications

Using the Preston (1974) system for chemically peculiar (CP) hot main-sequence stars:

| Class | Name | Temperature | Magnetic? | Key Abundance Anomalies | Example |
|-------|------|-------------|-----------|------------------------|---------|
| CP1 | Am (metallic-line) | 7,000–10,000 K | No | Ca/Sc low; Zn, Sr, Zr, Ba high | Sirius |
| CP2 | Ap (magnetic) | 8,000–15,000 K | Yes (1–30 kG) | Si, Cr, Sr, Eu high; He, C, N, O low | Alpha Circini |
| CP3 | HgMn | 10,000–15,000 K | No | Hg, Mn high | Alpheratz |
| CP4 | He-weak | 10,000–20,000 K | Sometimes | He lines anomalously weak | Several B stars |

"Peculiar A star" in strict usage refers to CP2 (Ap) stars. Colloquially it may include all four CP classes.
