## Definition

An Earth-crossing asteroid (ECA) is an asteroid whose orbital path around the Sun intersects Earth's orbit [Britannica: https://www.britannica.com/science/Earth-crossing-asteroid]. The term is defined geometrically: it does not imply imminent collision, since highly inclined orbits can cross Earth's orbital plane without actually approaching Earth closely [Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth-crossing_asteroid].

Earth-crossing asteroids are a subset of near-Earth asteroids (NEAs), which are themselves a subset of near-Earth objects (NEOs). NEOs are defined as asteroids and comets with perihelion distance q < 1.3 AU [NASA CNEOS: https://cneos.jpl.nasa.gov/about/neo_groups.html].

---

## Classification: Apollo vs. Aten Groups

Earth-crossing asteroids fall into two dynamical groups based on the size of their orbits [NASA CNEOS: https://cneos.jpl.nasa.gov/about/neo_groups.html]:

### Apollo Asteroids
- **Orbital criteria:** semi-major axis a > 1.0 AU and perihelion distance q < 1.017 AU
- Named after asteroid **1862 Apollo** (discovered 1932, Karl Reinmuth)
- Their orbits are larger than Earth's, but their perihelion (closest point to the Sun) falls inside Earth's aphelion (farthest point from the Sun), so they cross Earth's path
- As of early 2025, **21,083 Apollo asteroids** are known — the largest group of NEOs [Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_asteroid]
- Notable members: Bennu (101955), 1566 Icarus, 69230 Hermes

### Aten Asteroids
- **Orbital criteria:** semi-major axis a < 1.0 AU and aphelion distance Q > 0.983 AU
- Named after asteroid **2062 Aten** (discovered 1976, Eleanor Helin)
- Their orbits are smaller than Earth's, but their aphelion extends past Earth's perihelion, so they cross Earth's orbit
- Considerably fewer known than Apollos — their Earth-interior orbits make them harder to detect with ground-based surveys [Catalina Sky Survey: https://catalina.lpl.arizona.edu/faq/what-are-neo-groups]
- Notable member: 99942 Apophis

### Distinction from Related Groups
- **Amor asteroids** (a > 1.0 AU, 1.017 < q < 1.3 AU) approach but do **not** cross Earth's orbit; they are Earth-approaching but not Earth-crossing
- **Atira asteroids** (a < 1.0 AU, Q < 0.983 AU) orbit entirely within Earth's orbit; they are non-crossing

## Relation to NEOs and PHAs

All Earth-crossing asteroids are NEOs, but not all NEOs are Earth-crossers (Amors are not). The more operationally important category for planetary defence is **Potentially Hazardous Asteroids (PHAs)**, defined by two criteria [NASA CNEOS: https://cneos.jpl.nasa.gov/about/neo_groups.html]:

1. Earth Minimum Orbit Intersection Distance (MOID) ≤ 0.05 AU
2. Absolute magnitude H ≤ 22.0 (roughly diameter ≥ 140 m)

As of late 2024, **2,465** known NEOs qualify as PHAs [Wikipedia NEO article: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near-Earth_object]. The PHA designation replaced the earlier "ECA" designation because determining whether an orbit will actually intersect Earth's millennia into the future proved impractical; MOID provides a more physically meaningful hazard metric [Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth-crossing_asteroid].

## Discovery Rate and Current Numbers

As of 30 December 2024, **37,378 NEOs** have been discovered (37,255 asteroids and 123 comets) [Wikipedia NEO article: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near-Earth_object]. The current breakdown of Earth-crossing groups:
- Apollo: ~21,083 known (as of early 2025) [Wikipedia Apollo asteroid: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_asteroid]
- Aten: far fewer, given detection difficulty

Discovery has accelerated dramatically since the 1990s with dedicated survey programs. Over **90% of NEOs larger than 1 km** have been found, fulfilling a Congressional mandate assigned to NASA in 1998 (Spaceguard goal); the current focus is completing the catalogue of objects larger than **140 m** [NASA CNEOS: https://cneos.jpl.nasa.gov/stats/totals.html].

## Detection Surveys

The major programmes responsible for discovering Earth-crossing asteroids [ATLAS: https://atlas.fallingstar.com/other_surveys.php; Wikipedia ATLAS: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid_Terrestrial-impact_Last_Alert_System]:

| Survey | Operator | Notes |
|--------|----------|-------|
| **Catalina Sky Survey (CSS)** | University of Arizona | One of the top NEO discovery sites |
| **Pan-STARRS** | University of Hawaii | Full-sky survey, one of top 3 NEO discovery sites |
| **ATLAS** (Asteroid Terrestrial-impact Last Alert System) | University of Hawaii / NASA | Optimised for short-warning detection of smaller impactors; discovered 2024 YR4 |
| **LINEAR** | MIT / USAF / NASA | Pioneered automated NEO discovery in 1990s–2000s |

The umbrella term **Spaceguard** loosely describes collective efforts to discover, catalogue, and characterise NEOs [Wikipedia Spaceguard: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceguard].

A recent notable discovery: **2024 YR4**, found by ATLAS on 27 December 2024, briefly reached Torino Scale level 3 before refined observations removed any impact threat [NASA CNEOS news: https://cneos.jpl.nasa.gov/news/news210.html; arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05694].

## Notable Examples

### 99942 Apophis
- **Type:** Aten (a < 1.0 AU)
- **Discovered:** 19 June 2004, Kitt Peak National Observatory (Roy Tucker, David Tholen, Fabrizio Bernardi) [NASA Science: https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/asteroids/apophis/]
- **Size:** ~340–450 m across (elongated, possibly dual-lobed); classified S-type (silicate/metallic) [Wikipedia Apophis: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/99942_Apophis]
- **2029 close approach:** On 13 April 2029 Apophis will pass ~32,000 km from Earth's surface — closer than the ring of geostationary satellites, and visible to the naked eye (magnitude ~3.1) from Europe, Africa, and western Asia [Wikipedia Apophis: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/99942_Apophis]
- **Impact risk:** Ruled out for at least 100 years [NASA Science: https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/asteroids/apophis/]
- **Mission:** NASA's OSIRIS-APEX spacecraft is being redirected to rendezvous with Apophis during its 2029 flyby

### 101955 Bennu
- **Type:** Apollo (a > 1.0 AU)
- **Discovered:** 11 September 1999 by the LINEAR Project [Wikipedia Bennu: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/101955_Bennu]
- **Size:** ~490 m mean diameter (~1/3 mile wide); B-type carbonaceous asteroid [NASA Bennu: https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/asteroids/101955-bennu/]
- **Orbit:** Completes one orbit every 436.6 days (1.2 years); approaches Earth every ~6 years [Wikipedia Bennu: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/101955_Bennu]
- **Impact risk:** Cumulative impact probability through 2300 is ~1 in 1,750 (0.057%); highest single risk on 24 September 2182 (~1 in 2,700) [JPL: https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasa-spacecraft-provides-insight-into-asteroid-bennus-future-orbit/]
- **OSIRIS-REx:** First US mission to collect a sample from an asteroid; collected 121.6 g of material on 20 October 2020; capsule returned to Earth 24 September 2023 [NASA OSIRIS-REx: https://science.nasa.gov/mission/osiris-rex/]
- Sample analysis reveals organics, sugars, hydrated minerals — primordial solar system material ~4.6 Gyr old [Lauretta et al. 2024, Meteoritics & Planetary Science: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/maps.14227]

### 1566 Icarus
- **Type:** Apollo
- **Discovered:** 27 June 1949, Walter Baade, Palomar Observatory
- First asteroid observed by radar (1968); perihelion closer to the Sun than Mercury; crosses orbits of Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars [Wikipedia Icarus: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1566_Icarus]
- Lowest-numbered PHA

### 69230 Hermes
- **Type:** Apollo
- **Discovered:** 28 October 1937, Karl Reinmuth, Heidelberg; became a lost asteroid after four days of observations; rediscovered 15 October 2003 by Brian Skiff (LONEOS) [Cornell Chronicle: https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2003/10/asteroid-hermes-lost-66-years-two-objects]
- Found to be a binary asteroid system

## Origin and Dynamical Delivery

Earth-crossing asteroids do not stay on their current orbits indefinitely — orbital lifetimes are typically tens of millions of years. They are continuously replenished from two primary source regions [NASA NTRS: https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19800040395; ScienceDirect: https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/apollo-asteroids]:

### 1. Main Asteroid Belt (dominant source)
- Gravitational perturbations by Jupiter drive asteroid fragments into **mean-motion resonances** (e.g., 3:1, 5:2 with Jupiter) known as **Kirkwood gaps** [Wikipedia Asteroid Belt: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid_belt]
- Asteroids in these resonances undergo chaotic orbital evolution, eventually achieving planet-crossing orbits
- The **ν₆ secular resonance** at the inner edge of the main belt (~2.1 AU) is a major injection mechanism [MNRAS: https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/473/1/295/4160101]
- Collisions within the belt produce fragments; the **Yarkovsky effect** (thermal radiation recoil) gradually drifts small bodies into resonances over millions of years

### 2. Cometary Sources
- Extinct or dormant comet nuclei that have exhausted their volatiles can masquerade as asteroids
- Some NEAs on high-eccentricity orbits are suspected extinct comets; several have orbital parameters nearly identical to known short-period comets [A&A 2024: https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/full_html/2024/07/aa49635-24/aa49635-24.html]
- Jupiter-family comets evolving inward contribute a fraction of the Apollo and Aten populations

## Impact Hazard Context

Kilometre-scale Earth-crossing asteroids are estimated to collide with Earth "a few times every million years" [Britannica: https://www.britannica.com/science/Earth-crossing-asteroid]. Such an event releases energy equivalent to many nuclear warheads and would cause global climate disruption. The canonical example is the ~10 km impactor that ended the Cretaceous Period ~66 million years ago (Chicxulub event) [Britannica].

Approximately **1,000 Earth-crossing asteroids larger than 1 km** are estimated to exist; over 90% have now been catalogued [Britannica; NASA CNEOS: https://cneos.jpl.nasa.gov/stats/totals.html].
