## Definition
A peculiar star is a star whose spectrum shows elemental abundances that are significantly different from what is expected for stars of its temperature and luminosity class, arising from physical processes — primarily atomic diffusion driven by radiation pressure and gravity — that selectively concentrate or deplete certain elements in the stellar atmosphere. The term "peculiar" in stellar astronomy is a technical designation (suffix 'p' in spectral classifications), not a colloquial one, and the most common class are the chemically peculiar (CP) stars of the upper main sequence.

## Full Content

- The suffix 'p' in a spectral type (e.g. A2p, B9p) signals a star with anomalous spectral lines not explainable by its temperature or luminosity class alone. [Wikipedia: Chemically peculiar star]
- Chemical peculiarities occur predominantly in hot main-sequence stars (spectral types B and A), where radiative atmospheres are stable enough for element separation to operate over stellar lifetimes. [Wikipedia: Chemically peculiar star]
- The physical mechanism is **atomic diffusion**: radiation pressure selectively levitates some elements (e.g. Mn, Sr, Y, Zr) toward the surface while gravity causes others (e.g. He, N, O) to settle downward; the process requires a calm, non-convective atmosphere. [Wikipedia: Chemically peculiar star; Wikipedia: Am star]
- Approximately **5–10% of hot main-sequence stars** show chemical peculiarities, making this a common and astrophysically important phenomenon. [Wikipedia: Chemically peculiar star]
- Slow rotation is nearly universal among chemically peculiar stars: rapid rotation drives convective mixing that erases the diffusion-built abundance patterns. [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars; Wikipedia: Am star]
- The canonical classification is Preston (1974), which divides hot CP stars into four groups (CP1–CP4). [Preston 1974, ARA&A 12:257]
- **Ap/Bp stars (CP2)** are the most studied subclass: they carry strong organised magnetic fields (typically 1–30 kG, up to 33.5 kG in HD 215441), whose geometry inhibits convection and locks abundance patterns into "spots" on the stellar surface. [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]
- Abundance spots in Ap stars are spatially correlated with the magnetic field geometry — enhancements occur preferentially at magnetic poles, depletions near the magnetic equator. [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars; Wikipedia: Alpha2 CVn variables]
- The **oblique rotator model** (magnetic axis tilted relative to rotation axis) explains why Ap star spectra and brightness vary periodically with rotation: the Earth-facing hemisphere cycles through different spot configurations. [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars; Wikipedia: Alpha2 CVn variables]
- **Am stars (CP1)** are non-magnetic; their peculiarities arise purely from diffusion. They preferentially occur in binary systems where tidal braking has slowed rotation. Sirius A (α CMa) is the brightest known Am star. [Wikipedia: Am star]
- **HgMn stars (CP3)** are non-magnetic, very slow rotators with enormous overabundances of ionised Hg and Mn; they occupy spectral types B8–A0 (10,000–15,000 K). Alpheratz (α And), Gienah Corvi (γ Crv A), Maia (20 Tau), and Nath (β Tau) are the brightest examples. [Wikipedia: Mercury–manganese star]
- **He-weak stars (CP4)** show weaker helium lines than their effective temperature predicts; a related group, He-strong stars, show stronger helium lines and occupy hotter ranges (18,000–23,000 K). [Wikipedia: Chemically peculiar star]
- **Rapidly oscillating Ap (roAp) stars** are a magnetic Ap subclass that pulsate in high-overtone non-radial pressure modes with periods of 5–23 minutes; about 40 are known. The pulsation axis is aligned with the magnetic axis (oblique pulsator model). Przybylski's Star (HD 101065) is the most extreme known roAp, with overabundances of lanthanide rare-earth elements thousands of times solar and a rotation period of ~188 years. [Wikipedia: Rapidly oscillating Ap star; Wikipedia: Przybylski's Star]
- Cool chemically peculiar stars (G–M) arise by different mechanisms — nuclear fusion products mixed to the surface (carbon stars, S-type stars) or mass transfer in binary systems (barium stars, CH stars) — not atmospheric diffusion. [Wikipedia: Chemically peculiar star; Wikipedia: Barium star]
- **Carbon stars** are luminous red giants with C/O > 1; surface carbon excess comes from the third dredge-up on the asymptotic giant branch (AGB). They appear strikingly red (e.g. Y CVn "La Superba," R Leporis "Hind's Crimson Star"). [Wikipedia: Carbon star]
- **S-type stars** are AGB giants with C/O ≈ 0.5–1 and strong ZrO absorption bands; they are the transition state between M giants and full carbon stars. [Wikipedia: S-type star]
- **Barium stars** (spectral types G–K) show overabundances of s-process elements (Ba, Sr, Y, Zr) because an AGB companion transferred enriched material before evolving into a white dwarf; all confirmed barium stars are binaries. Identified by Bidelman & Keenan (1951). [Wikipedia: Barium star]
- **Lambda Boötis (λ Boo) stars** are Population I A-type stars with anomalously low iron-peak element abundances at their surfaces; proposed mechanisms include accretion of metal-poor gas from a circumstellar disk or from a decaying hot Jupiter. [Wikipedia: Lambda Boötis star]
- Spectral peculiarities can affect variability: ACV (Alpha2 CVn) variables — a subset of Ap stars — vary by 0.01–0.1 mag with periods of 0.5–160 days, all tied to the stellar rotation period. [Wikipedia: Alpha2 CVn variable]

## Historical Context

- The suffix 'p' for spectral peculiarity has been in use since the early 20th century, embedded in MK spectral classification (e.g. Ap, Bp) to flag anomalous spectra observed at Harvard and subsequent observatories.
- **Antonia Maury** at Harvard (1897) was among the first to note unusual spectral patterns in certain A-type stars, though the physical explanation remained elusive for decades.
- The foundational modern classification of chemically peculiar stars was established by **George W. Preston** (1974) in his review "The chemically peculiar stars of the upper main sequence," *Annual Review of Astronomy and Astrophysics* 12:257–277. This remains the standard reference for the CP1–CP4 scheme.
- The atomic diffusion mechanism was developed theoretically by **Georges Michaud** (1970), explaining how radiation pressure and gravity separate elements in stable stellar atmospheres.
- **Alpha2 Canum Venaticorum** (Cor Caroli, "Charles's Heart") is the prototype of the Ap variable subclass; the name was coined in 1660 by Sir Charles Scarborough. The star's magnetic and spectroscopic variability were intensively studied through the 20th century, cementing the oblique rotator model.
- **Przybylski's Star** (HD 101065) was identified by Polish–Australian astronomer **Antoni Przybylski** in 1961 as a star that defied standard spectral classification. Its 12.15-minute pulsation was discovered by **Donald Kurtz** in 1978, making it the first known roAp star.
- **Barium stars** were identified by **William P. Bidelman** and **Philip C. Keenan** in 1951; binary mass transfer was confirmed when white dwarf companions were detected in UV observations in the late 20th century.
- Sirius A's Am classification (metallic-line star) has been known since the mid-20th century, making the brightest star in the night sky also a textbook example of a chemically peculiar star.

## Sub-types / Classifications

### Preston (1974) hot CP classification (main-sequence B and A stars):

| Class | Common name | Magnetic? | Teff (K) | Key spectral signature |
|-------|-------------|-----------|----------|----------------------|
| CP1 | Am (metallic-line) | No | 7,000–10,000 | Weak Ca/Sc; enhanced Zn, Sr, Zr, Ba |
| CP2 | Ap/Bp | Yes (1–30 kG) | 8,000–15,000 | Enhanced Si, Cr, Sr, Eu; abundance spots |
| CP3 | HgMn | No | 10,000–15,000 | Extreme Hg II (398.4 nm), Mn overabundance |
| CP4 | He-weak (& He-strong) | Some | 10,000–23,000 | Anomalous helium line strength |

### Subclass of CP2:
- **roAp stars** — Rapidly oscillating Ap stars; pulsate in 5–23 min pressure modes aligned with the magnetic axis; ~40 known.
- **ACV variables** (Alpha2 CVn type) — Ap stars varying in brightness and spectrum with the rotation period (0.5–160 days).

### Lambda Boötis stars:
- Population I A-type main-sequence stars with iron-peak deficiencies (not diffusion-driven, probably accretion of metal-poor gas); prototype: λ Boo itself.

### Cool chemically peculiar stars (separate physical mechanisms):
- **Carbon stars (C-N, C-R, C-J, C-H, C-Hd)** — AGB giants with C/O > 1 from internal dredge-up; T ≈ 2,600–5,100 K.
- **S-type stars** — AGB giants with C/O ≈ 0.5–1; strong ZrO bands; transitional between M and carbon stars.
- **Barium stars** — G–K giants enriched in s-process elements (Ba, Sr, Y, Zr) via binary mass transfer from an AGB companion, now a white dwarf.
- **CH stars** — carbon-enriched metal-poor giants; related to barium stars but in older, more metal-poor populations.
