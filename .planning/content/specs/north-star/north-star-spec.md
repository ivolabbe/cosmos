## Definition
The North Star, formally known as Polaris (Alpha Ursae Minoris), is the current northern pole star — a star that lies almost directly above Earth's geographic North Pole, making it appear nearly stationary in the sky while all other stars appear to rotate around it. Due to Earth's axial precession, this role shifts between different stars over a ~25,770-year cycle; Polaris will hold the position until around 3100 AD. [Wikipedia: Polaris; Wikipedia: Pole Star]

## Full Content

### Identity and Location
- Polaris (α UMi) is a star in the constellation Ursa Minor, located at the end of the handle of the "Little Dipper" asterism [Britannica]
- Currently lies approximately 0.66° from the celestial north pole — close enough that all circumpolar northern stars appear to rotate around it during the night [Wikipedia: Polaris]
- Visible to the naked eye at apparent magnitude ~1.98; not the brightest star in the sky (that is Sirius), but uniquely fixed in direction [Wikipedia: Polaris]
- At northern latitudes, the altitude of Polaris above the horizon equals the observer's geographic latitude — a crucial navigational property [Wikipedia: Pole Star]

### Physical Properties
- Primary component (Polaris Aa): a yellow supergiant of spectral type F7Ib, surface temperature ~6,015 K [Wikipedia: Polaris]
- Luminosity ~1,260 times the Sun's; mass ~5.13 M☉; radius ~46 R☉ (measured by Evans et al. 2024 using CHARA Array interferometry) [Wikipedia: Polaris; Evans et al. 2024]
- Triple star system: Polaris Aa (primary supergiant) + Polaris Ab (close F6 main-sequence companion, ~30-year orbit) + Polaris B (distant F3 main-sequence star, separated by ~2,400 AU) [Wikipedia: Polaris]
- Polaris B was first identified as a companion by William Herschel in 1779; it can be resolved with a small telescope [Wikipedia: Polaris]

### Cepheid Variable Nature
- Polaris Aa is a classical Cepheid variable — a pulsating yellow supergiant whose brightness and size oscillate on a precise, repeatable period [Wikipedia: Polaris]
- Pulsation period: ~3.97 days; the amplitude of variation is small (~0.03 magnitudes), imperceptible to the naked eye [Wikipedia: Polaris; Britannica]
- Classification: Population I classical Cepheid (first-overtone pulsator according to Evans et al. 2024) [Wikipedia: Polaris]
- Scientific importance: Polaris is the **closest Cepheid variable to Earth**, making it a foundational calibration object for the cosmic distance ladder — the chain of methods astronomers use to measure distances across the universe [Wikipedia: Polaris; Britannica]
- The pulsation amplitude of Polaris has been changing over decades, and its period has been lengthening — both anomalous behaviours compared to typical Cepheids, subject of ongoing research [Wikipedia: Polaris]

### Pole Star Role and Precession
- Earth's axis of rotation points toward Polaris almost exactly (within ~0.66°), causing all other stars to appear to rotate around it over the course of a night [Wikipedia: Pole Star]
- This alignment is not permanent: Earth's rotation axis wobbles like a spinning top due to gravitational torques from the Moon and Sun — a 25,770-year cycle called **axial precession** [Wikipedia: Pole Star]
- Polaris will reach its closest approach to the pole (~0.45°) in February 2102, then will gradually drift away [Wikipedia: Pole Star]
- Past and future pole stars:
  - Past: Thuban (α Draconis, ~3000 BC), Kochab (β UMi, ~1700 BC–300 AD)
  - Future: Gamma Cephei (~3100 AD), Deneb (~9800 AD), Vega (~14,500 AD) [Wikipedia: Pole Star]
- The southern celestial pole has no bright counterpart: Polaris Australis (Sigma Octantis) is magnitude 5.5 and barely visible [Wikipedia: Pole Star]

### Navigation and Cultural Significance
- Mariners, travellers, and astronomers have used Polaris for navigation for millennia: it indicates geographic north and enables latitude determination without instruments [Wikipedia: Pole Star]
- The Phoenicians used Ursa Minor (with Polaris) for navigation; Greek sailors used Ursa Major [Wikipedia: Pole Star]
- During the era of transatlantic navigation, the "altitude of Polaris" method provided latitude; this was computationally simpler than solar noon methods [Wikipedia: Pole Star]
- Hindu tradition personified the pole star as Dhruva (meaning "fixed"), symbolising constancy and devotion [Wikipedia: Pole Star]
- In China, the pole star was called 天樞 (Tiānshū, "Heaven's Pivot") [Wikipedia: Pole Star]
- Polaris appears in an enormous number of cultural traditions as a symbol of constancy and direction

### Recent Research
- 2024: Evans et al. used the CHARA Array (long-baseline optical interferometry) to measure Polaris Aa's angular diameter directly, yielding a radius of ~46 R☉ and refining the mass estimate. Possible surface brightness features (bright and dark patches) were detected, requiring follow-up observations. The study supports a first-overtone pulsation interpretation [Wikipedia: Polaris citing Evans et al. 2024, ApJ 962, 159]
- Period changes: Polaris's pulsation period has increased from ~3.96 days in 1900 to ~3.97 days currently — a known puzzle for stellar pulsation theory [Wikipedia: Polaris]

## Historical Context

- Ancient Egyptians (Old Kingdom, ~2800–2500 BC) used Thuban as their pole star; Egyptian pyramid shafts align with the pole of that era, not with Polaris [Wikipedia: Pole Star]
- Polaris was not always recognised as the pole star: in Greek antiquity, the pole was near Kochab (β UMi) and Polaris was known as Phoenice or Kynosoura ("dog's tail") in Ursa Minor [Wikipedia: Pole Star]
- By ~800–900 AD, precession had brought Polaris close enough to the pole to be useful for navigation [Wikipedia: Pole Star]
- Medieval Islamic astronomers used Polaris ("al-Jady") and developed precise methods for latitude finding [Wikipedia: Pole Star]
- Polaris's companion Polaris B was discovered by William Herschel in 1779 [Wikipedia: Polaris]
- The inner companion Polaris Ab was discovered via spectroscopy — its 30-year orbital period was measured from radial velocity variations [Wikipedia: Polaris]
- Name "Polaris" derives from Latin stella polaris ("pole star"), introduced in Renaissance astronomy [Wikipedia: Polaris]
- The star's variable nature was established in the late 19th and early 20th centuries alongside the development of Cepheid theory by Henrietta Swan Leavitt (1912) who found the period–luminosity relation for Cepheids [general astronomical history]

## Sub-types / Classifications

**Star system components:**
- **Polaris Aa** — Primary: F7Ib yellow supergiant, classical Cepheid variable, the dominant light source
- **Polaris Ab** — Inner companion: F6V main-sequence star, orbits Aa with period ~30 years, detected spectroscopically; not individually resolved from Aa without very high resolution
- **Polaris B** — Outer companion: F3V main-sequence star, separated ~2,400 AU, visually resolvable with amateur telescope; co-moving with A

**Cepheid classification:**
- Population I classical Cepheid (metal-rich, evolved supergiant)
- Short period (~4 days) puts it in the first-overtone pulsator range (per Evans et al. 2024)
- Distinguished from Type II Cepheids (W Virginis stars) by being younger and more luminous

**Pole star status:**
- Northern pole star (most prominent and navigationally useful of historical pole stars due to its proximity to the pole and naked-eye visibility)
- Not a permanent designation — shifts over the precession cycle
