## Definition
An annular solar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes directly between Earth and the Sun but, being near its farthest point from Earth (apogee), appears too small to cover the solar disk completely, leaving a bright ring — or annulus — of sunlight visible around the Moon's silhouette. [NED/IPAC Glossary; NASA GSF Eclipse Glossary]

## Full Content

- An annular eclipse is a type of solar eclipse in which the Moon moves directly in front of the Sun but cannot fully cover it because it is too far from Earth; the Sun's outer rim remains visible as a complete, bright ring called the annulus. [NED/IPAC Glossary]
- The word "annular" derives from the Latin *annulus* (ring); the event is popularly called the "ring of fire." [timeanddate.com]
- The cause is the Moon's elliptical orbit: when an eclipse coincides with the Moon near apogee (~405,700 km from Earth), the Moon's apparent angular diameter (~29′26″) is smaller than the Sun's (~31′28″–32′42″), so the Moon cannot blot out the photosphere. [Wikipedia; NASA GSF Eclipse Glossary]
- Observers within the **antumbra** — the extension of the Moon's shadow cone beyond its tip — see the annular phase. From the antumbra the Sun appears larger than the Moon, which is silhouetted against it. This contrasts with the **umbra**, where total eclipse occurs, and the **penumbra**, where only partial eclipse is seen. [NASA GSF Eclipse Glossary]
- Eclipse **magnitude** (fraction of the Sun's diameter occulted by the Moon) is less than 1.000 for all annular eclipses; the closer to 1.000, the thinner and more dramatic the ring. [NASA GSF Eclipse Glossary]
- Because the Moon's average apparent size is about 2.1% smaller than the Sun's, roughly 60% of all central solar eclipses are annular rather than total. [Wikipedia]
- The antumbral shadow track sweeps west to east across Earth at roughly 1–3 km/s. The path width is typically 100–300 km — often wider than the umbral track of a total eclipse, because the antumbra expands with distance beyond the Moon's shadow tip. [NASA; Wikipedia]
- **Duration of annularity** (the period between second and third contact, when the Moon is fully on the solar disk) ranges from under one second to a maximum of 12 minutes 29 seconds, depending on the Moon's distance and the observer's location along the path. [NASA GSF Eclipse Glossary]
- Outside the antumbral path, observers in the penumbra see a **partial eclipse** — part of the solar disk is obscured, but no ring is visible. [NASA GSF Eclipse Glossary]
- A **hybrid eclipse** (annular-total eclipse) shifts between annular and total phases along its path due to Earth's curvature, some observers standing just within the umbral tip and others just beyond it. Purely annular eclipses remain annular throughout. [Wikipedia]
- During annularity the Sun is never fully blocked, so the **photosphere remains dangerously bright**; eclipse glasses or a solar filter must be worn throughout — it is never safe to view an annular eclipse with the naked eye, unlike the brief totality phase of a total eclipse. [NASA; timeanddate.com]
- Annularity can be observed through a pinhole projector, welder's glass (shade 14), or certified ISO 12312-2 solar eclipse glasses. [NASA]
- Annular eclipses occur roughly once every one to two years somewhere on Earth, though many tracks cross ocean or polar regions. [NASA / timeanddate.com]
- Notable recent examples: **October 14, 2023** (visible from Oregon to Texas and through Central/South America — the first annular eclipse over the contiguous US in a decade); **February 17, 2026** (path across Antarctica); **February 6, 2027** (path across Africa and South America). [NASA / nineplanets.org]
- The 2012 May 20 annular eclipse crossed East Asia and the western United States with a path width of 240–300 km and a maximum annularity of 5 minutes 46 seconds, one of the longest in recent memory. [NASA GSF 2012 eclipse page]
- Historically, ancient sources conflated annular and total eclipses; distinguishing the two scientifically required understanding lunar distance variation, which became clearer in the 19th century with improved observational records and eventually with photography (first eclipse photograph: 1851). [Wikipedia]
- The earliest solar eclipse possibly identified as annular in historical records is sometimes cited as the February 17, 478 BC event at Sardis, matched by astronomer J.R. Hind to an annular eclipse recorded by Herodotus. [timeanddate.com eclipse history]

## Historical Context

- Ancient Babylonian and Chinese astronomers tracked solar eclipses from at least 2500 BCE but did not systematically distinguish annular from total. The concept requires understanding that the Moon's apparent size varies with its orbital distance. [Wikipedia; NASA eclipse history]
- The **Saros cycle** (18 years 11 days 8 hours, or ~6,585.3 days) governs eclipse recurrence; an annular eclipse in one Saros series may become a total eclipse in the next or following series as the Moon's node geometry shifts. Each Saros series lasts 12–13 centuries and contains 70+ eclipses. [NASA GSF Eclipse Glossary]
- Herodotus recorded a solar eclipse that astronomer John Russell Hind later matched to an annular eclipse visible from Sardis on February 17, 478 BC — one of the earliest events tentatively identified as annular. [timeanddate.com eclipse history]
- Photography made detailed study of annular eclipses possible from the mid-19th century. The first photographic record of a solar eclipse was made in 1851. [Wikipedia]
- The October 14, 2023 annular eclipse was the first annular eclipse to cross the contiguous United States since May 20, 2012, generating major public interest and scientific observations. [NASA; Space.com]

## Sub-types / Classifications

- **Annular eclipse (pure)**: The antumbra reaches Earth and the eclipse is annular along the entire central track. Eclipse magnitude < 1.000 throughout. [NASA GSF Eclipse Glossary]
- **Hybrid eclipse (annular-total)**: Due to Earth's curvature, the umbra reaches Earth at some points along the path (producing total eclipse) while the antumbra grazes Earth at other points (producing annular eclipse). Observers near the ends of the central path see annular phases; observers near the centre of the path see totality. Hybrid eclipses are rare — roughly 5% of central eclipses. [Wikipedia; NASA]
- **Near-annular eclipse**: Eclipse magnitude is very close to 1.000; annularity lasts only a fraction of a second and the ring is extremely thin. This occurs when the Moon's apparent size almost exactly matches the Sun's. The May 9, 1948 annular eclipse had annularity lasting only seconds due to near-equal angular diameters. [NASA]
