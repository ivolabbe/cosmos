# Blackbody — Article Spec

## Category
Physics (Thermal Radiation, Quantum Mechanics)

## Definition (1–2 sentences)
A blackbody is an idealized theoretical object that absorbs all electromagnetic radiation incident upon it at every wavelength. In thermal equilibrium at a given temperature, a blackbody emits radiation with a characteristic spectrum that depends only on that temperature, not on the object's material, shape, or surface properties.

## Full Content (ALL facts needed for 200–400 words)

### The Ideal Blackbody Concept
A perfect blackbody absorbs 100% of all radiation striking it across all wavelengths. Real objects don't exist as perfect blackbodies, but materials like graphite and lamp black (carbon black) approximate ideal behavior closely with emissivities exceeding 0.95. Scientists reproduce blackbody radiation in laboratories using a hohlraum—a small hole drilled in a large cavity maintained at constant temperature. Radiation entering the hole bounces internally multiple times, ensuring nearly complete absorption regardless of wavelength.

### Kirchhoff's Pioneering Work (1860)
Gustav Kirchhoff introduced the term "blackbody" in 1860 and discovered the fundamental principle: at thermal equilibrium, an object's power radiated must equal its power absorbed. Kirchhoff recognized that the ratio of emission to absorption depends only on temperature and wavelength, not on the object's material. He postulated the existence of a universal function describing blackbody emission but could not determine its precise mathematical form. His work established that understanding blackbody radiation was central to understanding thermal radiation broadly.

### Planck's Quantum Breakthrough (1900)
Max Planck solved the blackbody puzzle on October 19, 1900, using a revolutionary assumption: radiation is emitted in discrete packets (quanta) proportional to frequency. Planck's law describes the spectral density of radiation emitted by a blackbody at temperature T. Unlike classical predictions that predicted infinite energy at high frequencies (the "ultraviolet catastrophe"), Planck's distribution correctly matches observations. His work introduced the concept of energy quanta and fundamentally launched quantum mechanics. Planck's constant h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s directly links energy to frequency.

### Wien's Displacement Law (1893)
Wilhelm Wien derived in 1893 that the peak wavelength of blackbody emission is inversely proportional to temperature: λ_peak = b / T, where b (Wien's displacement constant) = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K. This explains why heated metal progresses from deep red (lower temperature) to orange, yellow, and finally blue-white (higher temperature). The Sun's surface temperature (~5,778 K) peaks near 500 nm in green light. Cool stars like Betelgeuse (~3,800 K) emit mostly infrared radiation. Wien's law allows astronomers to estimate stellar surface temperatures by observing their color (peak wavelength).

### Stefan-Boltzmann Law: Total Power
The Stefan-Boltzmann law (derived from Planck's equation by integration) states that total radiant power emitted is proportional to the fourth power of absolute temperature: P = σAT⁴, where σ (Stefan-Boltzmann constant) = 5.670374 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴), A is surface area, and T is temperature in Kelvin. This means doubling absolute temperature increases total emitted power by a factor of 16. At room temperature (300 K), a 1 m² blackbody emits ~460 W. This law explains why hotter objects dominate radiation in mixed-temperature systems.

### Applications to Stars
Although stars are not perfect blackbodies (they have absorption lines, rotation, and non-uniform surfaces), blackbody radiation provides an excellent first approximation for stellar spectra. Stellar effective temperature is defined as the temperature of a perfect blackbody radiating the same total power as the star. The Hertzsprung-Russell diagram plots stars by luminosity and effective temperature, revealing their evolutionary stages. Color-magnitude diagrams rely on Wien's law to infer temperatures from observed colors.

### The Cosmic Microwave Background: The Perfect Blackbody
The cosmic microwave background (CMB) is the afterglow of the Big Bang, originating ~380,000 years after the Big Bang when the universe cooled enough for electrons and protons to combine into neutral hydrogen. The CMB has the most perfect blackbody spectrum ever measured. The COBE satellite's FIRAS instrument measured T_CMB = 2.72548 ± 0.00057 K with extraordinary precision—the spectrum matches a perfect blackbody to better than 1 part in 10,000. This precision is the foundation for modern cosmology, confirming the Big Bang model and allowing precise measurements of cosmological parameters. The CMB monopole (average temperature) is 2.725 K; minute temperature variations (1 part in 100,000) encode information about the early universe's structure.

### Historical Significance
The blackbody radiation problem was one of physics' greatest unsolved puzzles in the late 19th century. Classical physics completely failed to explain observations—it predicted absurd results like infinite energy at ultraviolet frequencies. Planck's solution by quantizing energy was so revolutionary that it marked the birth of quantum mechanics. This single problem, solved by one assumption, transformed all of physics and fundamentally changed how we understand light, matter, and reality itself.

## Historical Context

- **1860**: Gustav Kirchhoff introduces term "blackbody," formulates reciprocal law (emission power = absorption power at equilibrium). Recognizes blackbody spectrum is universal but unknown.
- **1893**: Wilhelm Wien derives displacement law relating peak wavelength to temperature using thermodynamic reasoning.
- **1879**: Stefan and Boltzmann derive fourth-power law relating total power to temperature.
- **October 19, 1900**: Max Planck presents his radiation law, postulating energy quantization (quanta). This date marks the birth of quantum mechanics.
- **1964**: Arno Penzias and Robert Woodrow Wilson accidentally discover the cosmic microwave background using a Dicke radiometer at Bell Labs.
- **1989–1993**: COBE satellite measures CMB spectrum to unprecedented precision with FIRAS instrument.
- **2006**: John Mather and George Smoot awarded Nobel Prize in Physics for CMB measurements.

## Sub-types / Classifications

1. **Ideal (Perfect) Blackbody**: Theoretical object absorbing all incident radiation at all wavelengths and emitting according to Planck's law.
2. **Gray Body**: Object with constant emissivity less than 1.0, but independent of wavelength and temperature.
3. **Selective Emitter**: Object with emissivity that varies with wavelength (e.g., real surfaces at room temperature).
4. **Hohlraum (Cavity Radiator)**: Laboratory apparatus approximating blackbody via small hole in large isothermal cavity.
5. **Astrophysical Approximations**: Stars, planets, dust grains often treated as blackbodies for first-order analysis despite deviations.
6. **The Cosmic Microwave Background**: The most perfect blackbody spectrum in nature (source: COBE FIRAS).
