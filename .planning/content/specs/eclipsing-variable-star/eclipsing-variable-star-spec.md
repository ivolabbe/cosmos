# Eclipsing Variable Star — Article Spec

## Category
stars

## Definition
An eclipsing variable star (also called an eclipsing binary) is a binary star system whose orbital plane is oriented nearly edge-on to the line of sight from Earth, so that the two stars alternately pass in front of one another, periodically dimming the total light reaching an observer. The brightness changes are purely geometric — neither star itself is intrinsically variable — making eclipsing binaries among the most powerful tools for determining fundamental stellar properties such as mass, radius, and luminosity.

## Full Content

### Overview
- Eclipsing variable stars are binary systems, not single pulsating stars; the variability arises from mutual eclipses rather than from intrinsic stellar changes [Britannica: eclipsing-variable-star]
- First correctly explained by John Goodricke in 1782 for the prototype Algol (β Persei); first variability of Algol recorded by Geminiano Montanari in 1667 [Wikipedia: Algol variable]
- Thousands of eclipsing binaries are now known; the General Catalogue of Variable Stars (2003 edition) lists 3,554 recognised Algol-type binaries alone [Wikipedia: Algol variable]
- The GCVS classification scheme assigns the prefix "E" and subdivides systems into three main subtypes: EA (Algol-type), EB (Beta Lyrae-type), and EW (W Ursae Majoris-type) [CFA SDU: Eclipsing Binaries; AAVSO]
- Orbital periods range from roughly 2.8 hours (HW Virginis, 0.1167 d) to decades (ε Aurigae, ~27 yr; 9,892 d) [Wikipedia: Algol variable]

### How Eclipsing Binaries Work
- When the cooler star passes in front of the hotter star (as seen from Earth), the system's total brightness drops to its deepest level — the **primary minimum** [CFA SDU: Eclipsing Binaries]
- Half an orbit later, when the hotter star moves in front of the cooler star, a shallower dip occurs — the **secondary minimum** [CFA SDU: Eclipsing Binaries]
- The depth of each minimum depends on the size, temperature, and surface brightness of each star, and on the orbital inclination [Britannica: Star — Eclipsing binaries]
- Between minima, the system brightness may be constant (well-separated stars) or continuously varying (gravitationally distorted, tidally elongated stars) [CFA SDU: Eclipsing Binaries]
- Duration and shape of ingress and egress encode the ratio of stellar radii to the orbital separation [Britannica: Star — Eclipsing binaries]

### Types of Eclipsing Binaries

#### EA — Algol Type (Detached or Semi-detached)
- Named after the prototype **Algol** (β Persei); the two stars are well-separated and near-spherical [CFA SDU: Eclipsing Binaries]
- Light curve shows a constant brightness between eclipses and sharply defined ingress/egress, making the start and end of each eclipse precisely determinable [CFA SDU: Eclipsing Binaries; Wikipedia: Algol variable]
- Primary and secondary minima differ markedly in depth [CFA SDU: Eclipsing Binaries]
- Components may be in a detached configuration (both inside their Roche lobes) or semi-detached (one evolved star filling its Roche lobe) [Wikipedia: Algol variable]
- Period range: a few hours to years [Wikipedia: Algol variable]

#### EB — Beta Lyrae Type (Semi-detached)
- Named after **Beta Lyrae** (β Lyr); components are so close that tidal distortion stretches each star into an ellipsoidal shape [CFA SDU: Eclipsing Binaries; Wikipedia: Beta Lyrae variable]
- Light curve is continuously varying — no flat baseline between minima — because the projected area of the tidally elongated stars changes at every orbital phase [Wikipedia: Beta Lyrae variable]
- Primary and secondary minima differ in depth; one eclipse is deeper [CFA SDU: Eclipsing Binaries]
- Orbital period of Beta Lyrae itself: 12.9414 d (increasing by ~19 s/yr due to mass transfer) [Wikipedia: Beta Lyrae]
- Spectral types of EB components typically B–A; Beta Lyrae Aa1 is classified B6–8 II [Wikipedia: Beta Lyrae]
- Ongoing mass transfer from the cooler, more evolved primary to the secondary via an accretion disk characterises Beta Lyrae itself [Wikipedia: Beta Lyrae]

#### EW — W Ursae Majoris Type (Contact Binary)
- Named after **W Ursae Majoris** (W UMa); both stars share a common convective envelope (the stars are "in contact") [Wikipedia: W Ursae Majoris variable; AAVSO: vsots_wuma]
- Despite different masses, the two components have nearly identical surface temperatures because energy flows freely through the shared envelope from the more massive star to the less massive one [Wikipedia: W Ursae Majoris variable]
- Light curves show nearly equal minima depths and a continuously varying brightness; discrete eclipse boundaries are absent [Wikipedia: W Ursae Majoris variable; OGLE Atlas: EW]
- Short periods: typically 0.22–0.8 d; spectral types F, G, or K [Wikipedia: W Ursae Majoris variable]
- Divided into A-type (hotter, F–A, period 0.4–0.8 d) and W-type (cooler, G–K, period 0.22–0.4 d) subtypes [Wikipedia: W Ursae Majoris variable]

### Algol as Prototype

- **Algol** (β Persei) is a triple star system in Perseus; the eclipsing pair consists of a hot B8V primary (T ≈ 12,000 K, luminosity ≈ 98 L☉) and a cooler K2IV subgiant secondary [AAVSO: vsots_betaper; Wikipedia: Algol]
- Visible to the naked eye; magnitude ranges from 2.1 at maximum to 3.4 at primary minimum over a period of **2.867315 days** [AAVSO: vsots_betaper]
- Primary eclipse lasts roughly 10 hours and is partial (minimum is rounded, not flat) [AAVSO: vsots_betaper]
- Known since antiquity as the "Demon Star" (from the Arabic *ra's al-ghūl*, "head of the ogre"); its periodic dimming was apparently noticed in ancient Egypt [Wikipedia: Algol]
- The **Algol paradox**: the subgiant K2IV secondary appears more evolved than the main-sequence B8V primary despite (presumably) forming together; resolved by mass transfer — the original more-massive star lost mass to its companion, reversing which star is now the heavier one [Wikipedia: Algol variable]

### Deriving Stellar Masses and Radii

- Eclipsing binaries are uniquely powerful because combining eclipse photometry with spectroscopic radial velocities yields stellar masses and radii with almost no reliance on theoretical models [Coughlin 2010, arXiv:1004.1395; Britannica: Star — Eclipsing binaries]
- From the **light curve** alone, analysts extract: ratio of stellar radii, each radius in terms of orbital separation, ratio of surface luminosities, and orbital inclination [Britannica: Star — Eclipsing binaries]
- Adding **double-lined radial velocity curves** (spectroscopic binary) yields: the absolute orbital semi-major axis (via Kepler's third law), and from that both individual stellar masses and absolute radii [Britannica: Star — Eclipsing binaries; Coughlin 2010]
- Precision of 1% or better in mass and radius is achievable for well-studied detached eclipsing binaries (DEBs); these are used as benchmark calibrators for stellar evolution models [V1094 Tau: Marcussen & Albrecht 2022, A&A; Coughlin 2010]
- Eclipsing binaries thus provide **model-independent** stellar parameters across a wide range of masses, ages, chemical compositions, and evolutionary states, from pre-main-sequence stars to red giants and white dwarfs [ScienceDirect: Eclipsing Binary Star overview]

### Limb Darkening

- Stars are not uniformly bright disks: the centre of the stellar disk is hotter and brighter, while the limb (edge) appears darker because the line of sight there penetrates to higher, cooler atmospheric layers — an effect called **limb darkening** [Astronomy & Astrophysics: limb darkening coefficients 2013]
- During an eclipse, as one star's disk slides across its companion, the fraction of the companion's light that is blocked changes in a way that depends on where on the disk the occulting star is positioned, so limb darkening imprints a characteristic curvature on the ingress and egress portions of the light curve [Britannica: Eclipse — Eclipsing binary stars]
- Careful modelling of this curvature allows observers to measure the **limb-darkening coefficients** of the occulted star — quantities that constrain stellar atmosphere models [A&A: Claret 2011, gravity and limb-darkening coefficients]
- Modern analyses with Kepler and TESS photometry have revealed small but systematic discrepancies between observed limb-darkening coefficients and theoretical stellar atmosphere predictions, suggesting refinements are still needed [Maxted 2023, MNRAS 519; Nature Astronomy 2024: magnetic origin]

### Importance in Astrophysics
- Eclipsing binaries supply the most precise empirical mass-radius-luminosity calibrations for stars across the HR diagram, directly testing stellar structure and evolution theory [Coughlin 2010; ScienceDirect overview]
- They calibrate the **mass–luminosity relation** used to infer masses of non-eclipsing binaries and single stars [Britannica: Star — Eclipsing binaries]
- Surveys (Kepler, TESS, OGLE) have discovered thousands of new eclipsing binaries, enabling statistical studies of binary-star properties, mass-transfer evolution, and the population of contact binaries [ScienceDirect: deep learning classification 2021]
- The technique of detecting planet transits — used by Kepler and TESS to find thousands of exoplanets — is geometrically identical to eclipse detection in binary stars [general astrophysics]
- Eclipsing red giants with solar-like oscillations allow simultaneous asteroseismic and geometric characterisation — cross-checking two independent techniques [A&A: Brogaard et al. 2021]

## Historical Context

The periodic dimming of Algol was first documented by Geminiano Montanari in 1667, and ancient Egyptian records (Cairo Calendar, c. 1244 BCE) may preserve the earliest numerical record of its ~2.87-day cycle. John Goodricke, a deaf-mute amateur astronomer, proposed the eclipsing binary explanation in 1782 and correctly estimated the period — a remarkable achievement. The full geometrical framework for extracting stellar radii and orbital parameters from light curves was developed in the early 20th century, notably by Henry Norris Russell and Harlow Shapley. The discovery that combining radial velocities with photometry yields model-independent masses and radii revolutionised empirical stellar astrophysics, and eclipsing binaries remain the primary distance-independent calibrators for stellar evolution models.
