## Definition

Mizar (Zeta Ursae Majoris, ζ UMa) is a quadruple star system located in the handle of the Big Dipper in Ursa Major, historically significant as the first telescopic visual binary identified in Europe (1617) and the first spectroscopic binary whose orbit was published (1890), and famous for its naked-eye companion Alcor with which it forms a gravitationally bound senary (six-star) system.

## Full Content

- Mizar is designated ζ Ursae Majoris (Zeta Ursae Majoris), the central star in the handle of the Big Dipper (Plough) asterism, Ursa Major; it is the fourth-brightest star in that constellation with combined apparent magnitude 2.04 [Wikipedia]
- The system is a quadruple star: two visual components, Mizar A (ζ¹ UMa, mag 2.23) and Mizar B (ζ² UMa, mag 3.88), are separated by 14.4 arcseconds and are both themselves spectroscopic binaries [Wikipedia]
- Mizar A (components Aa + Ab) are nearly identical A2Vp dwarf stars, each with mass ~2.2 M☉, radius ~2.4 R☉, temperature ~9,000 K, and luminosity ~33 L☉; the "p" in the spectral type denotes chemical peculiarity with overabundances of strontium and silicon [Wikipedia; star-facts.com]
- Mizar A's orbital period is 20.54 days; the two components cannot be resolved visually and were detected only via periodic Doppler splitting of calcium absorption lines [Wikipedia]
- Mizar B is an Am (metallic-line) star, spectral type kA1h(eA)mA7IV-V, with a cooler (8,280 K) primary of 1.85 M☉ and a low-mass secondary of 0.25 M☉ with orbital period 175.06 days [Wikipedia]
- Mizar A and Mizar B orbit each other with a period estimated to exceed 5,000 years, at a minimum separation of ~380 AU [star-facts.com]
- Naked-eye companion Alcor (80 UMa) lies ~12 arcminutes (0.2°) from Mizar; Alcor A is a rapidly rotating A5Vn dwarf (mag 3.99, 1.84 M☉, ~14 L☉) and its red dwarf companion Alcor B (M3-4V, mag 8.8, 0.25 M☉) was discovered in 2009 [Wikipedia (Alcor)]
- Mizar and Alcor together form a confirmed gravitationally bound senary (six-star) system — the second-closest known six-star system after Castor — with gravitational binding assessed at 100% probability (2011) [Wikipedia (Mizar and Alcor)]
- The Mizar–Alcor linear separation is estimated at ~74,000 AU; both systems share common proper motion as members of the Ursa Major Moving Group, a loose stellar association sharing a common origin [Wikipedia (Mizar and Alcor); Wikipedia (Alcor)]
- Historically, the naked-eye pair Mizar–Alcor served as a test of sharp vision across many cultures; separating the two stars without optical aid was regarded by Arab, Roman, and other traditions as a standard eyesight test [Britannica; EarthSky]
- Traditional Arabic names call the pair the "Horse and Rider" (*al-Suha* for Alcor); in Hindu tradition they represent the sage Vasishtha (Mizar) and his devoted wife Arundhati (Alcor), still symbolically invoked at Indian wedding ceremonies [Wikipedia (Mizar and Alcor)]
- In Japanese folklore, Alcor is the "Lifespan Star" (寿命星) — believed to foretell death if the observer cannot see it [Wikipedia (Mizar and Alcor)]
- Mizar A was the first spectroscopic binary discovered: in 1887 Edward Charles Pickering (Harvard College Observatory) noticed periodic splitting of Mizar A's calcium absorption lines, implying Doppler shifts from two orbiting stars too close to resolve optically [Wikipedia; sheisanastronomer.org]
- Antonia Maury computed the orbital solution and published the first spectroscopic binary orbit in 1890 (period ~20.5 days); this opened the field of determining stellar masses and physical properties from spectral data [Wikipedia; sheisanastronomer.org]
- In 1996, the Navy Prototype Optical Interferometer (NPOI) directly resolved the two components of Mizar A for the first time [Wikipedia]
- Mizar B's spectroscopic binary nature was also established in the 19th century; F.G. Pease and J.A. Anderson later resolved Mizar B interferometrically with the 100-inch reflector at Mount Wilson Observatory [Astronomy Now]
- The visual binary Mizar A+B was first resolved telescopically in 1617 by Benedetto Castelli, who asked his teacher Galileo Galilei to observe it; Galileo produced a detailed record — likely the first documented telescopic double star in Europe [EarthSky; Wikipedia]
- Giovanni Battista Riccioli is often credited with formally cataloguing Mizar as a visual binary c. 1650 [Britannica]

## Historical Context

- **Pre-telescopic era**: The naked-eye pair Mizar–Alcor was well-known across many ancient cultures. Arab astronomers called them the "Horse and Rider" (*al-Suha* for Alcor). Roman legions reportedly used them as a vision test for soldiers. The pair features in Hindu, Japanese, and other traditions.
- **1617**: Benedetto Castelli (Italian mathematician and student of Galileo) became the first European to observe Mizar as a telescopic double, and asked Galileo Galilei to confirm the observation. Galileo documented the finding in detail — the likely first recorded telescopic double star in Europe.
- **c. 1650**: Giovanni Battista Riccioli formally catalogued Mizar as a visual binary and is often credited with the discovery of visual binary stars.
- **1887**: Edward Charles Pickering (Harvard College Observatory) discovered that Mizar A's calcium spectral lines periodically split, revealing it as the first spectroscopic binary.
- **1890**: Antonia Maury computed and published the orbital solution (period ~20.5 days) for Mizar A — the first published spectroscopic binary orbit. This marked the beginning of stellar mass determination via spectroscopy.
- **Late 19th century**: Mizar B also found to be a spectroscopic binary, making Mizar the first known quadruple star system.
- **1996**: The two components of Mizar A directly resolved via interferometry by the Navy Prototype Optical Interferometer.
- **2009**: Alcor's red dwarf companion (Alcor B) discovered, confirming the system as senary (six stars).
- **2011**: Gravitational binding of Mizar and Alcor confirmed at essentially 100% probability.

**Etymology**: "Mizar" derives from the Arabic *miʼzar* ("apron," "veil," "cloak," "wrapper"). The IAU formally assigns the name only to component Aa; in common usage it refers to the whole system.

## Sub-types / Classifications

The Mizar senary system contains stars of three distinct types:

| Component | Designation | Spectral Type | Key Characteristics |
|-----------|-------------|--------------|---------------------|
| Mizar Aa | ζ¹ UMa Aa | A2Vp | Chemically peculiar (Sr, Si overabundance); primary of spectroscopic binary Mizar A; period 20.5 d |
| Mizar Ab | ζ¹ UMa Ab | A2Vp | Nearly identical to Aa; too close to resolve visually |
| Mizar Ba | ζ² UMa Ba | kA1h(eA)mA7IV-V | Am (metallic-line) star; spectroscopic binary primary; period 175 d |
| Mizar Bb | ζ² UMa Bb | — | Low-mass companion (0.25 M☉); orbit inferred, not directly resolved |
| Alcor A | 80 UMa A | A5Vn | Rapidly rotating A-dwarf; most luminous of the three visually resolvable stars |
| Alcor B | 80 UMa B | M3-4V | Red dwarf; discovered 2009; separated by 1 arcsecond from Alcor A |

All six stars share common proper motion and membership in the **Ursa Major Moving Group** — a sparse stellar association of common origin ~370–420 Myr old.

## Handoff: Researcher → Writer

**Target length:** 200–400 words

**Key decisions:**
- Article focuses on Mizar as the primary topic, with Alcor treated as essential context (they form one system) rather than a separate article
- Historical firsts (first telescopic double in Europe, first spectroscopic binary) are the editorial hook — lead with significance
- The quadruple/senary system structure is the key physical fact and should be explained clearly without over-complicating the naming scheme (Aa, Ab, Ba, Bb)
- Antonia Maury's role deserves a sentence — she computed the orbit, not just Pickering who found the signal

**Rejected alternatives:**
- Not limiting to Mizar A only — the Alcor connection and the full senary system are the story
- Not emphasising Am (metallic-line) classification of Mizar B unless space allows — it is a secondary point

**Risks:**
- Naming can confuse readers: Mizar A vs Mizar Aa; Alcor vs Alcor B. Writer should use plain language ("the close pair Mizar A" rather than "ζ¹ UMa components Aa and Ab").
- Do not conflate Mizar A = first spectroscopic binary (1887) with Mizar A+B = first visual binary (1617). These are two distinct firsts.
- The 1617 telescopic discovery is attributed to Benedetto Castelli, not Galileo (who confirmed it). Britannica credits Riccioli (1650) as discovering visual binaries; both facts can coexist if worded carefully.

**Watch for:**
- "data are" not "data is"
- Magnitudes: Mizar A = 2.23, Mizar B = 3.88, combined system = 2.04, Alcor = 3.99 — keep these straight
- The Alcor–Mizar gravitational binding was confirmed in 2011 (not earlier); the pairing was famous long before the physics was confirmed

**Gaps:** None significant. All key facts sourced from multiple concordant references.
