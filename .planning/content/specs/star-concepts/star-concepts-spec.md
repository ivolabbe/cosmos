## Definition
A star is a luminous spheroid of plasma held together by self-gravity, generating energy through nuclear fusion in its core. Stars are characterised by a set of interconnected physical properties — mass, radius, luminosity, surface temperature, and chemical composition — that together determine their spectral type, evolutionary path, and ultimate fate.

## Full Content

### What is a star?
- A star is a self-gravitating ball of hot plasma sustaining nuclear fusion reactions in its core [Wikipedia/Star]
- Stars produce energy by fusing hydrogen into helium on the main sequence; more massive stars later fuse heavier elements up to iron [NASA Science/Stars]
- Observable universe contains an estimated 10²² to 10²⁴ stars; the Milky Way holds >100 billion [NASA Science/Stars]
- Current Milky Way stars are ~71% hydrogen and ~27% helium by mass, with <2% heavier elements [Wikipedia/Star]
- Plasma is held in hydrostatic equilibrium: gravity inward balanced by thermal pressure from fusion energy outward [Wikipedia/Star]

### Fundamental stellar properties
- **Mass** is the most important stellar property — it determines energy output, lifespan, and the star's ultimate fate [Britannica/Bulk stellar properties]
- **Luminosity (L)**: total energy emitted per second; the Sun's nominal luminosity is 3.828 × 10²⁶ W [Wikipedia/Star]
- **Radius (R)**: ranges from ~10 km (neutron stars) to ~900 million km (extreme supergiants); Sun = 6.957 × 10⁸ m [Wikipedia/Star]
- **Surface temperature (T_eff)**: effective temperature defined by L = 4πR²σT⁴; ranges from ~2,300 K (cool M dwarfs) to >50,000 K (O supergiants) [Britannica/Bulk stellar properties]
- These three quantities are linked by the Stefan–Boltzmann law: L = 4πR²σT⁴ [Britannica/Bulk stellar properties]

### Spectral classification (Harvard sequence)
- Stars are classified O, B, A, F, G, K, M from hottest to coolest; mnemonic "Oh Be A Fine Guy/Girl: Kiss Me!" [Wikipedia/Stellar classification]
- Each type is subdivided 0–9 (hottest to coolest within the class); the Sun is G2 [Wikipedia/Stellar classification]
- **O-type** (≥33,000 K): ionised helium lines dominant; blue; only 0.00003% of main-sequence stars; 20–100 M☉ [Wikipedia/Stellar classification]
- **B-type** (10,000–33,000 K): neutral helium + hydrogen absorption; blue-white; 4–20 M☉ [Wikipedia/Stellar classification]
- **A-type** (7,300–10,000 K): strongest Balmer hydrogen lines; white; 2–4 M☉ [Wikipedia/Stellar classification]
- **F-type** (6,000–7,300 K): hydrogen + ionised calcium; yellow-white; 1.05–2 M☉ [astronomynotes.com]
- **G-type** (5,300–6,000 K): ionised calcium strongest + neutral hydrogen; yellow; 0.8–1.05 M☉; includes the Sun [Wikipedia/Stellar classification]
- **K-type** (3,900–5,300 K): neutral metals (Ca, Fe); orange; 0.5–0.8 M☉; ~12% of main-sequence stars [Wikipedia/Stellar classification]
- **M-type** (2,300–3,900 K): molecular bands (TiO, VO); red; 0.08–0.5 M☉; ~76% of main-sequence stars [Wikipedia/Stellar classification]
- Hotter types also exist: W (Wolf-Rayet, ≥25,000 K); cooler sub-stellar types L and T define brown dwarfs [Wikipedia/Stellar classification]

### Luminosity classes (Morgan–Keenan system)
- Added to spectral type as Roman numerals; based on pressure-sensitive spectral line widths that distinguish giants from dwarfs at the same temperature [Wikipedia/Stellar classification]
- **Ia/Ib**: luminous/normal supergiants; **II**: bright giants; **III**: normal giants; **IV**: subgiants; **V**: main-sequence dwarfs; **VI/sd**: subdwarfs; **VII/D**: white dwarfs [Wikipedia/Stellar classification]
- Sun's full MK classification: G2V (G2 spectral type, V = dwarf/main sequence) [Wikipedia/Stellar classification]

### The Hertzsprung–Russell (HR) diagram
- A plot of stellar luminosity (or absolute magnitude) against surface temperature (or spectral type); temperature increases to the left on the horizontal axis [Wikipedia/HR diagram]
- ~90% of all known stars lie on the diagonal **main sequence**, from hot massive O stars (upper left) to cool low-mass M dwarfs (lower right) [Wikipedia/HR diagram]
- Upper right: **red giant** and **red supergiant** branches — evolved stars with inert or shell-burning cores, expanded radii, and cool surfaces [Wikipedia/HR diagram]
- Lower left: **white dwarfs** — very common but intrinsically faint stellar remnants [Wikipedia/HR diagram]
- **Instability strip**: diagonal region hosting pulsating variable stars such as Cepheids and RR Lyrae [Wikipedia/HR diagram]
- A star's position on the main sequence is set almost entirely by its mass [Wikipedia/Main sequence]
- Created independently by Ejnar Hertzsprung (1911) and Henry Norris Russell (1913) [Wikipedia/HR diagram]

### Mass–luminosity relation (main sequence only)
- L/L☉ ≈ 0.23(M/M☉)^2.3 for M < 0.43 M☉ [Wikipedia/Mass–luminosity relation]
- L/L☉ ≈ (M/M☉)^4 for 0.43–2 M☉ [Wikipedia/Mass–luminosity relation]
- L/L☉ ≈ 1.4(M/M☉)^3.5 for 2–55 M☉ [Wikipedia/Mass–luminosity relation]
- L/L☉ ≈ 32000 (M/M☉) for M > 55 M☉ (near Eddington limit, luminosity nearly proportional to mass) [Wikipedia/Mass–luminosity relation]
- Relation does NOT apply to red giants or white dwarfs [Wikipedia/Mass–luminosity relation]
- Eddington demonstrated this theoretically in 1924 from stellar interior models [Wikipedia/Mass–luminosity relation]

### Stellar mass measurement
- Direct masses require binary star systems; Kepler's third law: (m₁ + m₂) = a³/P² (a in AU, P in years, yielding M in M☉) [Britannica/Bulk stellar properties]
- **Visual binaries**: orbital motion directly resolved; **spectroscopic binaries**: radial-velocity variations betray unseen companion; **eclipsing binaries**: inclination data allow individual masses [Britannica/Bulk stellar properties]

### Stellar lifetimes
- Main sequence lifetime τ ≈ 10¹⁰ yr × (M/M☉)^−2.5 [Wikipedia/Main sequence]
- O-type stars (~20 M☉): a few million years; Sun: ~10 billion years; extreme red dwarfs (0.08 M☉): ~12 trillion years [Wikipedia/Star; Wikipedia/Main sequence]
- Sun is ~4.5 Gyr into a ~10 Gyr main sequence lifetime [Wikipedia/Main sequence]

### Stellar formation
- Stars form from gravitational collapse of molecular clouds (10³–10⁷ M☉, up to hundreds of light-years across) [NASA Science/Stars]
- Collapsing cloud fragments heat up to form protostars; when core temperature reaches ~10 million K, hydrogen fusion ignites and the protostar joins the main sequence [NASA Science/Stars]
- Formation timescale: ~10 Myr for Sun-like stars, ~100 Myr for red dwarfs [Wikipedia/Star]

### Stellar evolution endpoints
- **Stars < ~8 M☉**: expand to red giant/AGB, shed a planetary nebula, leave a carbon–oxygen white dwarf supported by electron degeneracy pressure [Wikipedia/Stellar evolution]
- **Stars 8–~20 M☉**: core collapse supernova → neutron star (~10 km radius), supported by neutron degeneracy pressure [Wikipedia/Stellar evolution]
- **Stars > ~20 M☉**: if remnant core mass > 2–3 M☉, collapse continues to a black hole [Wikipedia/Stellar evolution]
- Material ejected in supernova enriches the interstellar medium, seeding future generations of stars [NASA Science/Stars]

## Historical Context

- **1890s–1901**: Harvard College Observatory astronomers — including Williamina Fleming, Antonia Maury, and Annie Jump Cannon — classify stellar spectra from photographs; Cannon reorganises the lettered sequence into O, B, A, F, G, K, M order (dropping unused letters) by 1901, establishing the modern Harvard spectral sequence [Wikipedia/Stellar classification]
- **1906**: Ejnar Hertzsprung notices that K and M stars separate into two distinct luminosity groups — what we now call dwarfs and giants [WebSearch]
- **1911–1913**: Hertzsprung and Henry Norris Russell independently publish luminosity–temperature diagrams for stars; the combined plot becomes the HR diagram [Wikipedia/HR diagram]
- **1924**: Arthur Eddington derives the mass–luminosity relation theoretically from stellar interior models, confirming the physical basis of stellar brightness [Wikipedia/Mass–luminosity relation]
- **1943**: William Wilson Morgan and Philip Childs Keenan introduce the two-dimensional MK (Morgan–Keenan) system, adding luminosity classes (I–V) to the Harvard temperature classes [Wikipedia/Stellar classification]
- **1950s–present**: quantum mechanics and nuclear physics explain why spectral type indicates temperature via ionisation equilibrium; stellar evolution models are confirmed by observations of star clusters [Wikipedia/Stellar classification]

### HR diagram regions
- **Main sequence** (class V): ~90% of all stars; hydrogen-burning dwarfs
- **Giant branch** (classes II–III): evolved stars with expanded radii; hydrogen-shell or helium-core burning
- **Supergiant region** (classes Ia/Ib): very rare; evolved massive stars with extreme luminosities
- **White dwarf region** (class D): stellar remnants; very common but intrinsically faint
- **Instability strip**: variable stars (Cepheids, RR Lyrae) that pulsate in brightness
