## Definition

A P Cygni star is a luminous blue variable (LBV) hypergiant whose spectrum displays the eponymous P Cygni profile: a distinctive combination of a broad emission line and an adjacent blueshifted absorption trough in the same spectral line, produced by a powerful, rapidly expanding stellar wind. More broadly, any star whose spectrum shows this profile — indicating an outflowing gaseous envelope — may be described as exhibiting "P Cygni characteristics," though the term is anchored to the prototype star P Cygni (34 Cygni) in the constellation Cygnus.

## Full Content

### The Star P Cygni

- P Cygni (also catalogued as 34 Cygni) is a luminous blue variable (LBV) hypergiant in the constellation Cygnus, roughly 5,300 light-years (1,610 ± 180 parsecs) from Earth. [Wikipedia: P Cygni]
- It is among the most luminous stars in the Milky Way, with a luminosity of approximately 610,000 times that of the Sun. [Wikipedia: P Cygni]
- Its spectral type is B1–2 Ia-0ep (sometimes written B1 Iapeq), placing it at the boundary between B supergiants and hypergiants. [Wikipedia: P Cygni]
- The star has a mass of ~37 solar masses, a radius of ~76 solar radii, and a surface temperature of about 18,700 K. [Wikipedia: P Cygni]
- Its apparent magnitude is roughly 4.82, making it visible to the naked eye, though it has varied from magnitude 3 to 6 over its recorded history. Absolute magnitude: −7.9. [Wikipedia: P Cygni]
- P Cygni is losing mass through a powerful stellar wind at high velocity. Its projected rotational velocity is 35 km/s. [Wikipedia: P Cygni]
- In 2021, interferometric observations confirmed a companion: a B-type star approximately 4.3 magnitudes fainter than the primary, separated by 13.0 ± 0.1 milliarcseconds and orbiting on a ~7-year period. [Wikipedia: P Cygni; Mahy et al. 2022]
- The H-alpha emission region of the stellar wind has an angular diameter of 5.64 ± 0.21 milliarcseconds, corresponding to ~25 stellar radii at the star's distance. [Wikipedia: P Cygni profile]

### The P Cygni Profile (Spectral Feature)

- A P Cygni profile consists of two components superimposed on the same spectral line: (1) a broad emission peak at or redward of the line's rest wavelength, and (2) a blueshifted absorption trough displaced to shorter wavelengths. [Wikipedia: P Cygni profile]
- The emission arises from the dense stellar wind surrounding the star — the wind gas re-emits radiation in all directions, producing isotropic emission. [Wikipedia: P Cygni profile]
- The absorption arises from the column of stellar wind material expanding directly toward the observer: this gas intercepts the stellar continuum and absorbs it at a shorter (blue-shifted) wavelength corresponding to the outflow velocity. [Wikipedia: P Cygni profile]
- The degree of blueshift of the absorption minimum provides a direct measurement of the wind's terminal velocity. For typical hot supergiants this is hundreds to ~2,000 km/s; for P Cygni itself the wind speed is several hundred km/s. [Wikipedia: Stellar wind]
- The profile is observed most prominently in hydrogen Balmer lines (especially H-alpha), as well as in UV resonance lines of C IV, Si IV, N V in hot stars and in other strong transitions depending on the star type. [Wikipedia: P Cygni profile]
- P Cygni profiles are a direct spectroscopic signature of an outflowing, not infalling, envelope — this distinguishes them from inverse P Cygni profiles (absorption redshifted relative to emission), which indicate infall and are seen in protostars and accreting systems. [Wikipedia: P Cygni profile]
- Although P Cygni profiles are often cited as indicators of luminous blue variables, they also occur in Wolf-Rayet stars, classical novae (especially during the expansion phase), some supernovae ejecta, Be stars during outbursts, and pre-main-sequence protostars. [Wikipedia: P Cygni profile; Wikipedia: Luminous blue variable]
- In supernovae spectroscopy, P Cygni profiles in specific lines are used to identify ejecta composition and measure expansion velocities. [Wikipedia: P Cygni profile]

### Physical Mechanism of LBV Winds

- LBVs like P Cygni are in a post-main-sequence evolutionary phase, burning hydrogen in a shell surrounding an inert helium core. [Wikipedia: P Cygni]
- The stellar wind is driven by radiation pressure on UV absorption lines of heavy elements (carbon, nitrogen, silicon) — the same line-driven mechanism operating in all hot OB stars, but much stronger due to extreme luminosity. [Wikipedia: Stellar wind]
- OB star mass-loss rates are typically below 10⁻⁶ solar masses per year, with terminal velocities of 1–2,000 km/s. LBVs can exceed these rates significantly during eruptions. [Wikipedia: Stellar wind]
- Past giant eruptions of P Cygni — estimated to have occurred ~20,000, ~2,100, and ~900 years ago — would have been equivalent to η Carinae–type events, expelling large shells of material. [Wikipedia: P Cygni]
- Massive stars can shed up to 50% of their total mass through stellar winds over their lifetimes, fundamentally altering their evolutionary pathway and final fate. [Wikipedia: Stellar wind]

### Observational Significance

- P Cygni profiles are one of the key tools in observational stellar astrophysics for diagnosing mass loss, outflow geometry, and wind velocity in a wide range of stellar types. [Wikipedia: P Cygni profile]
- They are also used in extragalactic astronomy and supernova science to measure ejecta velocities and identify composition from spectral lines. [Wikipedia: P Cygni profile]
- P Cygni itself serves as a photometric and spectroscopic standard for studying LBV behaviour, stellar winds, and the terminal stages of massive star evolution. [Wikipedia: P Cygni]

## Historical Context

- **1600, August 18**: Willem Janszoon Blaeu, a Dutch cartographer and astronomer, first recorded P Cygni when it suddenly brightened to 3rd magnitude, making it easily visible to the naked eye where previously it had been invisible. This event is now understood as a major LBV eruption. [Wikipedia: P Cygni]
- **1603**: Johann Bayer published his *Uranometria* star atlas and assigned the star the letter "P" in Cygnus — an unusual designation (letters A–Q were assigned from brightest to faintest at the time of cataloguing) which persisted into modern nomenclature. [Wikipedia: P Cygni]
- **1606**: P Cygni began fading. By **1626** it had dropped below naked-eye visibility. [Wikipedia: P Cygni]
- **1655**: P Cygni brightened again; it faded once more in **1662**, then had another outburst in **1665**. [Wikipedia: P Cygni]
- **~1715**: The star finally stabilised near 5th magnitude, where it has remained ever since, with low-amplitude photometric variability characteristic of a quiescent LBV. [Wikipedia: P Cygni]
- **17th–19th centuries**: The characteristic double-component spectral feature was identified by astronomers studying the star's spectrum, eventually leading to the recognition that this was a recurring pattern in other stellar spectra, and the name "P Cygni profile" was adopted as the generic term. [Wikipedia: P Cygni profile]
- **2021**: Interferometric observations (Mahy et al.) confirmed the existence of a close companion, resolving a longstanding question about whether P Cygni is a binary system. [Wikipedia: P Cygni]

## Sub-types / Classifications

**P Cygni as a star type:**
The star P Cygni itself belongs to the class of luminous blue variables (LBVs), also called S Doradus variables. LBVs are characterised by:
- Luminosities > 10⁵ L☉, often approaching 10⁶ L☉
- Spectral types B and A (occasionally cooler) with strong emission
- Irregular photometric variability on timescales of years to decades
- Giant eruptions that eject large shells of material
- P Cygni spectral profiles as a defining observational feature

**Stars showing P Cygni profiles (broader usage):**
The term "P Cygni star" or "P Cygni-type star" is sometimes used loosely to describe any star whose spectrum shows the P Cygni profile. Such stars include:
1. **Luminous blue variables (LBVs)**: η Carinae, P Cygni, S Doradus — extreme massive stars losing mass in powerful winds
2. **Wolf-Rayet stars**: hot, evolved massive stars whose strong winds produce P Cygni profiles in helium and nitrogen/carbon lines
3. **Classical novae**: during expansion phase, hydrogen lines show P Cygni profiles as the ejected shell expands
4. **Supernovae**: ejecta produce P Cygni profiles used to measure expansion velocities and identify composition
5. **Be stars** (during outbursts): rapidly rotating B stars with circumstellar discs
6. **Protostars** (inverse P Cygni profiles for infalling material, standard profiles for jets/outflows)
