## Definition

Metal-poor stars are stars whose abundances of elements heavier than hydrogen and helium (collectively called "metals" by astronomers) are significantly lower than those of the Sun, typically quantified as [Fe/H] < −1.0; they formed early in cosmic history when the interstellar medium had not yet been enriched by successive generations of stellar nucleosynthesis, making them living time capsules of the early Universe.

## Full Content

### What metal-poor means

- Astronomers call all elements heavier than helium "metals." The fraction of these elements in a star is its metallicity, written Z; for the Sun Z ≈ 0.014. [Wikipedia, Metallicity]
- The standard metallicity proxy is [Fe/H]: the logarithmic ratio of iron to hydrogen relative to the Sun's value. A star with [Fe/H] = −1 has 1/10 the solar iron abundance; [Fe/H] = −3 means 1/1000 solar. [Wikipedia, Metallicity]
- Stars are metal-poor because they formed before the interstellar medium had been enriched by many generations of supernova explosions. The older the star, the fewer metals it inherited. [Stellar population article; Beers & Christlieb 2005]

### Classification tiers (Beers & Christlieb 2005)

- **Metal-poor (MP):** [Fe/H] < −1.0
- **Very metal-poor (VMP):** [Fe/H] < −2.0
- **Extremely metal-poor (EMP):** [Fe/H] < −3.0
- **Ultra metal-poor (UMP):** [Fe/H] < −4.0
- **Hyper metal-poor (HMP):** [Fe/H] < −5.0
- [Beers & Christlieb 2005, ARA&A; arxiv 2504.06335]
- Some studies subdivide EMP further based on physical criteria such as the lithium Spite plateau: stars below [Fe/H] ≈ −2.8 show increased scatter and Li-depleted outliers. [arxiv 2504.06335]

### Physical properties

- Metal-poor stars are overwhelmingly old Population II (or hypothetical Population III) objects found in the Milky Way's stellar halo and in globular clusters. [Wikipedia, Stellar population]
- Because heavy elements are minority constituents, metal-poor stars are bluer and structurally simpler than metal-rich stars of the same mass — reduced opacity from fewer metals means hotter, more transparent outer layers. [Wikipedia, Metallicity]
- Despite low overall metallicity, many metal-poor halo stars show enhanced ratios of alpha elements (O, Mg, Si, Ca, Ti) relative to iron compared to Population I stars, because alpha elements were produced by core-collapse (Type II) supernovae that enriched the early ISM before Type Ia supernovae had time to deposit iron. [Wikipedia, Stellar population]
- Carbon-enhanced metal-poor (CEMP) stars show [C/Fe] > +0.7 and are an unexpected overabundance: they are absent from Population I but make up ~12% of stars with [Fe/H] < −2 and ~20% at [Fe/H] < −2.5. [Carollo et al. 2014]
- CEMP-s stars (carbon + s-process enhancement) are thought to arise from mass transfer in binary systems with an AGB companion. CEMP-no stars (carbon enhancement, no s-process) are thought to preserve signatures of early, massive "faint supernova" nucleosynthesis. [Carollo et al. 2014; Hansen et al. 2016]

### Where metal-poor stars are found

- Metal-poor stars populate the Galactic halo — the diffuse spherical region extending tens of kiloparsecs from the Galactic centre — and in old globular clusters orbiting the halo. [Wikipedia, Stellar population]
- They are rare in the thin disk, which has been continuously enriched over the galaxy's lifetime. The bulge contains a mix; some ultra-metal-poor stars have been identified with disk-like orbits (e.g., SDSS J102915 and Caffau's star). [arxiv 2504.06335]
- Globular clusters such as Messier 92 and NGC 6397 are archetypal metal-poor stellar systems, with ages close to that of the Universe itself (~13 Gyr). [NASA/ESA Hubble]

### Observational methods and surveys

- Metal-poor stars are identified by spectroscopy: weak or absent Ca II H&K absorption lines and weak iron lines signal low metallicity. [Wikipedia, Metallicity]
- The objective-prism technique used by the HK Survey (Beers, Preston, Shectman) and the Hamburg/ESO Survey (HES; Christlieb et al.) scanned wide fields for weak Ca II K lines, identifying thousands of candidates. HES covered 8,853 sq. deg. and delivered ~200 new stars with [Fe/H] < −3, trebling the known sample. [Christlieb et al. 2008; Bolte, EMP history]
- Modern photometric narrow-band surveys (Pristine, SkyMapper, S-PLUS, J-PLUS) use Ca II H&K or metallicity-sensitive filters and machine-learning classifiers to select candidates at fainter magnitudes. [arxiv 2504.06335]
- Spectroscopic follow-up from SDSS, LAMOST, GALAH, APOGEE, and Gaia RVS provides metallicities for millions of stars, enabling statistical studies of the very metal-poor tail. [arxiv 2504.06335]
- High-resolution echelle spectroscopy (e.g., Subaru/HDS, VLT/UVES, Magellan/MIKE) provides precise elemental abundances for individual EMP and HMP stars. [Bolte, EMP history]

### Notable examples

- **SMSS J031300.36−670839.3** — discovered by Keller et al. (2014) via SkyMapper; [Fe/H] < −7.1, the lowest iron abundance measured in any known star. Its spectrum shows no detectable iron at all. Chemical abundance pattern consistent with enrichment by a single ~60 M☉ supernova. [Keller et al. 2014, Nature 506, 463]
- **SDSS J102915+172927** ("Caffau's star") — [Fe/H] ≈ −5.0, discovered 2011; notable for being neither carbon-enhanced nor alpha-rich, making it difficult to explain with standard enrichment models; mass < 1 M☉, age >13 Gyr. [Caffau et al. 2011; arxiv 2504.06335]
- **HE 0107−5240** — [Fe/H] = −5.3; first hyper-metal-poor star discovered (Christlieb et al. 2002), found via HES. Strongly carbon-enhanced (CEMP). [Bolte, EMP history]
- **HE 1327−2326** — [Fe/H] = −5.4; also found via HES, another carbon-enhanced HMP star. [Bolte, EMP history]
- **SMSS J160540.18−144323.1** — [Fe/H] ≈ −6.2 detected via iron line; currently among the most iron-poor stars with a confirmed iron detection. [Nordlander et al. 2019]
- **Messier 92** — metal-poor globular cluster in Hercules; ~330,000 stars, age ~13 Gyr; one of the oldest known stellar systems in the Milky Way. [NASA/ESA Hubble]

### Significance

- **Galactic archaeology and stellar archaeology:** The surface chemical abundances of metal-poor halo stars are nearly unchanged from the ISM at the time of their formation. Reading these chemical fingerprints reconstructs the nucleosynthetic events that preceded them — including yields of the very first stars. [Frebel & Norris review; arxiv galactic archaeology]
- **Near-field cosmology:** Because UMP and HMP stars may have been enriched by as few as one supernova, their abundances directly constrain that supernova's explosion energy, mass, and nucleosynthetic yields — tests otherwise inaccessible observationally. [arxiv 2504.06335]
- **First-star connection:** Population III stars (the hypothetical metal-free first stars of the Universe) have not been directly detected, but their chemical imprint survives in the most metal-poor halo stars. These second-generation stars are the best observational proxy for Pop III nucleosynthesis. [Wikipedia, Stellar population; arxiv 2504.06335]
- **r-process nucleosynthesis:** Many metal-poor stars show large enhancements of rapid neutron-capture (r-process) elements such as europium and barium. Because the r-process requires extreme conditions (neutron star mergers or specific supernovae), these stars constrain where and when the heaviest elements were created in the early Galaxy. [MDPI Universe 11, 229, 2025]
- **Planet formation:** Stellar metallicity strongly correlates with the presence of giant planets. Metal-poor stars are much less likely to host gas giants, suggesting heavy elements are essential for efficient planetesimal formation. [Wikipedia, Metallicity]

## Historical Context

- **1802–1860s:** William Hyde Wollaston and Joseph von Fraunhofer map solar absorption lines; Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen identify them as elemental signatures, establishing the basis for stellar abundance measurements. Iron becomes the reference element due to its rich optical spectrum. [Wikipedia, Metallicity]
- **1951:** Chamberlain and Aller first demonstrate that some stars have systematically lower abundances of heavy elements — the discovery that metal-poor stars exist as a population. [Bolte, EMP history]
- **1954:** Nancy Roman establishes a connection between stellar kinematics (high-velocity stars) and metallicity — high-velocity halo stars show weak metal lines and UV excess. [Bolte, EMP history]
- **1960s–80s:** Theoretical motivation grows with Big Bang nucleosynthesis theory: the search for primordial "zero-metallicity" Population III stars begins. Bond (1981) catalogs metal-deficient halo giants; few stars with [Fe/H] < −2.5 known at this point. Key contributors: Carney, Bessell, Gratton, Wallerstein, Sneden, Kraft, Peterson. [Bolte, EMP history]
- **1985–1992:** The HK (Beers-Preston-Shectman) Survey uses objective-prism spectroscopy to systematically scan for weak Ca II K lines; delivers the first large sample of VMP and EMP candidates. [Bolte, EMP history]
- **1995:** McWilliam et al. high-resolution spectroscopy confirms ~35 stars with [Fe/H] < −3 and characterises alpha-element enhancements. [Bolte, EMP history]
- **1990s–2000s:** Hamburg/ESO Survey (HES) extends the HK Survey to fainter magnitudes and southern sky; Christlieb leads the program. ~200 new [Fe/H] < −3 stars discovered. [Christlieb et al. 2008]
- **2002:** Christlieb et al. discover HE 0107−5240 ([Fe/H] = −5.3) via HES — first confirmed hyper-metal-poor star, overturns assumptions about minimum metallicity floor. [Bolte, EMP history]
- **2005:** Beers & Christlieb publish the definitive classification framework (MP/VMP/EMP/UMP/HMP) in Annual Review of Astronomy & Astrophysics. [Beers & Christlieb 2005, ARA&A 43, 531]
- **2005–present:** SDSS, SkyMapper, Pristine, LAMOST, GALAH, and Gaia surveys industrialise the discovery of metal-poor stars at fainter magnitudes and in greater numbers. Machine-learning photometric classifiers allow pre-screening of millions of candidates. [arxiv 2504.06335]
- **2011:** Caffau et al. announce SDSS J102915+172927 ([Fe/H] ≈ −5.0); most metal-poor star known at the time without carbon enhancement. [Caffau et al. 2011]
- **2014:** Keller et al. announce SMSS J031300.36−670839.3 ([Fe/H] < −7.1) in Nature — the current record holder for lowest iron abundance. [Keller et al. 2014, Nature 506, 463]

## Sub-types / Classifications

### By metallicity (Beers & Christlieb 2005)

| Class | Abbreviation | [Fe/H] | Notes |
|-------|-------------|--------|-------|
| Metal-poor | MP | < −1.0 | Includes old disk and halo stars |
| Very metal-poor | VMP | < −2.0 | Predominantly halo Population II |
| Extremely metal-poor | EMP | < −3.0 | ~35 confirmed prior to HES; now hundreds |
| Ultra metal-poor | UMP | < −4.0 | Mostly carbon-enhanced |
| Hyper metal-poor | HMP | < −5.0 | Handful known; all chemically peculiar |

### By chemical pattern (independent of metallicity tier)

- **CEMP-no:** Carbon-enhanced ([C/Fe] > +0.7), no s-process or r-process enrichment; thought to preserve Population III nucleosynthetic signatures (faint supernovae, mixing/fallback). Most prevalent at [Fe/H] < −3.
- **CEMP-s:** Carbon + s-process enhanced; attributed to binary mass transfer from an AGB companion. More common at [Fe/H] ~ −2 to −1.
- **CEMP-r/s:** Both r- and s-process enrichment; rarer; unclear origin.
- **r-I and r-II stars:** Enriched in rapid neutron-capture elements (r-process; e.g., high [Eu/Fe]); r-II stars are most extreme. Thought to trace neutron star mergers or rare core-collapse supernovae in the early Galaxy.
- **"Normal" (non-enhanced) EMP stars:** Alpha-enhanced relative to iron (standard halo pattern) without extreme C or r-process signatures.
