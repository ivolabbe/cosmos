# Lunar Eclipse — Article Spec

## Category
solar-system

## Definition
A lunar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes into Earth's shadow, causing it to darken; unlike a solar eclipse, it is visible simultaneously from anywhere on the night side of Earth. There are three types—penumbral, partial, and total—depending on which part of Earth's shadow the Moon enters.

**Source:** Wikipedia: Lunar eclipse; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_eclipse

## Full Content

### Mechanism: Earth's Shadow
A lunar eclipse occurs when the Earth lies directly between the Sun and the full Moon, casting its shadow across the lunar surface. Earth projects two distinct shadow zones into space: the **umbra** (the darkest central cone where direct sunlight is completely blocked) and the **penumbra** (the outer, partially illuminated zone where only some sunlight is blocked). The type of eclipse experienced by the Moon depends on which zone—or combination of zones—it passes through.

**Source:** Britannica: Lunar eclipse; https://www.britannica.com/science/lunar-eclipse

Lunar eclipses do not occur every full Moon because the Moon's orbital plane is inclined approximately **5° relative to Earth's orbital plane** (the ecliptic). An eclipse can only happen when a full Moon occurs near one of the two **orbital nodes**—the points where the Moon's orbit crosses the ecliptic—so that Earth, Moon, and Sun are sufficiently aligned.

**Source:** Britannica: Lunar eclipse; https://www.britannica.com/science/lunar-eclipse

#### Shadow Geometry
At the Moon's average distance, Earth's umbral shadow has a diameter of approximately **9,000–9,200 km** (roughly 2.6 times the Moon's diameter of 3,476 km). Earth's atmospheric layer slightly enlarges the effective shadow: NASA analysis of crater-timing observations from 1972–1982 found a mean umbral enlargement of approximately 1.7–2.2%, and earlier work by Link (1969) found a mean enlargement of 2.3% across 57 eclipses.

**Source:** NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, "Enlargement of Earth's Shadows"; https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/LEcat5/shadow.html

### Types of Lunar Eclipses

#### Penumbral Eclipse
The Moon passes through only Earth's penumbra. The dimming of the Moon's surface is subtle—often scarcely noticeable to casual observers—because sunlight is only partially blocked. Approximately one-third of all lunar eclipses are penumbral. Total penumbral eclipses (where the entire Moon enters the penumbra but none enters the umbra) are rare, comprising only about 3% of penumbral events.

**Source:** Wikipedia: Lunar eclipse; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_eclipse

#### Partial Eclipse
Part of the Moon enters the umbra while the remainder stays in the penumbra or direct sunlight. The dark umbral bite taken out of the lunar disk is distinctly visible to the naked eye. Approximately 34.9% of all lunar eclipses over a 5,000-year period are partial.

**Source:** NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, Five Millennium Catalog of Lunar Eclipses; https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/LEcat5/LEcatalog.html

#### Total Eclipse (Blood Moon)
The entire Moon passes into Earth's umbra. Because Earth has no atmosphere extending into space in the shadow cone, totality does not produce a completely black Moon. Instead, sunlight refracted through Earth's atmosphere reaches the Moon indirectly. Total eclipses represent approximately 28.8% of all lunar eclipses over the 5,000-year period from 2000 BCE to 3000 CE.

**Source:** NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, Five Millennium Catalog of Lunar Eclipses; https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/LEcat5/LEcatalog.html

**Central eclipses** are a special sub-category of total eclipses in which the Moon passes close to or through the centre of Earth's umbral shadow. They represent 59.6% of all total lunar eclipses.

**Source:** Wikipedia: Lunar eclipse; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_eclipse

### Blood Moon Coloring and Rayleigh Scattering
During totality, the Moon is illuminated by a ring of refracted sunlight bent around Earth's limb through Earth's atmosphere. The reddish-orange glow that earned total lunar eclipses the popular name "blood moon" arises from **Rayleigh scattering**: shorter wavelengths (blue, violet) are scattered out of the light beam by atmospheric molecules and small particles, while longer wavelengths (red, orange) pass through and are refracted into the umbral shadow cone. This is the same physical mechanism responsible for red sunsets and the blue daytime sky.

**Source:** Wikipedia: Lunar eclipse; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_eclipse

The color and brightness of the eclipsed Moon vary with atmospheric conditions. Volcanic eruptions, high dust loading, or unusual cloud cover can deepen the red or even make the Moon nearly invisible at mid-totality. Conversely, a clear atmosphere produces a brighter, more copper-toned Moon.

**Source:** NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, "Danjon Scale of Lunar Eclipse Brightness"; https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/OH/Danjon.html

### The Danjon Scale
French astronomer André Danjon proposed a five-point luminosity scale for rating the appearance of the Moon during total lunar eclipses, now known as the **Danjon scale**:

| L value | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| L = 0 | Very dark eclipse. Moon almost invisible, especially at mid-totality. |
| L = 1 | Dark eclipse, gray or brownish. Details distinguishable only with difficulty. |
| L = 2 | Deep red or rust-colored. Very dark central shadow; outer umbral edge relatively bright. |
| L = 3 | Brick-red eclipse. Umbral shadow usually has a bright or yellow rim. |
| L = 4 | Very bright copper-red or orange. Umbral shadow has a bluish, very bright rim. |

The scale is most useful when assessed with the naked eye or binoculars near mid-totality. Because volcanic aerosols and water vapour in the stratosphere affect how much blue and green light is filtered out, the Danjon value can serve as an indirect measure of atmospheric transparency.

**Source:** NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, "Danjon Scale of Lunar Eclipse Brightness"; https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/OH/Danjon.html

### Frequency
In most calendar years there are two lunar eclipses; in some years there is one, three, or none. The 21st century contains 228 lunar eclipses, averaging 2.28 per year. Over the 5,000-year span from 2000 BCE to 3000 CE, approximately 12,064 lunar eclipses occur:

- Penumbral: 36.3%
- Partial: 34.9%
- Total: 28.8%

An observer at a fixed location on Earth can witness 19 or 20 lunar eclipses in any 18-year window (since a lunar eclipse is visible from the entire night hemisphere).

**Sources:**
- Britannica: Lunar eclipse; https://www.britannica.com/science/lunar-eclipse
- NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, Five Millennium Catalog; https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/LEcat5/LEcatalog.html
- Wikipedia: Lunar eclipse; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_eclipse

### Duration
Totality can last up to approximately **107 minutes**; the full event from first to last contact of the Moon's limb with Earth's shadow can span up to **236 minutes** (nearly 4 hours). The record duration for totality is 1 hour 42 minutes 57 seconds, achieved during the total lunar eclipse of 27 July 2018. Duration depends on how centrally the Moon passes through the umbra and on the Moon's orbital distance (eclipses near apogee, when the Moon is farther from Earth, take longer to traverse the shadow).

**Source:** Wikipedia: Lunar eclipse; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_eclipse

### The Saros Cycle
The recurrence of lunar eclipses is governed by the **Saros cycle**, a period of approximately **6,585.3 days (18 years, 11 days, 8 hours)**. This cycle arises because three distinct lunar orbital periods come into near-perfect alignment after one Saros:

- **223 synodic months** = 6,585.3223 days
- **239 anomalistic months** = 6,585.5375 days
- **242 draconic months** = 6,585.3575 days

After one Saros, the Sun, Moon, and Earth return to nearly the same geometry, so a similar eclipse recurs. A complete Saros series typically spans 1,226 to 1,550 years and contains 69 to 87 eclipses in total. A single Saros period contains approximately 70 eclipses, of which 29 are lunar.

**Source:** NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, "Eclipses and the Saros"; https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/SEsaros/SEsaros.html

The word "Saros" derives from the Babylonian term *sar* (a 3,600-year interval) but was not applied to eclipse prediction until English astronomer **Edmund Halley** adopted it in 1691, drawing on 11th-century Byzantine sources.

**Source:** NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, "Eclipses and the Saros"; https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/SEsaros/SEsaros.html

### Historical Significance
Lunar eclipses have been recorded and interpreted throughout human history:

- **Mesopotamia (~2300 BCE):** The earliest known written records of lunar eclipses are Babylonian clay tablets dating to around 2300 BCE. By 747 BCE, Babylonian astronomers could predict the timing of lunar (and solar) eclipses using the Saros cycle. Eclipses were treated as omens for kings and nations.

- **Athens (413 BCE):** During the Peloponnesian War, Athenian commander Nicias delayed the retreat of his fleet from Syracuse after a lunar eclipse. Interpreting it as an ill omen requiring a further 27-day wait, his hesitation allowed Syracusian forces to counterattack, contributing decisively to the destruction of the Athenian Sicilian Expedition.

- **Christopher Columbus (1504):** Stranded in Jamaica and facing a food embargo from indigenous inhabitants, Columbus used almanac predictions of the total lunar eclipse of 29 February 1504 to intimidate local leaders into resuming provisions, claiming divine wrath would darken the Moon. Just before the end of totality, he announced that God's pardon had been granted.

- **Aristotle and Earth's Shape:** Greek philosophers, including Aristotle (4th century BCE), cited the curved shadow Earth casts on the Moon during lunar eclipses as evidence that Earth is spherical.

**Sources:**

- EclipseWise, "Lunar Eclipses of History"; https://www.eclipsewise.com/extra/LEhistoryReis.html

## Historical Context

### Ancient Observations
Babylonian astronomers recognized the Saros cycle by ~747 BCE, enabling accurate eclipse prediction centuries before modern astronomy. Greek philosophers used lunar eclipses to demonstrate Earth's spherical shape. Chinese, Islamic, and Mesoamerican cultures also tracked eclipses as calendar and cosmological markers.

### The Saros Connection
Halley's adoption of the Babylonian term "Saros" in 1691 formalized the naming convention still used today. Each new eclipse in a Saros series shifts geographically by approximately 120° westward (due to the 8-hour offset in the 6,585.3-day cycle), meaning three Saros periods (an "Exeligmos" of ~54 years and 34 days) are required before an eclipse recurs at nearly the same location on Earth.

**Source:** NASA GSFC, "Eclipses and the Saros"; https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/SEsaros/SEsaros.html

## Sub-types / Classifications

### By Shadow Zone
- **Penumbral:** Moon in penumbra only (subtle dimming)
- **Partial:** Moon partly in umbra (dark bite visible)
- **Total:** Moon fully in umbra (blood moon coloring)

### By Totality Geometry
- **Central total eclipse:** Moon passes through or very near the umbral axis
- **Non-central total eclipse:** Moon fully enters umbra but not centrally

### By Danjon Brightness
- L = 0 (nearly invisible) through L = 4 (bright copper-red)
