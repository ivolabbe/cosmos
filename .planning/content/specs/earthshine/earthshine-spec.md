# Earthshine — Article Spec

## Category

## Definition
Earthshine is the pale luminosity visible on the dark portion of the Moon's face, caused by sunlight that has been reflected from Earth's surface and clouds onto the Moon and back again to an observer on Earth. The phenomenon is most easily seen during a thin crescent Moon phase, when the night-side of the Moon is faintly but unmistakably lit by this doubly-reflected light.

## Full Content

### What is Earthshine?

Earthshine — also called **earthlight** or historically **lumen cinereum** ("ashen light") — is the diffuse reflection of sunlight from Earth's surface and atmosphere that falls on the Moon's unilluminated face. It is an example of **planetshine**: the illumination of a moon by reflected light from its parent planet.

When the Moon is a thin crescent, Earth presents a nearly full face toward the Moon. Earth's large angular size as seen from the Moon (about four times the diameter the Moon presents from Earth) and its comparatively high reflectivity (~30% albedo vs. the Moon's ~12%) means Earth can illuminate the lunar night-side sufficiently for observers on Earth to detect the faint glow. The result is that the entire lunar disk becomes faintly visible — the dark part glowing with a soft silver-blue hue alongside the brilliantly lit crescent.

### "The Old Moon in the New Moon's Arms"

The poetic phrase **"the old Moon in the new Moon's arms"** describes the appearance of a waxing crescent phase when the full disc is faintly outlined by earthshine. The bright sliver of the crescent — lit by direct sunlight — forms the "new Moon," while the ghostly glow that reveals the rest of the disc is the "old Moon" appearing to nestle within it. The reverse expression, "the new Moon in the old Moon's arms," refers to the waning crescent just before New Moon. Both phrases have appeared in English verse and folklore for centuries, reflecting how long this delicate phenomenon has captured the human imagination.

### Leonardo da Vinci's Explanation

The first scientific explanation of earthshine was recorded by **Leonardo da Vinci** around **1510**, in a chapter of his *Codex Leicester* titled "Of the Moon: No Solid Body is Lighter than Air." Leonardo deduced that the pale glow on the dark face of the Moon was caused by sunlight bouncing off Earth and then illuminating the lunar surface. He specifically attributed the reflection to Earth's **oceans**, reasoning that water was the most reflective surface available.

Da Vinci's core insight was correct: Earth's reflected light does illuminate the Moon. However, modern science has refined the detail — it is Earth's **clouds**, not oceans, that are the dominant reflector. Clouds reflect approximately **50% of incident sunlight**, whereas open oceans reflect only about **10%** and land surfaces between **10% and 25%**. For this reason, earthshine is sometimes called the **"Da Vinci Glow"** in his honour.

### The Physics of Double Reflection

Earthshine results from two successive reflections:

1. **Sun → Earth:** Sunlight strikes Earth. Clouds, land, and ocean reflect a fraction back into space. Earth's overall **Bond albedo** is approximately **0.30** (30% of incoming sunlight is reflected).
2. **Earth → Moon → Observer:** The reflected light travels to the Moon. The lunar surface, with its own albedo of ~0.12, reflects a fraction back toward Earth.

The chain of two low-efficiency reflections means earthshine is very faint — it carries roughly **0.01% of the direct sunlight radiance**, corresponding to a maximum radiance at the lunar surface of approximately **0.15 W m⁻²**. From Earth, the illuminated dark limb reaches a maximum apparent magnitude of around **−3.7**, making it visible to the naked eye but far dimmer than the crescent itself.

### Albedo Monitor: Earthshine and Earth's Reflectivity

Because the brightness of earthshine directly encodes how much sunlight Earth is reflecting, it acts as a **natural albedo monitor**. Since 1998, astronomers at the **Big Bear Solar Observatory (BBSO)** of the New Jersey Institute of Technology, led by **Philip Goode**, have run **Project Earthshine** — a systematic program to track Earth's reflectivity by measuring the brightness of the dark face of the Moon.

Data collected between **1998 and 2017** revealed a gradual but climatologically significant decline: Earth reflected approximately **0.5 W m⁻² less** solar energy by the end of the period than at the start — a **0.5% decrease in albedo**. The primary cause identified was a reduction in low-lying, highly reflective cloud cover over the eastern Pacific Ocean, linked to warming sea-surface temperatures associated with the **Pacific Decadal Oscillation**. This additional absorbed solar energy is comparable in magnitude to the total anthropogenic climate forcing accumulated over the same two decades, underscoring the climatic importance of even small changes in Earth's reflectivity.

The earthshine method is complementary to satellite measurements (such as NASA's CERES instruments) and provides an independent, ground-based cross-check spanning longer baseline periods.

### Earthshine and Exoplanet Research

Earthshine has become a crucial tool in the search for life on worlds orbiting other stars. From the Moon's surface, earthshine carries the **disk-integrated light** of Earth — a composite signal of its entire sunlit face — just as a distant exoplanet would appear: a single, unresolved point of light. By studying earthshine spectra and polarization, astronomers can calibrate what a **living, inhabited planet looks like** from afar.

Spectral analysis of earthshine has detected several key **biosignatures**:

- **Molecular oxygen (O₂):** A strong absorption feature whose high atmospheric abundance is inconsistent with geochemical equilibrium and indicates biological production.
- **Methane (CH₄):** Present at concentrations far above what abiotic chemistry alone could sustain.
- **Water vapour (H₂O):** Indicative of a liquid-water ocean and an active hydrological cycle.
- **The Vegetation Red Edge (VRE):** A sharp rise in reflectance at wavelengths longer than ~700 nm caused by chlorophyll in land vegetation. This feature, detectable in both intensity spectra and linear polarization, would flag the presence of surface plant life on an exoplanet. Studies suggest a minimum vegetation coverage of roughly **10%** of the cloud-free surface area is needed to confidently detect the VRE.

Additionally, polarimetric analysis of earthshine can reveal **ocean glint** — enhanced polarization at large phase angles caused by specular reflection from liquid water surfaces — as well as indicators of an active water cycle. Research has demonstrated that "from polarization alone, we can infer oceans, vegetation, and an active water cycle — key indicators of a habitable planet."

These capabilities directly inform the design of future large observatories (such as the ELT) that aim to characterise the atmospheres and surfaces of Earth-like planets around other stars.

### Visibility and How to Observe

Earthshine is accessible to the **naked eye** under dark-sky conditions, making it one of the rare astronomical phenomena requiring no equipment. The best opportunities occur:

- **A few days before New Moon** (waning crescent): look to the **eastern horizon** before sunrise.
- **A few days after New Moon** (waxing crescent): look to the **western horizon** after sunset.

Binoculars or a small telescope sharpen the view considerably. The phenomenon is brightest and easiest to see when the crescent is thin, because Earth then presents its most fully-illuminated face toward the Moon. Once the Moon waxes past first quarter, the earthshine dims and the increasing glare of the sunlit portion makes it progressively harder to detect.

Spring is generally the most favourable season in the Northern Hemisphere: the ecliptic angle causes a thin waxing crescent to stand well clear of the horizon at dusk, giving observers a longer twilight window. Light pollution is the main adversary — a dark or moderately dark site significantly enhances visibility.

## Historical Context

### Pre-Scientific Awareness
The faint glow on the unlit Moon face was noticed by ancient and medieval sky-watchers but unexplained for millennia. It was recorded in poetry and folklore under vivid names — "the old Moon in the new Moon's arms" — long before anyone understood its physical cause.

### Leonardo da Vinci (~1510)
In the *Codex Leicester*, da Vinci provided the first scientific explanation: Earth reflects sunlight onto the Moon. He attributed the reflection principally to the oceans. His notebooks were not widely published in his lifetime, delaying acceptance of his insight.

### Danjon and Photometric Measurements (1928)
French astronomer **André-Louis Danjon** pioneered systematic photometric measurement of earthshine in 1928, establishing a quantitative approach that modern programs descend from.

**Source:** Project Earthshine, BBSO; https://bbso.njit.edu/np/es.html

### Project Earthshine (1998–present)
The Big Bear Solar Observatory launched the modern era of earthshine measurement in the mid-1990s as a climate monitoring tool, producing 20+ years of albedo data.

## Sub-types / Classifications

- **Planetshine:** The general phenomenon of a moon being illuminated by reflected light from its parent planet. Earthshine is the Earth-Moon instance; other examples include "Jupitershine" on Jupiter's Galilean moons.
- **Lumen cinereum / Ashen light:** Historical Latin and English names for earthshine, still used occasionally in descriptive literature.
- **Da Vinci Glow:** Popular modern nickname honouring Leonardo da Vinci.
