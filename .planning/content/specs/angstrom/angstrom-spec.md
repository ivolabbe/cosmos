## Definition
The ångström (symbol Å) is a unit of length equal to 10⁻¹⁰ metres (one ten-billionth of a metre, or 0.1 nanometres), used widely in astronomy, spectroscopy, and atomic physics to express wavelengths of light and sizes of atoms and molecules.

## Full Content

- The ångström (Å) is a unit of length equal to exactly 10⁻¹⁰ metres, or 0.1 nanometres [Wikipedia]
- It is not part of the International System of Units (SI) but remains widely used in spectroscopy, atomic physics, and observational astronomy alongside the nanometre [Wikipedia]
- In everyday astronomical usage, wavelengths of light are still routinely quoted in ångströms in the spectroscopic literature, even as nanometres increasingly predominate in modern publications [Wikipedia]
- The unit is especially convenient because it places visible light wavelengths in a range of four-digit integers (4,000–7,000 Å), which is intuitive for spectroscopists [Britannica]
- Blue light sits around 4,400 Å, green around 5,500 Å, yellow around 5,800 Å, and red around 6,500 Å [NED/IPAC Glossary]
- At atomic scales, the ångström is equally useful: most atoms have radii of 1–2 Å, and chemical bond lengths typically fall in the 1–3 Å range [Britannica]
- The Bohr radius of the hydrogen atom is ~0.529 Å, making the ångström a natural scale for quantum mechanical calculations [standard physics]
- In X-ray astronomy, the ångström remains the conventional unit: soft X-rays span 2.5–100 Å and hard X-rays 0.25–2.5 Å [NASA HEASARC]
- Extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) radiation covers 100–900 Å, and the Lyman limit (the hydrogen ionisation edge) falls at 912 Å — a critical boundary for observations of the interstellar medium [NED/IPAC]
- The "ångström star" (Å*) is a related, specialised unit introduced by J. A. Bearden in 1965 to standardise X-ray wavelength measurements; it is defined so that the tungsten Kα1 line has a wavelength of exactly 0.209 010 0 Å*, making 1 Å* = 1 Å to within 5 parts per million [ADS/Bearden 1967]
- The unit symbol Å uses a Swedish letter (A with a ring above), reflecting the name's Scandinavian origin; the anglicised spelling "angstrom" without diacritics is now standard in English texts [Wikipedia]

## Historical Context

- Anders Jonas Ångström (1814–1874) was a Swedish physicist born in Lögdö, Sweden, and spent his career at Uppsala University. He is regarded as one of the founders of spectroscopy [Wikipedia]
- In 1868, Ångström published *Recherches sur le spectre solaire* (Research on the Solar Spectrum), a landmark atlas mapping more than 1,000 Fraunhofer absorption lines in the solar spectrum. He expressed wavelengths in units of 10⁻¹⁰ metres (i.e., one ten-millionth of a millimetre in the notation of the time) [Wikipedia]
- Although Ångström's wavelength measurements contained small systematic errors (roughly one part in 7,000–8,000), the atlas was authoritative for decades and its units were adopted by the solar physics community [Wikipedia]
- Ångström also identified hydrogen in the solar spectrum in 1862 — the first identification of an element in the Sun's atmosphere — and conducted pioneering studies of the aurora borealis [Wikipedia]
- He received the Rumford Medal (1872) and was elected to the Royal Society (1870) and French Academy of Sciences (1873). He died in 1874, just before his 60th birthday [Wikipedia]
- In 1907, the International Union for Cooperation in Solar Research (predecessor to the IAU) formalised the unit by defining 1 Å as exactly 1/6438.4696 of the wavelength of the red line of the cadmium emission spectrum, establishing a precise spectroscopic standard [NED/IPAC Glossary]
- The International Congress of Weights and Measures formally recognised the unit in 1927 [NED/IPAC Glossary]
- When the metre was redefined in 1960 using the krypton-86 spectral line, the ångström was automatically fixed at exactly 10⁻¹⁰ m; this equivalence has been exact ever since [NED/IPAC Glossary / Wikipedia]
- In 1965, J. A. Bearden introduced the ångström star (Å*) to resolve ambiguities in X-ray wavelength standards, defining it via the tungsten Kα1 line [ADS, Bearden 1967, Rev. Mod. Phys. 39, 78]

## Sub-types / Classifications

- **Ångström (Å)** — standard unit, 10⁻¹⁰ m, used for optical and UV spectroscopy and atomic sizes
- **Ångström star (Å*)** — X-ray spectroscopy unit introduced by Bearden (1965); 1 Å* ≈ 1 Å (agreement within 5 ppm); defined by the tungsten Kα1 line at exactly 0.209 010 0 Å*
