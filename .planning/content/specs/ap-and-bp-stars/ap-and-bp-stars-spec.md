## Summary

Ap and Bp stars are chemically peculiar main-sequence stars of spectral types A and B (hence the suffix "p" for "peculiar") that display anomalously strong magnetic fields, dramatically overabundant rare-earth and iron-peak elements at their surfaces, and slow rotation compared with normal A- and B-type stars. Their photometric and spectroscopic variability is explained by the oblique rotator model, in which a dipolar magnetic field is tilted relative to the rotation axis, carrying surface chemical spots in and out of view as the star spins.

## Key Facts

### Definition and Classification

- Ap/Bp stars are the magnetic subgroup of chemically peculiar (CP) stars, designated **CP2** in the classification system introduced by Preston (1974). [Preston, G.W. 1974, ARA&A, 12, 257]
- The four CP groups are: CP1 (Am/metallic-line stars), CP2 (magnetic Ap/Bp stars), CP3 (HgMn mercury-manganese stars), CP4 (He-weak stars). [Wikipedia: Chemically peculiar star; Preston 1974]
- Spectral subtype coverage: roughly **B8p to A7p**, corresponding to effective temperatures of approximately **8,000–15,000 K**. [Wikipedia: Alpha2 Canum Venaticorum variable; Wikipedia: Chemically peculiar star]
- Approximately **5–10% of hot main-sequence stars** show chemical peculiarities; Ap/Bp stars make up the majority of these. [Wikipedia: Chemically peculiar star]

### Discovery and History

- Chemically peculiar upper main-sequence stars have been the subject of astrophysical study since their identification by American astronomer **Antonia Maury in 1897**, in her catalogue of spectra of 681 bright northern stars photographed with the 11-inch Draper Telescope — the first Harvard Observatory publication bearing a woman's name as author. [Wikipedia: Antonia Maury; massivesci.com]
- Maury introduced line-width subdivisions (normal "a", hazy "b", sharp "c") that later underpinned the luminosity criterion recognised by Hertzsprung (1905) and formally adopted by the IAU in 1922. [Wikipedia: Antonia Maury]
- The CP2 notation and four-group classification scheme was formalised by **George Preston in 1974** and remains in use today. [Preston 1974, ARA&A, 12, 257; arxiv.org/html/2409.02547]

### Magnetic Fields

- Ap/Bp stars carry **global, large-scale magnetic fields** that are typically well described by a tilted magnetic dipole. Fields range from **a few hundred gauss to tens of kilogauss** (roughly 1–20 kG for most stars). [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars; Preston 1974]
- The record field strength among main-sequence stars is held by **HD 215441 (Babcock's Star)** at **~33.5 kG (3.35 T)**, discovered by Horace Babcock. [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars; MNRAS 402, 1883 (2010)]
- HD 75049 rivals Babcock's star, reaching **30 kG** (polar dipole field ~42 kG). [MNRAS 402, 1883, Elkin et al. 2010]
- Two competing origin theories exist:
  1. **Fossil field hypothesis**: the field is a relic from the interstellar medium, frozen into the star during formation. [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]
  2. **Magnetic instability / dynamo** in the radiative zone. [A&A 2013, aa20262-12]
- The fossil hypothesis is currently favoured because the fields are large-scale, stable, and do not show the complex activity cycles associated with dynamo processes. [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]

### Chemical Peculiarities

- Surface abundances of **silicon (Si), chromium (Cr), strontium (Sr), and europium (Eu)** are enhanced by factors of 10 to 10,000 relative to solar values (up to ~+1.5 dex for europium in CP2 stars). [Wikipedia: Chemically peculiar star; arxiv.org/html/2409.02547; Preston 1974]
- Even larger overabundances are seen in **praseodymium (Pr) and neodymium (Nd)** in some stars. [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]
- Common elements such as iron-peak nuclei can be simultaneously depleted at some surface latitudes and enhanced at others. [arxiv.org/html/2409.02547]
- The peculiarities are driven by **atomic diffusion**: the competition between **gravitational settling** (pulling heavier atoms toward the stellar interior) and **radiative levitation** (photon momentum pushing partially ionised heavy atoms outward). In Ap/Bp stars, stable, non-convective envelopes allow diffusion to operate efficiently. [arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0304424; Wikipedia: Radiative levitation]
- The magnetic field controls where elements accumulate on the surface, creating **chemical spots** spatially correlated with the magnetic geometry. [arxiv.org/html/2409.02547; MNRAS 444, 1442, Silvester et al. 2014]

### Oblique Rotator Model

- The standard theoretical framework for Ap/Bp variability is the **oblique rotator model**: the global magnetic dipole is inclined at an angle β to the stellar rotation axis. As the star rotates, observers see different aspects of the magnetic field and the associated chemical spots, producing periodic photometric and spectroscopic variations with the rotation period. [MNRAS 361, 595 (2005); Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]
- A simple centred dipole is a good first approximation for most stars, though higher-order multipole components are detected via **Zeeman Doppler Imaging** in many cases. [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars; MNRAS 444, 1442, Silvester et al. 2014]
- Rotation periods range from **~0.5 days to over 160 days** (some known to exceed decades); the slow rotation compared with normal A/B stars is attributed partly to magnetic braking. [Wikipedia: Alpha2 CVn variable; Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars]

### Spectral Variability

- Spectral line strengths, equivalent widths, and radial velocities all vary periodically with the rotation period as chemical spots rotate in and out of view. [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars; A&A 2018, aa33037-18]
- The **Alpha2 Canum Venaticorum (ACV) variables** are the photometric manifestation of this rotation-driven variability; brightness changes are typically **0.01–0.1 magnitudes** over periods of 0.5–160 days. [Wikipedia: Alpha2 CVn variable]
- Space photometry missions (TESS, Kepler/K2, BRITE) have revolutionised the study of Ap/Bp rotational variability, revealing previously unknown complexity in light curves. [A&A 2018, aa33037-18; A&A 2018, aa32938-18]

### Notable Examples

| Star | Notes |
|------|-------|
| **Alpha2 Canum Venaticorum (Cor Caroli)** | Prototype of ACV variables; spectral type A0pSiEuHg; magnetic field ~5,000× Earth's; period 5.47 days; magnitude varies 2.84–2.98. [Wikipedia: Cor Caroli] |
| **HD 215441 (Babcock's Star)** | Strongest known magnetic field on any main-sequence star: ~33.5 kG. [Wikipedia: Ap and Bp stars; ADS: 1989ApJ...344..876L] |
| **Epsilon Ursae Majoris (Alioth)** | Brightest Ap star in the sky; conspicuous TESS light curve variability. [Wikimedia Commons: EpsUMaLightCurve.png, CC BY-SA 4.0] |
| **56 Arietis (SX Arietis)** | Prototype of SX Ari variables (hot Bp silicon stars); rotationally variable. [Wikipedia: 56 Arietis] |
| **HD 101065 (Przybylski's Star)** | Extreme roAp star; overabundances of rare-earth elements including actinides; pulsation period 12.15 min. [Wikipedia: Przybylski's Star] |

### Rapidly Oscillating Ap Stars (roAp)

- A subset of Ap stars — the **rapidly oscillating Ap (roAp) stars** — additionally pulsate in high-overtone, low-degree, non-radial pressure modes with periods of **5–23 minutes**. [Wikipedia: Rapidly oscillating Ap star]
- The first roAp star recognised as such was **HD 101065**, with pulsations detected by **Donald Kurtz in 1978** using South African Astronomical Observatory photometry. [Wikipedia: Rapidly oscillating Ap star]
- About **60+ roAp stars** are known, with TESS finding many new candidates. [Wikipedia: Rapidly oscillating Ap star; MNRAS 510, 5743 (2022)]
- The pulsation axis aligns with the magnetic axis (**oblique pulsator model**), so pulsation amplitudes are modulated by the rotation period. Strong magnetic fields near the poles may suppress convection and drive the oscillations. [Wikipedia: Rapidly oscillating Ap star]
- roAp stars sit at the main-sequence end of the **delta Scuti instability strip**. [Wikipedia: Rapidly oscillating Ap star]
