Cislunar

Cislunar space is the region of space encompassing Earth’s gravitational neighbourhood from geosynchronous orbit (~36,000 km altitude) out to and including the Moon’s orbital path (~384,400 km). The name derives from the Latin cis, meaning “on this side of,” combined with lunar. The total extent of the region is roughly 550,000 km, measured from Earth’s centre. Beyond this boundary lies interplanetary space; transit arcs to the Moon are sometimes called translunar trajectories, a narrower term referring specifically to the flight path rather than the full region.

Orbital dynamics. Spacecraft in cislunar space are subject to the combined gravity of the Earth, the Moon, and the Sun, producing complex, non-Keplerian trajectories that cannot be described by simple two-body solutions. The Earth–Moon three-body system has five Lagrange points: L1 and L2 lie along the Earth–Moon line (L1 between the bodies, L2 beyond the Moon) and are dynamically unstable; L3 sits on the far side of the Moon’s orbit and is similarly unstable; L4 and L5 lead and trail the Moon by 60° and are stable. Spacecraft can be placed in families of orbits around these points, called halo orbits, without large fuel expenditure, making them attractive for communication relays and staging depots.

Key orbital families. Two orbital regimes are of particular operational interest. The near-rectilinear halo orbit (NRHO) is a highly elongated orbit around the lunar L2 point with a period of roughly seven days and an altitude above the lunar poles ranging from approximately 1,500 km at closest approach to 70,000 km at the far point. Its eccentricity and inclination keep it in near-continuous contact with Earth and free from long lunar shadow periods. The distant retrograde orbit (DRO) is a large, quasi-stable orbit centred on the Moon at roughly 60,000–70,000 km that is naturally robust against perturbations, requiring minimal station-keeping.

Exploration history. Human cislunar operations began with NASA’s Apollo programme. Apollo 8 (December 1968) was the first crewed voyage beyond low Earth orbit; Apollo 17 (December 1972) was the last. No humans entered cislunar space for more than 50 years thereafter. The CAPSTONE CubeSat satellite (November 2022) became the first spacecraft to operate in an NRHO, validating navigation and station-keeping models. Artemis I (November 2022) sent an uncrewed Orion capsule on a distant retrograde orbit test flight. Artemis II (April 2026) carried a crew of four on the first crewed cislunar voyage since Apollo 17, completing a free-return trajectory around the Moon. NASA’s planned Gateway station was designed as a permanent NRHO outpost to support lunar surface operations, though its development was paused in March 2026.

Scientific and economic value. Cislunar space is of growing interest as a resource-rich and strategically important domain. Confirmed water ice at the lunar poles could be processed into propellant via in-situ resource utilisation (ISRU), reducing the cost of missions beyond the Earth–Moon system and serving as a stepping stone toward Solar System exploration. The lunar far side, accessible from cislunar L2, offers a radio-quiet environment screened from terrestrial interference, making it attractive for low-frequency radio telescope arrays. Cislunar space is also increasingly regarded as a proving ground for deep-space mission architecture, life support, and autonomous navigation technologies required for crewed missions to Mars.
