Kerr black hole

A black hole is described as a Kerr black hole when it possesses angular momentum — that is, when it rotates. In 1963, New Zealand mathematician Roy Kerr derived an exact solution to Einstein's field equations of general relativity describing the spacetime geometry around a rotating, uncharged black hole. Published in Physical Review Letters (11, 237–238), the Kerr solution closed a 47-year gap left open since Karl Schwarzschild described the non-rotating case in 1915; the additional complexity of broken spherical symmetry had resisted all earlier attempts. Because all astrophysical objects retain some rotation from their formation, virtually every black hole in nature is expected to be a Kerr black hole rather than an idealised Schwarzschild one.

Spin Parameter and Horizons

A Kerr black hole is characterised by two quantities: its mass M and its spin parameter a = J/(Mc), where J is the angular momentum. The parameter a ranges from zero (recovering the Schwarzschild solution) up to a maximum of GM/c², known as the extremal limit. Exceeding this limit would expose the singularity to the outside universe, forming a naked singularity; the cosmic censorship conjecture holds that physical processes cannot produce this outcome. Unlike the Schwarzschild case, a Kerr black hole possesses two distinct event horizons: an outer event horizon, which is the classical surface of no return, and an inner Cauchy horizon, where predictability of physics breaks down. The singularity itself is not a point but a ring lying in the equatorial plane, a direct consequence of the geometry imposed by rotation.

Ergosphere and Frame Dragging

One of the most distinctive features of a Kerr black hole is the ergosphere, a region bounded on the outside by the stationary limit surface and on the inside by the outer event horizon. The ergosphere is oblate: it touches the horizon at the poles but extends furthest from the horizon at the equator. Within the ergosphere, the rotation of spacetime itself — frame dragging, also known as the Lense–Thirring effect — is so strong that no particle or photon can remain stationary with respect to distant observers; all are dragged in the direction of rotation. Crucially, particles within the ergosphere are not inside the event horizon and can still escape to infinity. Frame dragging was confirmed experimentally by the Gravity Probe B satellite mission in 2011.

Energy Extraction and Astrophysical Importance

Because the ergosphere permits escape, energy can be extracted from a rotating black hole. In the Penrose process (1969), a particle entering the ergosphere splits into two fragments; one falls inward carrying negative energy, while the other escapes with more energy than the original particle possessed. For a maximally rotating Kerr black hole, this mechanism can recover up to approximately 20.7% of the rest-mass energy. The Blandford–Znajek mechanism extends this principle to magnetic fields threading the ergosphere, and is widely invoked to explain the relativistic jets observed from quasars and other active galactic nuclei, as well as gamma-ray bursts.

Rotation also shifts the innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO), which governs where an accretion disc can exist and how efficiently it radiates. For a non-rotating (Schwarzschild) black hole, the ISCO lies at 6GM/c²; for a prograde orbit around a maximally spinning Kerr black hole, it shrinks to GM/c², enabling far greater accretion efficiency. Spin measurements based on this effect find that the stellar black hole Cygnus X-1 has a spin parameter a* > 0.9985, approaching the extremal limit, while the supermassive black hole M87* has a* > 0.4.

Observational Confirmation

The detection of gravitational waves from the binary black hole merger GW150914 by LIGO in 2016 provided direct confirmation that merging Kerr black holes exist and that the resulting remnant is itself a Kerr black hole, consistent with general relativity. Extensions of the Kerr solution include the Kerr–Newman metric (1965), which adds electric charge; astrophysical black holes are expected to be electrically neutral in practice, making the Kerr solution the physically relevant description. The mathematical stability of the Kerr solution under perturbations was formally proven by Klainerman and Szeftel in 2022, settling a long-standing open problem in mathematical general relativity.
