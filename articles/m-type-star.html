M-type Star

An M-type star is the coolest and most numerous class of star in the Harvard spectral classification system, with surface temperatures from about 2,300 K to 3,900 K and a characteristic red-orange colour produced by molecular absorption — particularly titanium oxide (TiO) bands — dominating their spectra. M-type stars range from tiny, dim red dwarfs on the main sequence to vast, luminous red giants and supergiants such as Betelgeuse.

Spectral Classification and Physical Properties

The Harvard spectral sequence orders stars O–B–A–F–G–K–M by decreasing surface temperature; M-type stars occupy the cool end of this sequence. A full spectral type combines the letter M, a numeric subtype from 0 (hottest, ~3,850 K) to 9 (coolest, ~2,300 K), and a luminosity class Roman numeral — for example, M2V for a main-sequence dwarf or M1Ia for a supergiant.

The defining spectral signature is strong absorption by titanium oxide (TiO) molecules, which form in the cool stellar atmosphere; TiO bands are detectable at about M0 and dominate by M5. Vanadium oxide (VO) bands appear at late subtypes, and water (H2O) absorption becomes significant in the coolest M dwarfs.

Red Dwarfs: The Most Common Stars in the Galaxy

Main-sequence M-type stars, known as red dwarfs, account for approximately 76% of all main-sequence stars in the solar neighbourhood and roughly 70% of all stars in the Milky Way by number. Despite their numerical dominance, none is visible to the naked eye owing to their extreme intrinsic faintness.

Red dwarf masses span 0.08–0.6 solar masses (M☉), luminosities from about 0.0003 to 0.07 L☉, and radii between roughly 9% and 60% of the Sun's. Stars below 0.35 M☉ are fully convective throughout — heat is transported by convection rather than radiation across the entire star — preventing helium from accumulating at the core and allowing the full hydrogen supply to serve as fuel. This structure produces extraordinary lifetimes: a 0.1 M☉ M dwarf can fuse hydrogen for approximately 10 trillion years, far exceeding the current age of the universe (~13.8 billion years).

Flare Activity

Many M-type stars exhibit intense magnetic activity and are classified as flare stars, capable of doubling their brightness in minutes. Proxima Centauri (spectral type M5.5Ve), the nearest star to the Sun at 4.2465 light-years (1.302 pc), carries a magnetic field roughly 600 times stronger than the Sun's. Between January 2016 and March 2018, 24 flares were recorded from Proxima Centauri with bolometric energies from 1030.6 to 1033.5 erg. In 2019, the star emitted a flare some 14,000 times brighter than its average emission in ultraviolet wavelengths. The 'active' flare phase lasts approximately 1 billion years for early M0 dwarfs but may extend to 8 billion years or more for late M8 stars.

Habitability of M-dwarf Planetary Systems

The habitable zone around an M dwarf lies very close to the star. Proxima Centauri b, the nearest known exoplanet, orbits at only ~0.05 AU with a period of ~11.2 days. Several challenges affect habitability at such distances. Tidal locking is likely, keeping one hemisphere permanently facing the star and creating extreme temperature contrasts. Habitable-zone planets receive intense X-ray and ultraviolet flux; flare-driven proton events can deplete a planet's ozone column by up to 94%. Stellar winds and coronal mass ejections may strip planetary atmospheres over time. In addition, M dwarf light is heavily infrared-shifted, which complicates photosynthesis based on visible-wavelength chlorophyll.

The question of habitability remains unresolved. Cloud formation on tidally locked planets may moderate temperature extremes, and rocky planets are extremely common around M dwarfs. TRAPPIST-1, an ultra-cool M8 dwarf, hosts seven roughly Earth-sized planets, three of which orbit within its habitable zone.

M Giants and Supergiants

Not all M-type stars are dim dwarfs. Post-main-sequence stars that have expanded into giants or supergiants also carry M spectral types because their surface temperatures are comparably cool. Betelgeuse (α Orionis, spectral type M1–M2 Ia–Iab) is the prototypical M supergiant: with a surface temperature of ~3,600–3,800 K, a luminosity of approximately 65,000–87,000 L☉, and a radius 640–764 times that of the Sun, it is large enough to engulf the orbit of Mars if placed at the centre of the Solar System.

Mira (o Ceti, spectral class M5–M9 IIIe) is a long-period pulsating variable on the asymptotic giant branch, with pulsation periods exceeding 100 days and visual amplitude changes greater than 2.5 magnitudes. Mira variables undergo mass loss of ~10−7 to 10−6 M☉ per year and are in the penultimate phase of stellar evolution before shedding planetary nebulae and becoming white dwarfs.

At the cool end of the M sequence (M9), the distinction between the lowest-mass M dwarfs and brown dwarfs — sub-stellar objects that cannot sustain hydrogen fusion — becomes indistinct, marking the boundary of the hydrogen-burning minimum at approximately 0.08 M☉.
