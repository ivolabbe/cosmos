Starlink

Starlink is a broadband internet constellation built and operated by SpaceX. As of early 2026 it has more than 10,000 active satellites in low Earth orbit (LEO), making up roughly 65% of all working satellites. It is the largest satellite constellation ever launched. Since its first operational launch in May 2019, the constellation has become a growing problem for astronomers: the satellites leave bright streaks across telescope images and leak radio signals into protected bands.

A 333-second exposure from the Blanco 4-metre telescope at Cerro Tololo Inter-American Observatory (CTIO) showing at least 19 streaks left by Starlink satellites launched in November 2019.
CTIO/NOIRLab/NSF/AURA/DELVE

Naked-eye appearance

Starlink satellites are visible to the naked eye, especially in the days right after a launch. Fresh batches travel in a tight line, producing a chain of bright, evenly spaced dots that drift together across the sky. Once they spread out to their working altitude (around 550 km for the first generation), each satellite looks like a steady, unblinking point moving from horizon to horizon, easy to tell apart from aircraft because it does not flash.

They are easiest to spot one to two hours after sunset or before sunrise, when the ground is dark but the satellites are still lit by the Sun. The first batches turned out to be far brighter than anyone expected, reaching apparent magnitude 5, bright enough to see with the unaided eye.

Impact on optical astronomy

When a satellite crosses a long-exposure image it leaves a bright streak, and the affected pixels are lost. Twilight observations are hit hardest, because that is when the satellites are brightest and most numerous in the sky. Twilight is also prime time for finding near-Earth asteroids, outer Solar System objects, and optical counterparts to gravitational-wave events.

Data from the Zwicky Transient Facility (ZTF) show that the share of twilight images with at least one satellite streak grew from 0.5% in late 2019 to nearly 20% by mid-2021. With more than 10,000 satellites now in orbit, nearly every twilight image is expected to contain at least one streak. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory, with its wider field of view and higher sensitivity, will be even more affected.

Impact on radio astronomy

Starlink satellites broadcast internet signals in the Ku-, Ka-, and V-band frequencies. Some of that radiation spills into neighbouring bands set aside for radio astronomy. A study using 76 million images from a prototype Square Kilometre Array (SKA) station found Starlink signals in up to 30% of images, with over 112,000 individual detections from 1,806 satellites. Some were transmitting at 150.8 MHz, a band reserved for radio astronomy.

Newer satellites are worse. The v2 mini design puts out up to 32 times more stray electromagnetic radiation than the v1.0 satellites, making them roughly 10 million times brighter at radio wavelengths than the faint sources that instruments like LOFAR are trying to detect. Unlike optical streaks, radio leakage cannot simply be blocked: radio signals do not cut off sharply at a frequency boundary.

Brightness mitigation

SpaceX has tried several ways to make its satellites dimmer. An early test satellite with a dark coating, called DarkSat (STARLINK-1130), cut brightness by about 0.5 magnitudes but caused overheating and did not help much at near-infrared wavelengths, so the idea was dropped. A fold-out sunshade design called VisorSat did better, cutting brightness by 1.29 magnitudes (down to 31% as bright as the original v1.0). The v1.5 satellites reduced scattered sunlight by about 40%, reaching roughly magnitude 6.8 in normal operation. The v2 mini satellites have about four times the surface area of v1.0 but keep similar optical brightness using mirror films, black paint on exposed parts, and tilted solar panels.

In June 2020 the SATCON1 workshop, run by the American Astronomical Society (AAS) and NOIRLab with over 250 participants, concluded that large low-orbit constellations will fundamentally change ground-based optical and infrared astronomy. The workshop recommended that all satellites stay fainter than 7th magnitude. No version has consistently met that target.

Community response

A follow-up workshop, SATCON2, took place in July 2021 with over 350 participants from 40 countries. It set up working groups on observations, software, outreach, and policy, and called for a funded effort to tackle the problem. The community has built tools like TrailMask, software that flags, masks, and partly repairs satellite trails in images. The International Astronomical Union (IAU) has raised formal concerns about what satellite constellations mean for the night sky and for astronomical heritage.

Starlink is not the only constellation in play. OneWeb, Amazon Kuiper, and others are building their own large fleets. OneWeb satellites orbit at around 1,200 km, where they stay sunlit longer and affect observations through entire summer nights at high latitudes.
