K Capture

K capture is a form of radioactive decay in which the nucleus of a proton-rich atom absorbs one of its own electrons from the innermost (K) electron shell, converting a proton into a neutron and emitting an electron neutrino. The process is mediated by the weak nuclear force and leaves a vacancy in the K shell that is subsequently filled by outer-shell electrons, producing characteristic X-rays or Auger electrons. K capture was first proposed theoretically by Gian-Carlo Wick in 1934 and observed experimentally by Luis Alvarez in 1937.

Leading-order Feynman diagrams for electron capture decay at the quark level. An up quark in the proton emits a W+ boson that is absorbed by the incoming electron, converting the up quark to a down quark and producing an electron neutrino (νe).

The fundamental reaction is p + e− → n + νe. A proton in the nucleus captures an inner-shell electron and converts to a neutron, while an electron neutrino carries away the full decay energy. Unlike beta-minus decay (which emits an electron) or positron emission (which emits a positron), K capture produces no charged particle in the primary reaction. The 'K' refers to the principal quantum number n = 1 shell, the innermost shell, which has the highest electron density at the nucleus. Electrons from the L shell (n = 2) and higher shells may also be captured in analogous L-capture and M-capture processes, but these are far less probable.

Energy conditions. K capture occurs when the mass difference between parent and daughter atom is positive but less than 1.022 MeV (twice the electron rest-mass energy). If the mass difference exceeds 1.022 MeV, positron emission becomes energetically possible and typically competes with K capture. If the mass difference is less than 1.022 MeV, positron emission is forbidden and K capture is the only available decay pathway.

Secondary emissions. When the K-shell vacancy is created, an electron from an outer shell falls in to fill the gap, releasing energy equal to the difference in binding energies between the two shells. This energy is emitted either as a characteristic X-ray photon specific to the daughter element, or it may eject another outer electron via the Auger effect. Whether X-ray emission or the Auger effect dominates depends on the atomic number: lighter elements favour Auger emission, heavier elements favour X-ray fluorescence.

Key Examples

Beryllium-7 (7Be) has a half-life of 53.28 days and decays exclusively by K capture (Q ≈ 0.862 MeV, below the 1.022 MeV threshold for positron emission): 7Be + e− → 7Li + νe. The neutrinos are monoenergetic, with 90% carrying 0.861 MeV (decay to the 7Li ground state) and 10% carrying 0.383 MeV (decay to an excited state). 7Be is the only isotope for which the decay rate has been observed to differ measurably between metallic and insulating chemical environments, because beryllium's valence electrons sit unusually close to the nucleus.

Potassium-40 (40K) has a half-life of 1.248 × 109 years and undergoes branched decay: approximately 89% beta-minus decay to 40Ca and approximately 11% electron capture to 40Ar. About 99% of the K-capture decays produce an excited state of 40Ar, which de-excites by emitting a 1.46 MeV gamma ray detectable in environmental radioactivity surveys. The accumulation of 40Ar from this process is the basis of potassium-argon (K-Ar) and argon-argon (Ar-Ar) geochronology, used to date rocks from millions to billions of years old.

Astrophysical Significance

In the interiors of main-sequence stars such as the Sun, 7Be produced by 3He + 4He fusion captures a free electron from the surrounding plasma, yielding 7Li + νe in the pp-II branch of the proton–proton chain. This branch accounts for approximately 16.4% of the total solar luminosity. The 7Be neutrinos have been observed by the Borexino experiment to better than 3% precision. At solar interior conditions (temperature approximately 15 million K, density approximately 150 g cm−3), 7Be is fully ionised and captures free electrons from the plasma continuum rather than from a bound K shell.

Stars with initial masses of approximately 8–10 solar masses develop degenerate oxygen–neon–magnesium cores. When the core reaches a critical density of approximately 4 × 109 g cm−3, electrons are captured by 24Mg and 20Ne nuclei in rapid succession. This reduces the electron pressure supporting the degenerate core against gravity and triggers gravitational collapse, producing an electron-capture supernova. These events produce neutron stars with relatively low kick velocities. During supernova nucleosynthesis more broadly, cascades of electron captures on iron-group nuclei reduce the electron fraction and shift the composition toward neutron-rich isotopes.
