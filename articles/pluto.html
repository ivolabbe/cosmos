Pluto

Pluto is a dwarf planet in the Kuiper Belt, a disc of icy bodies stretching from roughly 30 to 50 AU beyond the orbit of Neptune. It is about two-thirds rock and one-third ice, with a diameter of 2,377 km – roughly two-thirds the size of Earth's Moon – and a surface gravity just 6.3% of Earth's. Long counted as the ninth planet after its discovery in 1930, Pluto was reclassified in 2006 by the International Astronomical Union (IAU) as a dwarf planet: a body that orbits the Sun and is massive enough to be roughly spherical (hydrostatic equilibrium), but has not cleared its orbital neighbourhood of other debris.

Discovery and Naming

Clyde W. Tombaugh discovered Pluto on 18 February 1930 while working as a 24-year-old assistant at Lowell Observatory in Flagstaff, Arizona. He found it by comparing photographic plates with a blink comparator. An 11-year-old Oxford schoolgirl, Venetia Burney, suggested the name, honouring the Roman god of the underworld; the initials PL also nod to Percival Lowell, whose search programme had set the discovery in motion.

The 2006 reclassification followed the 2005 discovery of Eris, a trans-Neptunian object slightly more massive than Pluto. Keeping Pluto as a full planet would have meant admitting several similar bodies to the same category. Many planetary scientists still dispute the IAU definition.

Interior and Orbit

Pluto's interior appears to be layered: a rocky core roughly 1,700 km across, surrounded by a mantle of water ice. New Horizons data suggest a liquid water ocean, 100–180 km thick, lies between the core and mantle, kept from freezing by heat from radioactive decay.

Pluto follows a highly elliptical orbit (eccentricity 0.2488), tilted 17.16° to the ecliptic. It orbits in a 3:2 resonance with Neptune – completing two orbits for every three of Neptune's. Objects sharing this rhythm are called plutinos. Although Pluto's orbit brings it closer to the Sun than Neptune near perihelion (29.7 AU), the resonance ensures the two never come close to each other.

Surface and Atmosphere

Pluto's surface is remarkably varied. The bright nitrogen-ice plain Sputnik Planitia – the western lobe of the heart-shaped Tombaugh Regio – spans roughly 1,400 × 1,200 km and has no detectable impact craters, suggesting it is less than 10 million years old. Slow-churning convection cells, averaging 33 km across, keep the surface geologically active. Water-ice mountains rise 2–3.5 km above the surrounding plains, comparable in height to the Rocky Mountains, while older terrain preserves craters up to 260 km in diameter.

The dark equatorial band called Cthulhu Regio gets its reddish-brown colour from tholins – complex organic molecules that form when ultraviolet light breaks down methane and nitrogen on the surface.

Pluto has a thin atmosphere, mostly nitrogen with traces of methane and carbon monoxide, at a surface pressure of roughly 1 Pa (about 10–5 of Earth's). The atmosphere swells as Pluto approaches perihelion and collapses as it moves away. A blue haze of tholins scattering sunlight extends up to ~1,600 km above the surface.

Moons

Pluto has five known moons, all named after figures from underworld mythology. The largest, Charon, was discovered by James Christy at the US Naval Observatory in 1978. At 1,212 km across, it is the largest moon relative to its parent body in the Solar System. Pluto and Charon are effectively a binary system: their common centre of mass lies outside Pluto's surface, and both are tidally locked to each other. The Hubble Space Telescope revealed two smaller moons, Nix and Hydra, in 2005; Kerberos and Styx followed in 2011 and 2012.

New Horizons

NASA launched the New Horizons spacecraft on 19 January 2006 – the same year Pluto lost its planetary status. On 14 July 2015 it made the first close flyby of the Pluto system, passing within 12,500 km of the surface at over 84,000 km/h. Instead of the inert, featureless world many had expected, New Horizons found a geologically complex and active one. The spacecraft went on to fly past the Kuiper Belt object Arrokoth on 1 January 2019, and as of 2025 continues outward at roughly 62 AU from Earth.
