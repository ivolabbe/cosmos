Mare

A mare (plural: maria, from the Latin for "sea") is a large, dark, flat plain on the Moon composed of ancient basaltic lava that flooded impact basins billions of years ago. The name was applied by 17th-century telescopic astronomers who mistakenly believed these dark regions were bodies of water. Maria are visually distinct from the brighter, heavily cratered lunar highlands and cover approximately 16% of the total lunar surface, overwhelmingly concentrated on the near side facing Earth; they are the dark patches visible to the naked eye on the full Moon.

The near side of the Moon showing the prominent dark maria — ancient basaltic lava plains — contrasting with the brighter, heavily cratered highlands. Major features include Mare Imbrium (upper left), Mare Tranquillitatis (centre right), Mare Serenitatis (upper right), and Oceanus Procellarum (left).
NASA

Maria formed through a two-step process. Giant asteroid impacts approximately 3.9 billion years ago excavated the large multi-ring impact basins during the Late Heavy Bombardment. Magma then welled up from hundreds of kilometres within the Moon’s interior, erupting through fissures to flood these basins over hundreds of millions of years; most volcanic activity occurred between 3.0 and 3.5 billion years ago. Spectroscopic and sample analyses confirm that mare basalts are iron- and titanium-rich tholeiitic lavas, significantly darker and denser than the surrounding highland material, giving them their low albedo. The 2020 Chang’e-5 sample-return mission retrieved basalt from Oceanus Procellarum dated to 2.03 billion years ago, extending the known volcanic history of the Moon well beyond the range established by geochemical analyses of Apollo samples.

Maria cover approximately 26% of the lunar near side but only about 2% of the far side, an asymmetry attributed to the thinner crust on the near side permitting easier volcanic intrusion. The largest individual mare is Oceanus Procellarum (“Ocean of Storms”), which spans roughly 4,000,000 km2 and is the only lunar dark plain designated an oceanus; NASA GRAIL gravity data revealed ancient rectangular rift structures beneath it, suggesting a tectonic rather than pure impact-basin origin. Smaller related features carry the IAU designations lacus (lake), sinus (bay), and palus (marsh). Maria are also associated with mascons — dense concentrations of solidified basalt that produce positive gravitational anomalies detectable by orbiting spacecraft and can destabilise spacecraft orbits. Crater-counting surveys suggest the youngest lava flows on the Moon may be as recent as 1.2 billion years ago, though these estimates await confirmation by sample return.

The nomenclature system remains that of Giovanni Battista Riccioli, who assigned sea-related Latin names to the dark plains in his Almagestum Novum (1651) and named craters for astronomers; his scheme was later formally adopted by the International Astronomical Union. All six Apollo crewed landings targeted maria, which offered flat, accessible terrain: Apollo 11 (1969) landed in Mare Tranquillitatis, and Apollo 12 (1969) in Oceanus Procellarum. The true volcanic nature of the maria was confirmed definitively by Apollo sample analysis, which radiometrically dated the returned basalts to between 3.1 and 3.8 billion years ago — consistent with the geological epoch of intense Solar System volcanism.
