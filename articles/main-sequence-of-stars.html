Main Sequence of Stars

The main sequence is the broad diagonal band on a Hertzsprung–Russell diagram where the vast majority of stars spend most of their lives, generating energy by fusing hydrogen into helium in their cores. A star's position along this band is determined almost entirely by its mass, which governs its luminosity, surface temperature, and lifespan.

The prominent diagonal band running from the upper-left (hot, luminous O stars) to the lower-right (cool, dim M dwarfs) is the main sequence. Giants, supergiants, and white dwarfs occupy distinct regions off the main sequence.

The Hertzsprung–Russell Diagram

On a Hertzsprung–Russell diagram — a plot of stellar luminosity against surface temperature or spectral type — the main sequence appears as a prominent diagonal band running from the upper-left to the lower-right. When Ejnar Hertzsprung (1911) and Henry Norris Russell (1913) independently constructed the first such diagrams, this diagonal concentration immediately stood out as the dominant stellar population. Most stars in the solar neighbourhood lie on the main sequence; it is both the longest-lived and most populated stage of stellar evolution.

Hydrogen Fusion and Hydrostatic Equilibrium

A star is on the main sequence if it is actively fusing hydrogen into helium in its core and if that fusion energy supports the star against gravitational collapse — a state called hydrostatic equilibrium. This phase begins when gravitational contraction raises the core temperature high enough to ignite nuclear fusion, and it ends when core hydrogen is exhausted. Two fusion pathways operate, depending on stellar mass:

  • Proton–proton (PP) chain — dominates in stars of ≤ 1.5 M☉, including the Sun; directly fuses four protons into one helium-4 nucleus.
  • CNO cycle — dominates in stars of > 1.5 M☉, using carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen as catalysts. At approximately 1.5 M☉ both processes contribute equally.

Zero-Age Main Sequence and Terminal Age Main Sequence

The Zero-Age Main Sequence (ZAMS) is the theoretical curve on the H–R diagram marking where stars first arrive when core hydrogen fusion begins and gravitational contraction effectively ceases. As stars age, their luminosity and temperature slowly increase, so they drift slightly away from the ZAMS over time. The corresponding endpoint — when core hydrogen is exhausted — is the Terminal Age Main Sequence (TAMS).

Mass–Luminosity Relation and Stellar Lifetimes

For main-sequence stars in the range 0.1–50 M☉, luminosity scales with mass as L ∝ M3.5. A star twice as massive as the Sun is approximately 23.5 ≈ 11 times more luminous. Because fuel supply scales with mass while the burn rate scales as M3.5, the main-sequence lifetime shrinks rapidly with increasing mass: τMS ≈ 1010 yr × (M/M☉)−2.5. An O5 star of ~40 M☉ exhausts its core hydrogen in about 1 million years, whereas an M5 red dwarf of ~0.2 M☉ has a main-sequence lifetime exceeding 1 trillion years — far longer than the present age of the universe.

Spectral Types on the Main Sequence

The Morgan–Keenan (MK) classification system assigns luminosity class V (Roman numeral five) to main-sequence, or 'dwarf', stars. The sequence from hottest to coolest — O, B, A, F, G, K, M — is traditionally remembered by the mnemonic "Oh Be A Fine Guy/Girl, Kiss Me." The Sun is classified G2V, with an effective temperature of ~5,778 K, mass 1 M☉, and spectral type placing it in the middle of the sequence. At the hot end, O-type stars reach ~40,000 K and luminosities of ~500,000 L☉; at the cool end, M-type dwarfs have temperatures as low as ~2,400 K and luminosities as faint as 0.001 L☉.

Stellar Interior Structure

Energy transport inside main-sequence stars varies systematically with mass. Very low-mass stars (below ~0.4 M☉) are fully convective throughout. Solar-mass stars (~0.3–1.5 M☉) have a radiative core surrounded by a convective outer envelope; in the Sun, the convective zone begins at approximately 71% of its radius. High-mass stars (above ~1.5–2 M☉), where the CNO cycle's strong temperature sensitivity drives vigorous energy generation, possess a convective core surrounded by a radiative outer region.

The Vogt–Russell Theorem

First formulated independently by Heinrich Vogt (published 1931, Astronomische Nachrichten) and Henry Norris Russell (1931, Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society), the Vogt–Russell theorem states that the internal structure of a star in hydrostatic and thermal equilibrium is uniquely determined by its total mass and chemical composition. For main-sequence stars this is an excellent approximation: given mass and composition, all observable properties — radius, luminosity, surface temperature — are fixed. The theorem breaks down near extreme conditions such as rapid rotation, strong magnetic fields, or electron degeneracy, but remains the conceptual foundation of stellar structure theory.

The Main Sequence Turn-Off Point

In a star cluster, all members formed at roughly the same time. More massive stars exhaust their core hydrogen first and depart the main sequence towards the red giant region, while lower-mass stars remain. The point on the cluster's H–R diagram where stars are currently leaving the main sequence is the turn-off point. Because stellar lifetime scales precisely with mass through the mass–luminosity relation, the luminosity of the turn-off point directly yields the cluster age — a technique called isochrone fitting. Globular clusters show turn-off points corresponding to ages of 10–13 billion years; young open clusters show high-luminosity turn-offs corresponding to ages of only tens of millions of years.

The Sun's Position

The Sun has been on the main sequence for approximately 4.6 billion years and has roughly 5 billion years of hydrogen-burning lifetime remaining, placing it slightly past the midpoint of its main-sequence life. Over this time the Sun has slowly grown approximately 30% more luminous as helium accumulates in the core, raising the mean molecular weight and gradually increasing the core temperature and energy generation rate.
