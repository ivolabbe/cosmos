Sputnik

Sputnik 1, launched by the Soviet Union on 4 October 1957, was the world's first artificial Earth satellite, a polished metal sphere roughly the size of a beach ball that orbited Earth for three months, transmitting radio signals detectable worldwide. Its launch marked the beginning of the Space Age. The word "sputnik" derives from Russian, meaning "fellow traveller," a direct parallel of the Latin root underlying the English word "satellite."

A replica of Sputnik 1 on display at the National Air and Space Museum, Smithsonian Institution, Washington D.C. The replica faithfully reproduces the 58 cm polished metal sphere and four whip antennas of the 1957 original.

Design and operation

Sputnik 1 was a hollow sphere 58 cm in diameter, constructed from an aluminium-magnesium-titanium alloy with a highly polished surface designed to reflect sunlight and help ground observers track it. It weighed 83.6 kg and carried two pairs of whip antennas (2.4 m and 2.9 m long) trailing behind the sphere. Three silver-zinc batteries powered two radio transmitters broadcasting on 20.005 MHz and 40.002 MHz, producing the iconic "beep-beep" pulses heard worldwide by amateur radio operators and broadcast on commercial radio.

The pulse timing and duration were not arbitrary: changes in the pattern encoded temperature and pressure data from inside the satellite. If the signal had stopped abruptly, it would have meant thermal failure or a puncture by a micrometeorite. The batteries were designed for two weeks but lasted 22 days. The silent satellite then stayed in orbit for another two and a half months before re-entering the atmosphere on 4 January 1958, having completed 1,440 orbits. Its orbit ranged from 215 km at perigee to 939 km at apogee, tilted at 65.1° to the equator, so it passed over most of Earth's populated land surface.

Observers who spotted the satellite as a moving point of light were actually seeing the much larger rocket booster, not the small sphere itself. Tracking was carried out by Soviet ground stations, the Lovell radio telescope at Jodrell Bank in Britain, and amateur radio operators worldwide.

Origins and the space race

The satellite emerged from the International Geophysical Year (IGY), which ran from 1 July 1957 to 31 December 1958 with 67 participating nations. In July 1955 the US announced plans to launch small Earth satellites during the IGY; four days later, Soviet scientists announced the same intention. The rivalry was already well under way before October 1957.

The original Soviet ambition was a complex scientific satellite called "Object D." When it proved too difficult to finish in time, a simpler design called PS-1 (Prosteyshiy Sputnik, "simplest satellite") was used instead. The launch rocket was the 8K71PS, a stripped-down variant of the R-7 Semyorka, the world's first intercontinental ballistic missile, with the nuclear warhead replaced by the satellite.

The man behind the Soviet programme was its chief designer, Sergei Korolev, whose identity was kept secret throughout the Cold War to prevent assassination attempts. His name was not publicly revealed until after his death on 14 January 1966. Korolev had survived arrest and imprisonment during Stalin's Great Purge, spending months in a Siberian gold mine before being transferred to a prison research facility and released in 1944. He went on to lead Soviet rocket development and proposed using the R-7 for an orbital satellite in December 1954.

In the United States, the launch shook confidence in American technological superiority. It was widely called the "Sputnik crisis." The real fear was not the satellite itself but what it meant: the same rocket could deliver nuclear warheads across intercontinental distances. When the US Vanguard rocket failed publicly in December 1957, the worry only grew. Within months, Congress passed the National Defense Education Act (1958) boosting science and mathematics funding, and the Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA, later DARPA) was established in February 1958. NASA was created on 1 October 1958, formed from the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA). The US Explorer 1 satellite, launched 31 January 1958, produced the first major scientific result of the space race: the discovery of the Van Allen radiation belts.

Sputnik 2 and Sputnik 3

Only three spacecraft were officially named "Sputnik" by the Soviet Union. Spacecraft later designated Sputnik 4–25 in Western reporting bore different official Soviet names that were unknown to Western observers at the time.

Satellite Launch date Mass Key payload Historic first
Sputnik 1 4 Oct 1957 83.6 kg Radio transmitter, temperature/pressure sensors First artificial satellite
Sputnik 2 3 Nov 1957 ~500 kg Dog Laika, spectrophotometers, Geiger counters First living creature in orbit
Sputnik 3 15 May 1958 1,327 kg 12-instrument geophysical laboratory First comprehensive scientific satellite

Sputnik 2 carried Laika, a stray Moscow dog and the first living creature in orbit. The USSR initially reported she had survived for several days, but research published in 2002 revealed she died from cabin overheating within five to seven hours of launch. The capsule re-entered on 14 April 1958. Sputnik 3 weighed roughly 100 times more than the heaviest US satellite then in orbit and carried 12 scientific instruments including mass spectrometers, Geiger counters, and magnetic field sensors.
