Earth

Earth is the third planet from the Sun, orbiting at a mean distance of 1.50 × 108 km (1.00 AU). It is the only planet known to harbour life, the only world in the Solar System with stable liquid water on its surface, and the densest of all the planets. Earth has one natural satellite, the Moon.

Atmosphere
Earth’s atmosphere is composed primarily of nitrogen (78%) and oxygen (21%), with trace amounts of argon, carbon dioxide, water vapour and other gases. The greenhouse effect — driven chiefly by water vapour, carbon dioxide, and methane — raises the mean surface temperature from roughly −18 °C (the bare radiative equilibrium value) to about +15 °C, maintaining conditions suitable for liquid water. An ozone layer in the stratosphere absorbs the majority of incoming solar ultraviolet radiation, shielding the surface and enabling complex life. Dynamic weather systems — driven by differential solar heating, the Coriolis effect, and latent heat release — redistribute energy from the equator toward the poles.

Structure
Earth has an equatorial diameter of 12,742 km, a mass of 5.97 × 1024 kg, and a mean density of 5.51 g/cm3 — the highest of any planet in the Solar System. Its interior is differentiated into a solid iron–nickel inner core, a liquid iron–nickel outer core, a thick silicate mantle, and a thin rocky crust. Convection currents in the outer core generate Earth’s global magnetic field through a self-sustaining dynamo mechanism. The mantle convects on timescales of tens of millions of years, driving plate tectonics — the slow motion and recycling of lithospheric plates that shapes the surface through volcanism, mountain building, and seafloor spreading.

Axial tilt and seasons
Earth’s rotational axis is tilted at 23.44° relative to the perpendicular of its orbital plane. This obliquity is the primary cause of the seasons: as Earth orbits the Sun, the Northern and Southern Hemispheres alternate between receiving more and less direct solar radiation. Earth completes one rotation on its axis in 23 h 56 m (one sidereal day) and one orbit around the Sun in 365.25 days (one sidereal year), which necessitates the insertion of a leap day every four years to keep the calendar aligned with the seasons.

Water and life
Approximately 71% of Earth’s surface is covered by oceans, with a total water volume of about 1.335 × 109 km3. The water cycle — evaporation, condensation, precipitation and runoff — continuously redistributes water between the oceans, atmosphere, and land. Earth occupies the habitable zone (also called the Goldilocks zone) around the Sun, the region where surface temperatures permit liquid water to exist. The data from decades of exoplanet surveys indicate that rocky worlds in the habitable zones of their host stars are common, but Earth remains the only body where life has been confirmed. Earth’s magnetic field plays a critical role in habitability: it deflects the solar wind and shields the atmosphere from erosion by charged particles, preserving the air and water that sustain life.

The Moon
Earth’s sole natural satellite, the Moon, has a diameter of 3,474 km — about one-quarter that of Earth — making the Earth–Moon system unusual among the terrestrial planets for the large relative size of the satellite. The Moon is thought to have formed from debris ejected by a giant impact between the proto-Earth and a Mars-sized body roughly 4.5 billion years ago. Its gravitational influence stabilises Earth’s axial tilt, preventing large chaotic oscillations in obliquity that could otherwise produce extreme climatic swings. Tidal interactions between Earth and the Moon raise ocean tides and gradually slow Earth’s rotation, lengthening the day by approximately 2.3 milliseconds per century.

Frequently Asked Questions

How big is the Earth?

Earth has an equatorial diameter of 12,742 km (7,918 miles) and a polar diameter of 12,714 km, making it the largest of the four terrestrial planets. Its total surface area is approximately 510.1 million km2, of which about 71% is covered by oceans and 29% by land. Earth’s mean density of 5.51 g/cm3 is the highest of any planet in the Solar System, a consequence of its substantial iron core.

What is the circumference of the Earth?

The circumference of the Earth at the equator is 40,075 km (24,901 miles). The polar circumference is slightly shorter at 40,008 km because Earth is not a perfect sphere but an oblate spheroid, bulging at the equator due to its rotation. The Greek mathematician Eratosthenes first estimated Earth’s circumference around 240 BCE by comparing shadow angles at two locations in Egypt.

What is the radius of the Earth?

Earth has a mean radius of 6,371 km. The equatorial radius is 6,378 km, while the polar radius is 6,357 km — a difference of 21 km caused by the planet’s rotational flattening.

What is the shape of the Earth?

Earth is an oblate spheroid — a sphere slightly flattened at the poles and bulging at the equator. The equatorial diameter exceeds the polar diameter by about 43 km, giving a flattening ratio of approximately 1/298. This shape results from the centrifugal effect of Earth’s rotation.

What is the mass of the Earth?

The mass of the Earth is 5.972 × 1024 kg, making it the most massive of the terrestrial planets and roughly 330,000 times less massive than the Sun. Surface gravity is 9.81 m/s2, and the escape velocity is 11.2 km/s.

How was the Earth formed?

Earth formed approximately 4.54 billion years ago by accretion from the solar nebula. Over tens of millions of years, planetesimals collided and merged, building up the proto-Earth. Heat from impacts and radioactive decay caused the interior to differentiate into a metallic core and silicate mantle. A giant impact with a Mars-sized body roughly 4.5 billion years ago produced the debris that coalesced into the Moon.

Who named Earth?

The name “Earth” derives from the Old English word eorthe and the Germanic erde, meaning ground or soil. Earth is the only planet in the Solar System whose English name does not derive from Greco-Roman mythology. No single person is credited with naming the planet; the word evolved through common usage over centuries.

What is Earth’s atmosphere made of?

Earth’s atmosphere is composed primarily of nitrogen (78.08%) and oxygen (20.95%), with trace amounts of argon (0.93%), carbon dioxide (0.04%), and variable water vapour. The atmosphere is divided into layers — the troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere, and exosphere — each defined by how temperature changes with altitude.

What is the surface area of the Earth?

The total surface area of the Earth is approximately 510.1 million km2 (196.9 million square miles). Of this, about 361 million km2 (71%) is covered by water and 149 million km2 (29%) is land.

How fast does the Earth rotate?

Earth completes one rotation on its axis every 23 hours 56 minutes and 4 seconds (one sidereal day). At the equator, this corresponds to a surface speed of approximately 1,670 km/h (1,037 mph). Earth rotates from west to east, which is why the Sun appears to rise in the east.

What is the Earth made of?

Earth is composed of an iron–nickel core, a thick silicate mantle, and a thin rocky crust. By mass, the planet is approximately 32% iron, 30% oxygen, 15% silicon, and 14% magnesium. The solid inner core reaches temperatures of approximately 5,400 °C.

How much of the Earth is water?

Approximately 71% of Earth’s surface is covered by water, with the remaining 29% consisting of continents and islands. The total volume of water on Earth is about 1.335 × 109 km3, of which 96.5% resides in the oceans. Only about 2.5% of Earth’s water is fresh, and most of that is locked in ice caps and glaciers.

Why does the Earth have seasons?

Seasons occur because Earth’s rotational axis is tilted at 23.44° relative to the perpendicular of its orbital plane. As Earth orbits the Sun, the Northern and Southern Hemispheres alternate between receiving more and less direct solar radiation. The tilt — not Earth’s varying distance from the Sun — is the primary cause of seasonal temperature changes.

What is the temperature of the Earth?

The mean surface temperature of the Earth is approximately 15 °C (288 K / 59 °F). Surface temperatures range from a recorded minimum of −89.2 °C in Antarctica to a maximum of 56.7 °C in Death Valley, California. The greenhouse effect raises the mean temperature by about 33 °C above the bare radiative equilibrium value.
